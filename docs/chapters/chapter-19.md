# Chapter XIX — Trial Before a Court of Session

## Sections 248–249 — Whose case it is, and how it opens

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute A Sessions trial is not a private fight between a complainant and an accused, even where a private complaint originally set the case in motion. Section 248 states the rule that makes that so: in every trial before a Court of Session, the prosecution shall be conducted by a Public Prosecutor — a state law officer, not private counsel, carries the case forward, regardless of who first set the machinery in motion. Section 249 then fixes the trial's actual starting move once the accused appears or is brought before the Court following committal under section 232 (or under any other law): the prosecutor opens the case, describing the charge and stating what evidence he intends to use to prove it. Nothing happens before that opening statement — it is the formal beginning of the trial proper, after the paperwork of committal and charge-framing is behind the parties.
:::

::: story A private complainant's advocate, and the limits of his role
A complaint of a Sessions-exclusive offence is committed to the Court of Session under section 232. The private complainant, who has retained his own advocate throughout the Magistrate-stage proceedings, wants that advocate to conduct the trial before the Sessions Judge. Section 248 answers that directly: the prosecution in a Sessions trial must be conducted by a Public Prosecutor. The complainant's advocate may assist the Public Prosecutor — attending, briefing, even making submissions — but only under the Public Prosecutor's directions; he cannot conduct the prosecution independently, examine witnesses in his own right, or address the Court as though he, rather than the Public Prosecutor, were in charge of the case. Once the accused appears before the Sessions Judge, it is the Public Prosecutor, not the complainant's counsel, who rises first under section 249 to open the case.
:::

> **248.** In every trial before a Court of Session, the prosecution [[!shall]] be conducted by a Public Prosecutor.
>
> **249.** When the accused appears or is brought before the Court, in pursuance of a commitment of the case under section 232, or under any other law for the time being in force, the prosecutor [[!shall]] open his case by describing the charge brought against the accused and stating by what evidence he proposes to prove the guilt of the accused.

::: proviso Section 248 is about who conducts the trial, not who may be present or assist
Section 248 does not exclude a private complainant's advocate from the courtroom or from assisting the prosecution — it excludes him from *conducting* it. Control of the trial's conduct — what evidence is led, which witnesses are called, how the case is opened and argued — remains with the Public Prosecutor throughout.
:::

### In plain English

Sections 248 and 249 set the stage before the trial's substance begins. Section 248 answers a question of institutional control: however the case reached the Sessions Court — police report or private complaint — a state law officer, not private counsel, conducts it from here. Section 249 answers a question of sequence: once the accused is actually before the Judge, the very first substantive act is the prosecutor laying out, in outline, what the charge is and what evidence he intends to use to prove it — the roadmap the rest of the trial will follow.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct A private complainant, dissatisfied with the Public Prosecutor's handling of the case, instructs his own advocate to independently cross-examine a defence witness without the Public Prosecutor's involvement.
::: ruling The independent cross-examination is not permitted under section 248
Section 248 vests conduct of the prosecution in the Public Prosecutor. A private complainant's advocate may assist — including, with the Public Prosecutor's leave and under his directions, taking an active role — but cannot act independently of the Public Prosecutor's control over how the case is conducted.
:::
::::

:::: instinct After committal, the accused appears before the Sessions Judge, and the Judge proceeds directly to framing the charge without the prosecutor first describing the case and the evidence relied on.
::: ruling The sequence skips section 249's opening-of-case step
Section 249 contemplates the prosecutor opening the case — describing the charge and the evidence intended to prove it — as the trial's starting move once the accused appears following committal. Proceeding straight to charge-framing without that step departs from the sequence the section lays down, though whether the departure is fatal on given facts turns on whether it actually prejudiced the accused.
:::

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

Shiv Kumar v. Hukam Chand and Another, (1999) 7 SCC 467. The Court held that in a Sessions trial the prosecution must be conducted by a Public Prosecutor, and a private complainant's counsel may only assist the Public Prosecutor under his directions — he cannot conduct the prosecution, examine or cross-examine witnesses, or address the Court independently of the Public Prosecutor's control. **Practitioner takeaway**: a private complainant's advocate seeking an independent role in a Sessions trial should be met with the objection that section 248 vests conduct of the prosecution exclusively in the Public Prosecutor.

#### Sword and shield

As a **shield**, section 248 protects the accused from a Sessions trial effectively run by a private complainant's advocate pursuing a personal or adversarial agenda outside the checks that attach to a state law officer's conduct of a prosecution. As a **sword**, the defence can object to any private-counsel conduct of the trial that exceeds mere assistance to the Public Prosecutor, citing Shiv Kumar v. Hukam Chand directly; the prosecution's countervailing tool is simply to keep private counsel's role visibly confined to assistance under the Public Prosecutor's directions, avoiding the objection altogether.

#### The limitation clock

No independent limitation period is fixed by this cluster; it governs trial structure and sequence, not a filing deadline.

#### Interlocking matrix

Section 249 interlocks directly with section 232 (the committal mechanism, Chapter XVII, already built) as the trigger for the accused's appearance before the Sessions Court, and with section 251 (framing of charge, next in this chapter) as the step that follows the prosecutor's opening. #### Strategy and drafting

**Model checklist for reviewing this cluster's provisions:**

1. Where a private complainant's advocate seeks a role beyond assisting the Public Prosecutor in a Sessions trial, object promptly and specifically under section 248, citing Shiv Kumar v. Hukam Chand.
2. Confirm the trial record actually reflects the prosecutor's opening statement under section 249 before charge-framing proceeds — its absence is a procedural irregularity worth flagging even where not independently fatal.
3. Where representing a private complainant, secure a clear understanding in advance with the Public Prosecutor's office about the scope of assistance counsel will be permitted to provide.
