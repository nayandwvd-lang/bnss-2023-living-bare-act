# Chapter XXVI — General Provisions as to Inquiries and Trials

## Section 337 — Autrefois convict and autrefois acquit: the bar against being tried twice

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Once a competent Court has actually tried a person for an offence and either convicted or acquitted him, section 337 raises a general bar: while that conviction or acquittal stands, he cannot be tried again for the same offence, nor — on the same facts — for any other offence that could have been charged in the alternative under section 244(1) or that he could have been convicted of under section 244(2). That is the rule in sub-section (1), and it is close to absolute. But four narrow, textually distinct doors stay open: sub-section (2) lets a fresh trial proceed for a genuinely distinct offence chargeable at the former trial under section 243(1), but only with the State Government's consent; sub-section (3) lets a conviction be followed by a trial for a graver consequence of the same act that had not yet happened, or was not yet known to the Court, at the time of the first conviction; sub-section (4) lets a second trial proceed where the first Court simply was not competent to try the offence now being charged; and sub-section (5) preserves a fresh trial after a section 281 discharge, but only with the consent of the discharging Court or its superior. The Explanation then draws a line the rule itself depends on: a dismissed complaint or a discharge is not an acquittal, so neither one closes the door the way an actual acquittal or conviction does.
:::

::: story The servant, the theft charge, and the trial that could not happen twice
A domestic servant is tried on a charge of theft as a servant and is acquitted. Some months later, the same facts resurface and the prosecution considers a fresh charge — theft simply, or criminal breach of trust, framed around the identical transaction. Section 337(1) closes that door outright: while the acquittal remains in force, he cannot be tried again on the same facts for any offence that could have been charged in the alternative at the first trial. Contrast a second case the same week: a man is tried for causing grievous hurt and convicted; the victim later dies of the injury. Sub-section (3) lets the prosecution try him again — for culpable homicide this time — because that consequence had not happened, and could not have been known to the first Court, when the first conviction was recorded.
:::

> **337. (1)** A person who has once been tried by a Court of competent jurisdiction for an offence and convicted or acquitted of such offence [[!shall]], while such conviction or acquittal remains in force, not be liable to be tried again for the same offence, nor on the same facts for any other offence for which a different charge from the one made against him might have been made under sub-section (1) of section 244, or for which he might have been convicted under sub-section (2) thereof.
>
> **(2)** A person acquitted or convicted of any offence [[?may]] be afterwards tried, with the consent of the State Government, for any distinct offence for which a separate charge might have been made against him at the former trial under sub-section (1) of section 243.
>
> **(3)** A person convicted of any offence constituted by any act causing consequences which, together with such act, constituted a different offence from that of which he was convicted, [[?may]] be afterwards tried for such last-mentioned offence, if the consequences had not happened, or were not known to the Court to have happened, at the time when he was convicted.
>
> **(4)** A person acquitted or convicted of any offence constituted by any acts [[?may]], notwithstanding such acquittal or conviction, be subsequently charged with, and tried for, any other offence constituted by the same acts which he may have committed if the Court by which he was first tried was not competent to try the offence with which he is subsequently charged.
>
> **(5)** A person discharged under section 281 [[!shall]] not be tried again for the same offence except with the consent of the Court by which he was discharged or of any other Court to which the first-mentioned Court is subordinate.
>
> **(6)** Nothing in this section [[!shall]] affect the provisions of section 26 of the General Clauses Act, 1897 ⚠️ or of section 208 of this Sanhita.
>
> *Explanation.* — The dismissal of a complaint, or the discharge of the accused, is not an acquittal for the purposes of this section.
>
> *Illustrations.*
>
> (a) A is tried upon a charge of theft as a servant and acquitted. He cannot afterwards, while the acquittal remains in force, be charged with theft as a servant, or, upon the same facts, with theft simply, or with criminal breach of trust.
>
> (b) A is tried for causing grievous hurt and convicted. The person injured afterwards dies. A may be tried again for culpable homicide.
>
> (c) A is charged before the Court of Session and convicted of the culpable homicide of B. A may not afterwards be tried on the same facts for the murder of B.
>
> (d) A is charged by a Magistrate of the first class with, and convicted by him of, voluntarily causing hurt to B. A may not afterwards be tried for voluntarily causing grievous hurt to B on the same facts, unless the case comes within sub-section (3) of this section.
>
> (e) A is charged by a Magistrate of the second class with, and convicted by him of, theft of property from the person of B. A may subsequently be charged with, and tried for, robbery on the same facts.
>
> (f) A, B and C are charged by a Magistrate of the first class with, and convicted by him of, robbing D. A, B and C may afterwards be charged with, and tried for, dacoity on the same facts.

::: proviso Sub-section (1) is close to absolute; sub-sections (2)-(5) are four narrow, textually distinct doors, not a general discretion to retry
Nothing in sub-sections (2) to (5) lets a Court or a prosecutor retry a person merely because it later seems desirable. Each door has its own trigger and, in two cases, its own consent gate: sub-section (2) requires State Government consent for a genuinely distinct former-trial-eligible offence; sub-section (5) requires the discharging Court's own consent (or that of its superior) after a section 281 discharge. Sub-sections (3) and (4) need no external consent, but each is gated on a specific factual condition — an unknown-at-the-time consequence, or the first Court's own incompetence to try the later charge — not on prosecutorial convenience.
:::

::: proviso The Explanation is doing real work: a dismissal or a discharge is not an acquittal, so section 337(1) never engages on those facts at all
Illustration (a)'s bar depends entirely on an actual acquittal being in force. A complaint dismissed under section 226, or an accused discharged under section 262 or section 268 (⚠️ not yet independently cross-checked against this chapter for exact numbering), is neither a conviction nor an acquittal — the Explanation says so expressly — so a fresh complaint or a fresh prosecution on the same facts is not barred by this section at all. The four sub-section (2)-(5) doors are irrelevant to that scenario; they only matter once sub-section (1)'s bar has actually been triggered by a real conviction or acquittal.
:::

### In plain English

Once you have actually been tried and either convicted or acquitted, the State does not get a second attempt on the same facts — that is the baseline rule, and it holds while the conviction or acquittal stays in force. But the rule is not absolute: it has four precise, narrow exits, each tied to a specific factual trigger rather than to anyone's discretion. And the rule only switches on in the first place if what happened earlier was a genuine conviction or acquittal — a complaint that was simply dismissed, or an accused who was merely discharged, never triggers the bar, so a fresh attempt on the same facts remains open in those cases without needing any of the four exceptions at all.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct A person is tried for theft as a servant and acquitted. Two months later, on the very same set of facts, the prosecution files a fresh complaint alleging criminal breach of trust instead.
::: ruling Barred — this is illustration (a) itself
Section 337(1) bars not only a retrial for the identical offence but also a trial, on the same facts, for any other offence for which a different charge might have been made at the first trial under section 244(1), or for which he might have been convicted under section 244(2). Illustration (a) states this exact scenario and confirms the bar applies.
:::
::::

:::: instinct A complaint alleging cheating is dismissed by a Magistrate for want of evidence. The complainant later files a fresh complaint on the same facts, having since located a key document.
::: ruling Not barred — a dismissal is not an acquittal
The Explanation to section 337 expressly states that the dismissal of a complaint is not an acquittal for the purposes of this section. Section 337(1)'s bar never engages on these facts at all, so the fresh complaint is not barred by this section, whatever other procedural hurdles it may face.

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

⚠️ *Research gap: no citation meets this guide's confidence bar for this cluster. Given the constitutional weight of the autrefois convict/acquit principle (Article 20(2) operates alongside this section for the narrower double-jeopardy-for-the-same-offence band), a reader relying on this cluster in practice should independently locate and verify the leading Supreme Court authority on section 300 CrPC/section 337 BNSS before citing it — this guide treats the gap as a research gap, not a citation to be guessed.*

#### Sword and shield

As a **shield**, the defence's first move on a repeat prosecution is always to establish precisely what the first proceeding actually was — a conviction, an acquittal, a dismissal, or a discharge — because only the first two trigger section 337(1) at all; conflating a discharge with an acquittal is a common and fatal error for the side relying on the bar. As a **sword**, the prosecution's four exit doors are genuinely narrow: before invoking sub-section (3)'s "consequence not yet known" exception, confirm on the record precisely when the consequence occurred or came to the first Court's knowledge, since the exception fails entirely if the consequence was already known at the time of the first conviction.

#### The limitation clock

No independent limitation period runs under section 337 itself; the bar (or its absence) is a continuing legal condition tested at the moment the second proceeding is instituted, not a filing deadline.

#### Interlocking matrix

Section 337(1) cross-refers to section 244 (Chapter XVIII, already built — the alternative-charge and included-offence conviction framework). Sub-section (2) cross-refers to section 243(1) (Chapter XVIII, already built — the joinder-of-charges gateway). Sub-section (5) cross-refers to section 281 (Chapter XXI, already built — the Magistrate's stop-proceedings power for non-complaint summons-cases). Sub-section (6) preserves section 208 of this Sanhita (⚠️ not yet built in this repository) and section 26 of the General Clauses Act, 1897 (⚠️ outside this guide's verified corpus).

#### Strategy and drafting

**Model checklist for reviewing a section 337 objection:**

1. Pin down exactly what the first proceeding produced — conviction, acquittal, dismissal, or discharge — before assuming the bar applies at all.
2. If relying on the bar as a shield, plead it with the specific former charge and former Court identified, not as a generic double-jeopardy assertion.
3. If seeking to overcome the bar as a prosecutor, identify which one of the four sub-section (2)-(5) doors is actually being invoked, and satisfy its specific trigger (and consent requirement, where one exists) before filing the fresh charge.
4. Do not conflate this section's same-facts bar with Article 20(2)'s narrower same-offence double-jeopardy guarantee — the two overlap but are not coextensive, and a reader should verify which is actually being argued before pleading either.
