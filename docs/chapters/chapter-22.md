# Chapter XXII — Summary Trials

## Section 283 — Which offences a Magistrate must, and may, try summarily

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Summary trial is the fastest procedure this guide covers — a compressed record, a capped sentence, and (for the discretionary route) no appeal against the decision to use it at all. Section 283 sets the gate. Sub-section (1) opens with a non-obstante clause — "notwithstanding anything contained in this Sanhita" — and then hands the Chief Judicial Magistrate or a Magistrate of the first class a closed, itemised list of petty offences they *must* try summarily: theft, receiving or concealing stolen property (each capped at ₹20,000 in value), specified assault-type offences under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, insult intended to provoke a breach of the peace, criminal intimidation of the lesser kind, the abetment or attempt of any of these, and certain Cattle-trespass Act complaints. Sub-section (2) then opens a second, discretionary door: any offence not punishable with death, life imprisonment, or imprisonment exceeding three years may also be tried summarily — but only after the Magistrate gives the accused a hearing and records reasons in writing, and the price of using this door is that no appeal lies against the decision to try the case summarily. Sub-section (3) is the safety valve: if it becomes apparent mid-trial that summary disposal is undesirable, the Magistrate must recall any witnesses already examined and start over under the ordinary procedure.
:::

::: story A ₹15,000 theft, and a Magistrate who would rather not be bothered
A first-class Magistrate is handed a theft case where the stolen property is valued at ₹15,000 — squarely within section 283(1)(i)'s ₹20,000 cap. The Magistrate, thinking the matter deserves a fuller record, proposes to try it as an ordinary warrant-case instead. He has no such choice: section 283(1)'s "shall," reinforced by its non-obstante opening, forecloses any preference-based departure from summary trial for an offence that falls within the closed list.
:::

> **283. (1)** Notwithstanding anything contained in this Sanhita—
> (a) any Chief Judicial Magistrate;
> (b) Magistrate of the first class,
> [[!shall]] try in a summary way all or any of the following offences:—
> (i) theft, under sub-section (2) of section 303, section 305 or section 306 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, where the value of the property stolen does not exceed twenty thousand rupees;
> (ii) receiving or retaining stolen property, under sub-section (2) of section 317 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, where the value of the property does not exceed twenty thousand rupees;
> (iii) assisting in the concealment or disposal of stolen property under sub-section (5) of section 317 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, where the value of such property does not exceed twenty thousand rupees;
> (iv) offences under sub-sections (2) and (3) of section 331 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023;
> (v) insult with intent to provoke a breach of the peace, under section 352, and criminal intimidation, under sub-sections (2) and (3) of section 351 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023;
> (vi) abetment of any of the foregoing offences;
> (vii) an attempt to commit any of the foregoing offences, when such attempt is an offence;
> (viii) any offence constituted by an act in respect of which a complaint may be made under section 20 of the Cattle-trespass Act, 1871.
>
> **(2)** The Magistrate [[?may]], after giving the accused a reasonable opportunity of being heard, for reasons to be recorded in writing, try in a summary way all or any of the offences not punishable with death or imprisonment for life or imprisonment for a term exceeding three years:
>
> Provided that no appeal shall lie against the decision of a Magistrate to try a case in a summary way under this sub-section.
>
> **(3)** When, in the course of a summary trial it appears to the Magistrate that the nature of the case is such that it is undesirable to try it summarily, the Magistrate [[!shall]] recall any witnesses who may have been examined and proceed to re-hear the case in the manner provided by this Sanhita.

::: proviso Section 283(1)'s non-obstante clause displaces the ordinary classification of these offences
Several of the listed offences — theft or receiving stolen property up to ₹20,000, for instance — might otherwise attract warrant-case or summons-case procedure depending on their punishment. The "notwithstanding anything contained in this Sanhita" opening overrides that ordinary classification: once an offence falls within section 283(1)'s closed list, summary trial is not optional and no other provision of the Sanhita can be invoked to insist on a different procedure.
:::

::: proviso The no-appeal bar in section 283(2) is narrow — it bars appeal only against the decision to try summarily, not against the eventual conviction or sentence
The proviso to section 283(2) says precisely what it says and no more: "no appeal shall lie against the decision of a Magistrate to try a case in a summary way." It does not touch the accused's ordinary right of appeal against a conviction or sentence recorded at the end of that summary trial, which continues to run under the Sanhita's general appeal provisions. ⚠️ *Those general appeal provisions have not yet been built in this guide; treat the cross-reference as a placeholder pending that chapter.*
:::

### In plain English

Section 283 draws two doors into summary trial. The first is mandatory and mechanical: if the offence is on the list — capped-value theft or handling of stolen property, certain assault-type and intimidation offences, their abetment or attempt, or a Cattle-trespass Act complaint — the Magistrate has no discretion, the non-obstante clause overrides anything else in the Sanhita, and summary trial follows. The second is discretionary: any offence carrying three years' imprisonment or less can also be tried summarily, but only after a hearing and recorded reasons, and the accused gives up nothing but the right to appeal that particular procedural decision — not the right to appeal the conviction itself. Either way, the Magistrate keeps an emergency brake: if the case turns out to be more complicated than it looked, section 283(3) requires recalling the witnesses and starting again under the ordinary procedure, rather than forcing a compressed trial onto a case that doesn't fit it.

### What changed from the CrPC

⚠️ *Provisional mapping, pending dedicated verification pass: BNSS section 283 appears to track CrPC section 260 (power to try summarily). The itemised offence list and the three-year discretionary band both read as structurally continuous with the CrPC provision. One change is visible directly on the face of the text and appears genuine rather than merely provisional: the monetary cap for theft, receiving, and concealing stolen property is set at twenty thousand rupees — this is a substantial increase over the CrPC's long-standing (and separately amended) lower threshold, though the exact CrPC figure has not been independently cross-checked against a bare CrPC text in this repository and should be verified before being cited as a precise "changed from ₹X to ₹20,000" proposition.*

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct A first-class Magistrate, faced with a theft case valued at ₹15,000, decides to try it as a full warrant-case instead because he wants a more complete record.
::: ruling Section 283(1) leaves no such choice
The offence falls within section 283(1)(i)'s closed list, and the non-obstante clause overrides any competing procedural preference found elsewhere in the Sanhita. The Magistrate's reasons — however sound as a matter of record-keeping — do not create a discretion the statute does not give him.
:::
::::

:::: instinct A convicted accused, tried summarily under the discretionary route in section 283(2) for a two-year offence, files an appeal challenging the Magistrate's decision to try the case summarily in the first place.
::: ruling That specific appeal is barred, but the conviction itself is a different question
The proviso to section 283(2) bars only an appeal against the decision to try the case in a summary way. It does not touch the accused's ordinary right to appeal the conviction or sentence that followed. Counsel drafting the appeal memo should keep the two grounds clearly separated — one is foreclosed by name, the other is not mentioned at all.
:::

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

#### Sword and shield

As a **sword**, a complainant or prosecutor facing a mandatory-list offence can insist on summary disposal — the non-obstante clause forecloses any defence argument for a fuller warrant-case procedure, which can matter where delay itself favours the accused. As a **shield**, an accused facing a proposed discretionary summary trial under section 283(2) has exactly one effective window to resist it: the hearing that must precede the Magistrate's decision, since no appeal lies against that decision once made. If the trial later turns out to be more complex than it looked, section 283(3) gives the accused a second, structural safeguard — the Magistrate must recall witnesses and restart under ordinary procedure rather than force a compressed disposal.

#### The limitation clock

No independent limitation period runs from this cluster; section 283 governs a procedural classification made at or after the accused's appearance, not a filing deadline.

#### Interlocking matrix

Section 283(1)'s itemised list cross-refers to the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 — sections 303(2), 305, 306 (theft), 317(2) and 317(5) (receiving, retaining, and concealing stolen property), 331(2)–(3), 351(2)–(3) (criminal intimidation), and 352 (insult to provoke breach of peace) — and to section 20 of the Cattle-trespass Act, 1871. ⚠️ *None of these substantive-offence provisions are within this guide's BNSS-focused scope and have not been built here; they are flagged for cross-reference only.* Within this guide, section 283(3)'s reversion sends the case back into the ordinary trial track already built in Chapters XIX–XXI, depending on the offence's proper classification.

#### Strategy and drafting

**Model checklist for reviewing this cluster's provisions:**

1. Check the stolen-property value against the ₹20,000 cap precisely before assuming section 283(1)(i)–(iii) applies — a value even marginally above the cap takes the case out of the mandatory list entirely.
2. Where the Magistrate proposes discretionary summary trial under section 283(2), use the pre-decision hearing to argue against it if the case has factual complexity — this is the only stage at which the decision itself can be contested.
3. Do not conflate the section 283(2) no-appeal bar with a bar on appealing the conviction; draft any appeal memo to make that distinction explicit.
4. If new facts emerge mid-trial suggesting the case is unsuitable for summary disposal, flag section 283(3) promptly — the earlier this is raised, the less evidence needs to be recalled and re-heard.
