# Chapter XXII — Summary Trials

## Section 283 — Which offences a Magistrate must, and may, try summarily

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Summary trial is the fastest procedure this guide covers — a compressed record, a capped sentence, and (for the discretionary route) no appeal against the decision to use it at all. Section 283 sets the gate. Sub-section (1) opens with a non-obstante clause — "notwithstanding anything contained in this Sanhita" — and then hands the Chief Judicial Magistrate or a Magistrate of the first class a closed, itemised list of petty offences they *must* try summarily: theft, receiving or concealing stolen property (each capped at ₹20,000 in value), specified assault-type offences under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, insult intended to provoke a breach of the peace, criminal intimidation of the lesser kind, the abetment or attempt of any of these, and certain Cattle-trespass Act complaints. Sub-section (2) then opens a second, discretionary door: any offence not punishable with death, life imprisonment, or imprisonment exceeding three years may also be tried summarily — but only after the Magistrate gives the accused a hearing and records reasons in writing, and the price of using this door is that no appeal lies against the decision to try the case summarily. Sub-section (3) is the safety valve: if it becomes apparent mid-trial that summary disposal is undesirable, the Magistrate must recall any witnesses already examined and start over under the ordinary procedure.
:::

::: story A ₹15,000 theft, and a Magistrate who would rather not be bothered
A first-class Magistrate is handed a theft case where the stolen property is valued at ₹15,000 — squarely within section 283(1)(i)'s ₹20,000 cap. The Magistrate, thinking the matter deserves a fuller record, proposes to try it as an ordinary warrant-case instead. He has no such choice: section 283(1)'s "shall," reinforced by its non-obstante opening, forecloses any preference-based departure from summary trial for an offence that falls within the closed list.
:::

> **283. (1)** Notwithstanding anything contained in this Sanhita—
> (a) any Chief Judicial Magistrate;
> (b) Magistrate of the first class,
> [[!shall]] try in a summary way all or any of the following offences:—
> (i) theft, under sub-section (2) of section 303, section 305 or section 306 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, where the value of the property stolen does not exceed twenty thousand rupees;
> (ii) receiving or retaining stolen property, under sub-section (2) of section 317 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, where the value of the property does not exceed twenty thousand rupees;
> (iii) assisting in the concealment or disposal of stolen property under sub-section (5) of section 317 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, where the value of such property does not exceed twenty thousand rupees;
> (iv) offences under sub-sections (2) and (3) of section 331 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023;
> (v) insult with intent to provoke a breach of the peace, under section 352, and criminal intimidation, under sub-sections (2) and (3) of section 351 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023;
> (vi) abetment of any of the foregoing offences;
> (vii) an attempt to commit any of the foregoing offences, when such attempt is an offence;
> (viii) any offence constituted by an act in respect of which a complaint may be made under section 20 of the Cattle-trespass Act, 1871.
>
> **(2)** The Magistrate [[?may]], after giving the accused a reasonable opportunity of being heard, for reasons to be recorded in writing, try in a summary way all or any of the offences not punishable with death or imprisonment for life or imprisonment for a term exceeding three years:
>
> Provided that no appeal shall lie against the decision of a Magistrate to try a case in a summary way under this sub-section.
>
> **(3)** When, in the course of a summary trial it appears to the Magistrate that the nature of the case is such that it is undesirable to try it summarily, the Magistrate [[!shall]] recall any witnesses who may have been examined and proceed to re-hear the case in the manner provided by this Sanhita.

::: proviso Section 283(1)'s non-obstante clause displaces the ordinary classification of these offences
Several of the listed offences — theft or receiving stolen property up to ₹20,000, for instance — might otherwise attract warrant-case or summons-case procedure depending on their punishment. The "notwithstanding anything contained in this Sanhita" opening overrides that ordinary classification: once an offence falls within section 283(1)'s closed list, summary trial is not optional and no other provision of the Sanhita can be invoked to insist on a different procedure.
:::

::: proviso The no-appeal bar in section 283(2) is narrow — it bars appeal only against the decision to try summarily, not against the eventual conviction or sentence
The proviso to section 283(2) says precisely what it says and no more: "no appeal shall lie against the decision of a Magistrate to try a case in a summary way." It does not touch the accused's ordinary right of appeal against a conviction or sentence recorded at the end of that summary trial, which continues to run under the Sanhita's general appeal provisions. ⚠️ *Those general appeal provisions have not yet been built in this guide; treat the cross-reference as a placeholder pending that chapter.*
:::

### In plain English

Section 283 draws two doors into summary trial. The first is mandatory and mechanical: if the offence is on the list — capped-value theft or handling of stolen property, certain assault-type and intimidation offences, their abetment or attempt, or a Cattle-trespass Act complaint — the Magistrate has no discretion, the non-obstante clause overrides anything else in the Sanhita, and summary trial follows. The second is discretionary: any offence carrying three years' imprisonment or less can also be tried summarily, but only after a hearing and recorded reasons, and the accused gives up nothing but the right to appeal that particular procedural decision — not the right to appeal the conviction itself. Either way, the Magistrate keeps an emergency brake: if the case turns out to be more complicated than it looked, section 283(3) requires recalling the witnesses and starting again under the ordinary procedure, rather than forcing a compressed trial onto a case that doesn't fit it.

⚠️ *Provisional mapping, pending dedicated verification pass: BNSS section 283 appears to track CrPC section 260 (power to try summarily). The itemised offence list and the three-year discretionary band both read as structurally continuous with the CrPC provision. One change is visible directly on the face of the text and appears genuine rather than merely provisional: the monetary cap for theft, receiving, and concealing stolen property is set at twenty thousand rupees — this is a substantial increase over the CrPC's long-standing (and separately amended) lower threshold, though the exact CrPC figure has not been independently cross-checked against a bare CrPC text in this repository and should be verified before being cited as a precise "changed from ₹X to ₹20,000" proposition.*

#### Sword and shield

As a **sword**, a complainant or prosecutor facing a mandatory-list offence can insist on summary disposal — the non-obstante clause forecloses any defence argument for a fuller warrant-case procedure, which can matter where delay itself favours the accused. As a **shield**, an accused facing a proposed discretionary summary trial under section 283(2) has exactly one effective window to resist it: the hearing that must precede the Magistrate's decision, since no appeal lies against that decision once made. If the trial later turns out to be more complex than it looked, section 283(3) gives the accused a second, structural safeguard — the Magistrate must recall witnesses and restart under ordinary procedure rather than force a compressed disposal.

#### The limitation clock

No independent limitation period runs from this cluster; section 283 governs a procedural classification made at or after the accused's appearance, not a filing deadline.

#### Interlocking matrix

Section 283(1)'s itemised list cross-refers to the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 — sections 303(2), 305, 306 (theft), 317(2) and 317(5) (receiving, retaining, and concealing stolen property), 331(2)–(3), 351(2)–(3) (criminal intimidation), and 352 (insult to provoke breach of peace) — and to section 20 of the Cattle-trespass Act, 1871. ⚠️ *None of these substantive-offence provisions are within this guide's BNSS-focused scope and have not been built here; they are flagged for cross-reference only.* Within this guide, section 283(3)'s reversion sends the case back into the ordinary trial track already built in Chapters XIX–XXI, depending on the offence's proper classification.

#### Strategy and drafting

**Model checklist for reviewing this cluster's provisions:**

1. Check the stolen-property value against the ₹20,000 cap precisely before assuming section 283(1)(i)–(iii) applies — a value even marginally above the cap takes the case out of the mandatory list entirely.
2. Where the Magistrate proposes discretionary summary trial under section 283(2), use the pre-decision hearing to argue against it if the case has factual complexity — this is the only stage at which the decision itself can be contested.
3. Do not conflate the section 283(2) no-appeal bar with a bar on appealing the conviction; draft any appeal memo to make that distinction explicit.
4. If new facts emerge mid-trial suggesting the case is unsuitable for summary disposal, flag section 283(3) promptly — the earlier this is raised, the less evidence needs to be recalled and re-heard.

## Sections 284–285 — Delegating summary-trial power downward, and the sentence cap that comes with it

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Section 283 gives summary-trial power to the Chief Judicial Magistrate and Magistrates of the first class as of right or by discretion. Section 284 lets the High Court extend a narrower version of that power downward to a Magistrate invested with second-class powers — but only for offences punishable with fine only, or with imprisonment not exceeding six months (with or without fine), plus the abetment or attempt of such an offence. Section 285 then answers the question every summary trial eventually asks: what procedure governs it, and what can the sentence actually be? The procedure is the summons-case procedure already built in Chapter XXI, followed except as this Chapter separately provides — and the sentence, regardless of what the underlying offence would ordinarily carry, is capped: no term of imprisonment exceeding three months may be passed on any conviction recorded under this Chapter.
:::

::: story A theft carrying up to seven years, tried and sentenced to a fortnight
A first-class Magistrate tries a theft case under section 283(1)(i)'s mandatory list — an offence that, tried in the ordinary way, could carry a substantially longer sentence under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023. Because it was tried summarily under this Chapter, section 285(2) caps whatever sentence follows at three months' imprisonment; the Magistrate, on the facts, imposes a fortnight. The mandatory-list offence's own maximum punishment is simply irrelevant to the sentencing ceiling once the case is disposed of under this Chapter.
:::

> **284.** The High Court [[?may]] confer on any Magistrate invested with the powers of a Magistrate of the second class power to try summarily any offence which is punishable only with fine or with imprisonment for a term not exceeding six months with or without fine, and any abetment of or attempt to commit any such offence.
>
> **285. (1)** In trials under this Chapter, the procedure specified in this Sanhita for the trial of summons-case [[!shall]] be followed except as hereinafter mentioned.
>
> **(2)** No sentence of imprisonment for a term exceeding three months [[!shall]] be passed in the case of any conviction under this Chapter.

::: proviso Section 284's delegation is itself discretionary, and narrower than section 283's own jurisdiction
The High Court "may" confer summary-trial power on a second-class Magistrate — nothing compels it to. And even where conferred, the power is capped at offences punishable only with fine, or with imprisonment not exceeding six months — a Magistrate invested with second-class powers never acquires jurisdiction, under this section, over section 283(1)'s mandatory-list offences or section 283(2)'s up-to-three-year discretionary band.
:::

::: proviso Section 285(2)'s three-month cap overrides the underlying offence's ordinary punishment, not just the second-class Magistrate's delegated jurisdiction
The cap is not confined to convictions recorded under the section 284 delegation — it applies to "any conviction under this Chapter," which includes the mandatory-list offences a CJM or first-class Magistrate tries under section 283(1). An offence that could ordinarily carry several years' imprisonment, once disposed of summarily under this Chapter, cannot draw more than three months, whichever Magistrate tried it.
:::

### In plain English

Two separate ideas sit in these two sections. Section 284 is about *who* gets to try a case summarily — the High Court can push that power down to a second-class Magistrate, but only for the least serious offences, fine-only or up to six months. Section 285 is about *how* the trial runs and *what* it can produce — the familiar summons-case procedure, and a hard three-month ceiling on imprisonment no matter which Magistrate tried the case or how severe the offence ordinarily is. The second idea is the one that defines summary trial as a category: speed and a simplified record are traded for a sentencing ceiling that applies uniformly across the whole Chapter.

⚠️ *Provisional mapping, pending dedicated verification pass: BNSS section 284 appears to track CrPC section 261 (summary trial by Magistrate of the second class), and BNSS section 285 appears to track CrPC section 262 (procedure for summary trials). Both read as structurally continuous with their CrPC predecessors — the delegation mechanism, the fine-or-six-months ceiling for second-class Magistrates, and the three-month sentencing cap all track familiar CrPC structure — but this has not been independently cross-checked clause-by-clause against a bare CrPC text in this repository.*

## Sections 286–288 — The compressed record that stands in for a full judgment, and the language it must be written in

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute A summary trial dispenses with much of the ordinary procedural record — no formal charge in most cases, a compressed evidence stage, a capped sentence. Sections 286–288 fix exactly what record must survive it, because this compressed record is what any later appeal or revision will have to work with. Section 286 is the mandatory baseline: in *every* summarily tried case, guilty plea or not, the Magistrate must enter ten specified particulars — case number, offence date, report or complaint date, complainant's name, the accused's name, parentage and residence, the offence complained of and (if any) proved, with the property's value where the offence falls under section 283(1)'s clauses (i)–(iii), the plea and examination, the finding, the sentence or final order, and the date proceedings terminated. Section 287 layers a further requirement on top of that baseline, but only where the accused did *not* plead guilty: the Magistrate must additionally record the substance of the evidence and a judgment containing a brief statement of reasons for the finding. Section 288 fixes the language — the language of the Court — and permits, with High Court authorisation, the physical preparation of the record or judgment to be delegated to an officer appointed by the Chief Judicial Magistrate, provided the Magistrate himself signs what is prepared.
:::

::: story A contested summary conviction, and an appeal that turns entirely on what the record actually shows
An accused, having pleaded not guilty in a summarily tried case, is convicted and appeals. Because the trial was summary, there is no elaborate judgment of the kind an ordinary trial would generate — the appellate court's entire basis for review is the section 286 particulars register together with, since the plea was not guilty, the section 287 substance-of-evidence and reasoned-finding record. Where either is thin or missing an item, the appeal is argued on the state of the record itself, not merely on the facts — because for a summarily tried case, the record *is* effectively the trial, as far as an appellate court can see it.
:::

> **286.** In every case tried summarily, the Magistrate [[!shall]] enter, in such form as the State Government may direct, the following particulars, namely:—
> (a) the serial number of the case;
> (b) the date of the commission of the offence;
> (c) the date of the report or complaint;
> (d) the name of the complainant (if any);
> (e) the name, parentage and residence of the accused;
> (f) the offence complained of and the offence (if any) proved, and in cases coming under clause (i), clause (ii) or clause (iii) of sub-section (1) of section 283, the value of the property in respect of which the offence has been committed;
> (g) the plea of the accused and his examination (if any);
> (h) the finding;
> (i) the sentence or other final order;
> (j) the date on which proceedings terminated.
>
> **287.** In every case tried summarily in which the accused does not plead guilty, the Magistrate [[!shall]] record the substance of the evidence and a judgment containing a brief statement of the reasons for the finding.
>
> **288. (1)** Every such record and judgment [[!shall]] be written in the language of the Court.
>
> **(2)** The High Court [[?may]] authorise any Magistrate empowered to try offences summarily to prepare the aforesaid record or judgment or both by means of an officer appointed in this behalf by the Chief Judicial Magistrate, and the record or judgment so prepared [[!shall]] be signed by such Magistrate.

::: proviso Section 286's ten particulars are a mechanical baseline that applies whatever the plea; section 287 is an additional layer triggered only by a not-guilty plea
A guilty-plea case tried summarily needs only the section 286 register to be validly recorded. Section 287's substance-of-evidence and reasoned-finding requirement is not a general rule for every summary trial — it applies specifically, and only, where the accused does not plead guilty, where there is actually a contested finding that needs reasons.
:::

::: proviso Section 288(2)'s delegation covers only physical preparation of the document, not the judicial act itself
An officer appointed by the Chief Judicial Magistrate may draft the record or judgment where the High Court has authorised this, but the section is explicit that "the record or judgment so prepared shall be signed by such Magistrate." Ownership of the record's content, and responsibility for it, stays with the Magistrate personally — delegation under section 288(2) is a drafting convenience, not a delegation of the judicial function.
:::

### In plain English

Think of sections 286–288 as the paperwork that has to survive a trial that otherwise leaves almost no paper trail. Every summarily tried case gets, at minimum, a ten-item particulars register — the who, what, when, and outcome of the case in skeletal form. If the accused actually contested the case, that skeleton gets fleshed out with a short account of the evidence and the reasons for the finding. And whatever form the record takes, it has to be in the Court's own language, and while a court officer can be authorised to physically write it up, the Magistrate has to sign it — the paperwork can be delegated, the responsibility cannot.

All three read as structurally continuous with their CrPC predecessors, with no departure flagged, though this has not been independently cross-checked clause-by-clause against a bare CrPC text in this repository.*

Section 287's additional requirement is expressly confined to cases "in which the accused does not plead guilty." A guilty-plea case is fully and validly recorded through the section 286 particulars register alone; requiring more than the statute asks for is not itself a defect, but its absence is not one either.

#### Sword and shield

As a **shield**, an accused challenging a summary conviction on appeal or revision should audit the record item-by-item against section 286, and, where the plea was not guilty, against section 287's substance-of-evidence and reasoned-finding requirement — because this compressed record is effectively the entire appellate record, a missing or defective item is a ground that stands independently of the merits. As a **sword**, the prosecution's countervailing position is that these are recording formalities, not substantive rights, and a technical gap in the register does not by itself undermine a conviction otherwise well-founded on the evidence actually taken — the practical contest is over how strictly a reviewing court treats the completeness of this record.

#### The limitation clock

No independent limitation period runs from this cluster; it governs the content and form of the trial record, not a filing deadline.

#### Interlocking matrix

Section 286(f)'s property-value entry cross-refers to section 283(1)'s clauses (i)–(iii), already built earlier in this chapter. The completeness of the section 286–288 record is, in practice, the primary battleground on appeal or revision against a summary conviction. ⚠️ *This guide's appeal and revision provisions have not yet been built; the cross-reference is flagged as a placeholder.*

#### Strategy and drafting

**Model checklist for reviewing this cluster's provisions:**

1. On any challenge to a summary conviction, audit the record against all ten section 286 particulars item-by-item before raising a merits argument — a missing item is a distinct, freestanding ground.
2. Where the plea was not guilty, separately confirm section 287's substance-of-evidence-and-reasoned-judgment requirement was met; its absence is independent of any section 286 deficiency.
3. Confirm the record is written in the language of the Court under section 288(1) — a record in the wrong language is vulnerable regardless of its substantive completeness.
4. Where an officer prepared the record under section 288(2), confirm the Magistrate's own signature appears on it; an unsigned or officer-only-signed record does not satisfy the section.
