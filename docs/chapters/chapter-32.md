# Chapter XXXII — Reference and Revision

## Sections 436–437 — Referring a live question of law to the High Court, and disposing of the case once it answers

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute A trial court sometimes runs into a question it is not the right forum to finally settle — most sharply, a question about whether an Act, Ordinance, or Regulation (or a provision within one) is actually valid. Section 436 addresses exactly that situation: where a pending case turns on such a question, and the court itself believes the law in question is invalid or inoperative, but no High Court or the Supreme Court has yet said so, the court cannot simply decide the point itself — it must state a case and refer it to the High Court. Section 436(2) adds a second, narrower and purely discretionary reference power for a Court of Session on any other question of law arising in a pending case. Section 436(3) lets the referring court manage the accused's custody status while the reference is pending. Section 437 closes the loop: once the High Court answers, its order binds the referring court, which must dispose of the case conformably to it, and the High Court may also direct who pays the costs of the reference.
:::

::: story A Magistrate who suspects the law itself is the problem
A Magistrate trying a case under a State-level regulatory Act starts to suspect that a particular provision of that Act is itself invalid — perhaps because it conflicts with a constitutional guarantee, or exceeds the enacting legislature's competence — and that this question is squarely necessary to dispose of the case pending before it. No High Court or the Supreme Court has ruled on the point. Section 436(1) does not let the Magistrate simply hold the provision invalid and move on; it requires the Magistrate to state a case, set out the opinion and reasons, and refer the matter to the High Court for decision. Meanwhile, in an unrelated case before a Court of Session, a purely legal question arises — not going to the validity of any enactment, but a genuinely contested point of law relevant to the case — and the Sessions Judge, though not obliged to, decides under section 436(2) that referring it to the High Court is the more prudent course. In both cases, section 436(3) lets the referring court decide, pending the High Court's decision, whether to commit the accused to jail or release them on bail.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 436 — Reference to High Court
"436. (1) Where any Court is satisfied that a case pending before it involves a question as to the validity of any Act, Ordinance or Regulation or of any provision contained in an Act, Ordinance or Regulation, the determination of which is necessary for the disposal of the case, and is of opinion that such Act, Ordinance, Regulation or provision is invalid or inoperative, but has not been so declared by the High Court to which that Court is subordinate or by the Supreme Court, the Court [[!shall]] state a case setting out its opinion and the reasons therefor, and refer the same for the decision of the High Court.
Explanation.—In this section, 'Regulation' means any Regulation as defined in the General Clauses Act, 1897, or in the General Clauses Act of a State.
(2) A Court of Session [[?may]], if it thinks fit in any case pending before it to which the provisions of sub-section (1) do not apply, refer for the decision of the High Court any question of law arising in the hearing of such case.
(3) Any Court making a reference to the High Court under sub-section (1) or sub-section (2) [[?may]], pending the decision of the High Court thereon, either commit the accused to jail or release him on bail to appear when called upon."
:::

::: proviso Section 437 — Disposal of case according to decision of High Court
"437. (1) When a question has been so referred, the High Court [[!shall]] pass such order thereon as it thinks fit, and [[!shall]] cause a copy of such order to be sent to the Court by which the reference was made, which [[!shall]] dispose of the case conformably to the said order.
(2) The High Court [[?may]] direct by whom the costs of such reference [[!shall]] be paid."
:::

### In plain English

Section 436 is really two different reference powers stitched into one section. The first, in sub-section (1), is a genuine duty: if a court believes the very law it is applying is invalid, and no higher court has already said so, it cannot just rule on that belief itself — it has to send the question up to the High Court and wait. The second, in sub-section (2), is a much looser, purely optional power available only to a Court of Session, for any other legal question it would rather have settled by the High Court before proceeding. Sub-section (3) is the practical housekeeping provision — while either kind of reference is pending, the referring court still has to manage the accused's custody, and it can either commit them to jail or release them on bail. Section 437 is simply the other half of the transaction: the High Court answers, sends its order back down, and the referring court is bound to dispose of the case exactly in line with that answer — with the High Court also free to allocate who bears the cost of having made the reference in the first place.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct A Magistrate of the first class, trying a case under a State Act, forms the private view that a provision of that Act is unconstitutional, but decides the point is not strictly necessary to decide the case since the case can be disposed of on other grounds. Must the Magistrate still refer the question to the High Court under section 436(1)?

::: ruling No. Section 436(1)'s mandatory reference duty is triggered only where the validity question's determination is 'necessary for the disposal of the case' -- it is not a general licence, still less an obligation, to refer every legal doubt a court happens to form about a statute's validity. If the case can genuinely be disposed of without resolving the validity question, the necessity condition is not met, and no mandatory reference arises. A Magistrate who refers a question that was not actually necessary to the disposal of the case risks being seen as having exceeded the narrow scope of section 436(1).
:::
::::

:::: instinct A Court of Session, while a case is pending before it, wants to refer a genuinely difficult question of law to the High Court under section 436(2), but the accused objects, arguing the reference will simply delay the trial. Can the accused block the reference?

::: ruling No. Section 436(2) commits the decision to refer entirely to the Court of Session's own discretion ('if it thinks fit') -- it is not conditioned on the consent of either party, and an accused's objection, however well-founded as a practical matter, does not create a veto over the Sessions Judge's choice to seek the High Court's guidance. The accused's remedy for genuine, unjustified delay lies elsewhere (for instance, in raising the delay before the referring court or, in an appropriate case, before the High Court itself), not in blocking the reference outright.
:::
::::

#### Sword and shield

For the **defence**, section 436(1) is worth raising affirmatively wherever the case genuinely turns on the validity of an Act, Ordinance, or Regulation and the trial court appears inclined to simply rule on that validity itself rather than referring it -- an order that resolves a validity question without a mandatory reference, where one was actually required, is a procedural irregularity worth flagging on appeal or revision. For the **prosecution**, section 436(2)'s purely discretionary character is worth emphasising when opposing a defence push to compel a reference on an ordinary point of law that does not go to an enactment's validity -- there is no entitlement to a reference outside sub-section (1).

#### The limitation clock

This cluster does not create a limitation period of its own; it governs an interlocutory reference procedure within an already-pending case, not a freestanding proceeding with its own filing deadline. The trial itself continues to run on whatever limitation and procedural clock otherwise applies to the underlying case.

#### Interlocking matrix

- **Section 442 (this Chapter, cluster 4)** — the High Court's own revisional powers, which operate on a different trigger (calling for a record to check correctness, legality, or propriety) than the section 436 reference, which is triggered by a pending case's own need to resolve a validity or legal question.
- **General Clauses Act, 1897 (and State equivalents)** — supplies the definition of 'Regulation' imported by section 436(1)'s Explanation; confirm the applicable State General Clauses Act where relevant.
- **Bail and custody provisions (Chapter XXXV)** — section 436(3)'s power to commit to jail or release on bail pending a reference should be read alongside the general bail framework, not as a freestanding, unconnected custody power.

#### Strategy and drafting

- Before urging a trial court to refer a validity question under section 436(1), confirm on the record that resolving the question is actually necessary to dispose of the case -- a reference sought or made on a question that turns out to be unnecessary invites later challenge.
- Where a Court of Session is asked to make a discretionary section 436(2) reference, frame the request around the genuine difficulty or importance of the legal question, since the power is expressly conditioned on the court itself 'thinking fit' -- a bare request without that framing gives the court little to hang its discretion on.
- While a reference is pending, address the accused's custody status explicitly under section 436(3) rather than leaving it unaddressed -- silence on this point can create unnecessary uncertainty about whether the accused remains in custody or on bail during what may be a lengthy reference process.
- On receiving the High Court's order under section 437, draft the resulting disposal order to track the High Court's reasoning and directions precisely -- section 437(1) requires disposal 'conformably to the said order,' leaving no room for the referring court to depart from it even partially.

## Sections 438–439 — Calling for the record to check an inferior court's correctness, and ordering further inquiry into a dismissed complaint or a discharge

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Reference is about a live legal question inside a pending case; this cluster is about revision — a different, after-the-fact check on an inferior court's work. Section 438 gives the High Court, or any Sessions Judge, the power to call for and examine the record of any proceeding before an inferior criminal court within its jurisdiction, purely to satisfy itself about the correctness, legality, or propriety of a finding, sentence, or order, and the regularity of the proceedings — with the power, while calling for the record, to suspend execution of the sentence or order and release a confined accused on bond or bail. Sub-section (2) fences off interlocutory orders from this power altogether, and sub-section (3) prevents a litigant from trying both the High Court and the Sessions Judge for the same complaint. Section 439 then supplies a specific, targeted revisional tool: on examining a record under section 438 or otherwise, the High Court or Sessions Judge can direct further inquiry into a complaint dismissed under section 226 or section 227(4), or into the case of a person who has been discharged — but only after that discharged person has had a chance to show cause against it.
:::

::: story A dismissed complaint that deserved a second look
A Magistrate dismisses a complaint under section 226 without issuing process, apparently overlooking material that, on a closer reading of the record, suggested the complaint had real substance. The complainant has no straightforward further complaint-stage remedy at that point. Under section 438, the Sessions Judge (or the High Court) can call for and examine the record of that dismissal to satisfy itself as to the correctness, legality, or propriety of the Magistrate's order. If, on doing so, it forms the view that the dismissal was premature or otherwise unsatisfactory, section 439 lets it direct the Chief Judicial Magistrate — either personally or through a subordinate Magistrate — to make further inquiry into the complaint. In a separate case, an accused person has been discharged at the inquiry stage; before any direction for further inquiry into that discharged person's case can be made under section 439, the proviso requires that the discharged person first be given an opportunity to show cause why such a direction should not be made.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 438 — Calling for records to exercise powers of revision
"438. (1) The High Court or any Sessions Judge [[?may]] call for and examine the record of any proceeding before any inferior Criminal Court situate within its or his local jurisdiction for the purpose of satisfying itself or himself as to the correctness, legality or propriety of any finding, sentence or order, recorded or passed, and as to the regularity of any proceedings of such inferior Court, and [[?may]], when calling for such record, direct that the execution of any sentence or order be suspended, and if the accused is in confinement that he be released on his own bond or bail bond pending the examination of the record.
Explanation.—All Magistrates, whether Executive or Judicial, and whether exercising original or appellate jurisdiction, [[!shall]] be deemed to be inferior to the Sessions Judge for the purposes of this sub-section and of section 439.
(2) The powers of revision conferred by sub-section (1) [[!shall]] not be exercised in relation to any interlocutory order passed in any appeal, inquiry, trial or other proceeding.
(3) If an application under this section has been made by any person either to the High Court or to the Sessions Judge, no further application by the same person [[!shall]] be entertained by the other of them."
:::

::: proviso Section 439 — Power to order inquiry
"439. On examining any record under section 438 or otherwise, the High Court or the Sessions Judge [[?may]] direct the Chief Judicial Magistrate by himself or by any of the Magistrates subordinate to him to make, and the Chief Judicial Magistrate [[?may]] himself make or direct any subordinate Magistrate to make, further inquiry into any complaint which has been dismissed under section 226 or sub-section (4) of section 227, or into the case of any person accused of an offence who has been discharged:
Provided that no Court [[!shall]] make any direction under this section for inquiry into the case of any person who has been discharged unless such person has had an opportunity of showing cause why such direction should not be made."
:::

### In plain English

Revision is the High Court's or a Sessions Judge's housekeeping check on the courts below them — not a fresh trial, but a look at whether the record shows the finding, sentence, or order was correct, legal, and proper, and whether the proceedings that produced it were regular. Section 438 is the entry point: it lets either forum pull the record and, while doing so, put the sentence or order on hold and get a confined accused out on bond or bail while the review happens. Two guardrails keep this from becoming unlimited: interlocutory orders are off-limits entirely, and a litigant only gets one shot — apply to the High Court or the Sessions Judge, not both. Section 439 is the specific, sharpest tool that can come out of a revision: if the record shows a complaint was wrongly dismissed, or an accused wrongly discharged, the reviewing court can order the Chief Judicial Magistrate (or a Magistrate under him) to dig further — but if it is a discharge being revisited, the discharged person has to be heard first on why that should not happen, since a discharge is exactly the kind of relief a person will reasonably have relied on.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct A person applies to a Sessions Judge under section 438 seeking revision of a Magistrate's order. The Sessions Judge declines to interfere. Can the same person then apply to the High Court on the very same order?

::: ruling No. Section 438(3) provides that once an application under this section has been made to either the High Court or the Sessions Judge, no further application by the same person on the same matter shall be entertained by the other of them -- and this bar operates regardless of whether the first forum granted or refused relief. Having chosen the Sessions Judge first, the applicant cannot then go to the High Court on the same order; the remedy, if any, against the Sessions Judge's refusal lies elsewhere, not in a second bite under section 438 before a different forum.
:::
::::

:::: instinct On examining a record under section 438, a Sessions Judge concludes that an accused person's discharge at the inquiry stage was clearly wrong on the material available, and immediately directs the Chief Judicial Magistrate to conduct further inquiry into that person's case. The discharged person was never given notice or an opportunity to respond before the direction issued. Is the direction valid?

::: ruling No. Section 439's proviso is an express, non-negotiable precondition specifically for directions concerning a discharged person: no court shall make such a direction unless that person has had an opportunity of showing cause why it should not be made. A direction for further inquiry into a discharged person's case, issued without first affording that opportunity, is made in violation of the proviso and is vulnerable to challenge on that ground alone -- regardless of how strong the underlying case for further inquiry might otherwise be.
:::
::::

#### Sword and shield

For an **applicant** seeking revision, section 438's twin safeguards cut both ways: use the interlocutory-order bar defensively when opposing an opponent's premature revision application against a mid-proceeding order, but respect it offensively too -- do not waste an application on an order that is, on close analysis, interlocutory. For the **defence** of a discharged accused, section 439's proviso is a genuine procedural shield -- any further-inquiry direction issued without first giving the discharged person an opportunity to show cause is challengeable on that basis alone, independent of the merits of the underlying case for further inquiry.

#### Strategy and drafting

- Before filing a revision application under section 438, choose the forum -- High Court or Sessions Judge -- deliberately and finally; section 438(3)'s one-forum rule means there is no opportunity to try the other if the first choice does not succeed.
- Screen the order under challenge for its interlocutory character before filing; an application against a genuinely interlocutory order is barred outright by section 438(2) and invites summary rejection.
- Where seeking a section 439 further-inquiry direction against a discharged person, build the show-cause opportunity into the application itself -- propose that the discharged person be put on notice and heard, rather than waiting for the court to raise the proviso on its own.
- When defending a discharged client against a section 439 application, check first and immediately whether the show-cause opportunity was actually given before addressing the substantive case for further inquiry -- a proviso violation can dispose of the application without reaching the merits.

## Sections 440–441 — The Sessions Judge's own revisional powers, and what an Additional Sessions Judge can do

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Section 438 lets a Sessions Judge call for a record and check it; this cluster is about what the Sessions Judge can actually do once that record is in hand, and who else can wear the Sessions Judge's hat for this purpose. Section 440(1) gives the Sessions Judge, for a proceeding whose record the Sessions Judge personally called for, all the powers a High Court could exercise under section 442(1) — the substantive revisional toolkit. Section 440(2) imports the surrounding procedural rules from section 442's other sub-sections into Sessions-Judge-level revision, reading references to the High Court as references to the Sessions Judge. Section 440(3) closes the loop with a finality rule: once the Sessions Judge decides a revision application from a particular person, that person cannot then go to the High Court or any other court on the same matter. Section 441 is a short but important structural provision — it lets an Additional Sessions Judge exercise every one of the Sessions Judge's powers under this Chapter for any case transferred to him.
:::

::: story A Sessions Judge who calls for the record personally
A Sessions Judge, concerned about how a Magistrate handled a bail-related order, calls for the record under section 438 on his own initiative. Having examined it, section 440(1) means the Sessions Judge is not limited to some watered-down version of revisional power — he can exercise all or any of the powers a High Court would have under section 442(1) on the very same record. Because section 440(2) imports section 442's surrounding procedural safeguards, the Sessions Judge must, among other things, avoid prejudicing the accused without a hearing opportunity, and cannot use the power to convert an acquittal into a conviction. Once the Sessions Judge disposes of the matter, section 440(3) closes the door on the applicant going to the High Court afterward. Meanwhile, in a separate district, the regular Sessions Judge's caseload is heavy, and a batch of cases is transferred by general order to the Additional Sessions Judge; section 441 confirms the Additional Sessions Judge has exactly the same Chapter XXXII powers over those transferred cases as the Sessions Judge would have had.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 440 — Sessions Judge's powers of revision
"440. (1) In the case of any proceeding the record of which has been called for by himself, the Sessions Judge [[?may]] exercise all or any of the powers which [[?may]] be exercised by the High Court under sub-section (1) of section 442.
(2) Where any proceeding by way of revision is commenced before a Sessions Judge under sub-section (1), the provisions of sub-sections (2), (3), (4) and (5) of section 442 [[!shall]], so far as may be, apply to such proceeding and references in the said sub-sections to the High Court [[!shall]] be construed as references to the Sessions Judge.
(3) Where any application for revision is made by or on behalf of any person before the Sessions Judge, the decision of the Sessions Judge thereon in relation to such person [[!shall]] be final and no further proceeding by way of revision at the instance of such person [[!shall]] be entertained by the High Court or any other Court."
:::

::: proviso Section 441 — Power of Additional Sessions Judge
"441. An Additional Sessions Judge [[!shall]] have and [[?may]] exercise all the powers of a Sessions Judge under this Chapter in respect of any case which [[?may]] be transferred to him by or under any general or special order of the Sessions Judge."
:::

### In plain English

Once a Sessions Judge has pulled a record under section 438, section 440 tells him exactly how much power he has over it — and the answer is: the same substantive toolkit a High Court would have under section 442(1), not some reduced version. Section 440(2) then makes sure the procedural guardrails that apply to a High Court's revisional power — the hearing-opportunity requirement, the bar on converting acquittal into conviction, the rules about when revision is barred because an appeal was available — apply just as fully at the Sessions Judge level, simply reading 'High Court' as 'Sessions Judge' throughout. Section 440(3) is the finality backstop: once the Sessions Judge has ruled on a particular person's revision application, that is genuinely the end of the road for that person on that matter — no second attempt at the High Court. Section 441 is purely structural but practically important for busy districts: an Additional Sessions Judge is not some lesser official for these purposes — transfer a case to him by general or special order, and he has every power under this Chapter that the Sessions Judge himself would have had.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct A Sessions Judge exercises revisional power under section 440(1) over a record he called for himself, and dismisses the revision application. The applicant, dissatisfied, files a fresh revision application on the same matter before the High Court. Should the High Court entertain it?

::: ruling No. Section 440(3) makes the Sessions Judge's decision on a revision application final as against the person who made it, and expressly bars any further proceeding by way of revision at that person's instance before the High Court or any other court. The outcome being unfavourable to the applicant does not create an exception -- the finality rule applies regardless of which way the Sessions Judge decided. The High Court should decline to entertain the fresh application on this ground.
:::

There is no narrower reading under which the Additional Sessions Judge gets only some of these powers; the transfer carries the full Chapter XXXII authority with it.

#### Sword and shield

For an **applicant**, section 440(3)'s finality rule means the choice to approach the Sessions Judge first (rather than the High Court directly, where the underlying section 438 choice is still open) should be made deliberately -- there is no appeal or further revision from an adverse Sessions Judge decision on the same matter at the same person's instance. For a party facing a revision application before an **Additional Sessions Judge**, section 441 forecloses any argument that the Additional Sessions Judge lacks jurisdiction merely because he is not the Sessions Judge himself -- a properly transferred case carries full Chapter XXXII authority.

#### The limitation clock

This cluster does not itself prescribe a limitation period; the applicable limitation period for a section 438 application before a Sessions Judge (which section 440's powers operate upon) should be checked against the general limitation framework for revision applications under this Sanhita.

#### Interlocking matrix

- **Section 438 (this Chapter, cluster 2)** — the record-calling power that section 440(1)'s revisional authority operates upon; a section 440 exercise of power presupposes a valid section 438 call for records by the Sessions Judge personally.
- **Section 442 (this Chapter, cluster 4)** — the High Court's own revisional powers and procedural framework, which section 440(1)-(2) imports wholesale (with 'Sessions Judge' substituted for 'High Court') for Sessions-Judge-level revision.
- **General or special transfer orders under the Sessions Judge's administrative authority** — the mechanism by which a case reaches an Additional Sessions Judge for the purposes of section 441; confirm the transfer order's validity and scope before relying on it.

#### Strategy and drafting

- Before filing a revision application with a Sessions Judge, weigh section 440(3)'s finality consequence deliberately against the alternative (where available) of proceeding directly under section 438 before the High Court -- once the Sessions Judge decides, that route is closed for good as against this applicant.
- When a matter has been transferred to an Additional Sessions Judge, confirm the transfer was made by a genuine general or special order of the Sessions Judge before proceeding -- section 441's grant of power is conditioned on a valid transfer, not on the Additional Sessions Judge's mere physical handling of the file.
- In drafting a revision application to a Sessions Judge, address the section 442(2)-(5) procedural requirements imported by section 440(2) directly -- particularly the hearing-opportunity point and the bar on seeking a conversion of acquittal into conviction -- since these constrain the relief that can realistically be sought.

## Sections 442–443 — The High Court's full revisional powers, and deciding which forum handles co-accused revision applications

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute This cluster is the High Court's own revisional toolkit, and the traffic-control rule for when co-convicts split their revision applications between the High Court and a Sessions Judge. Section 442(1) gives the High Court, on a record called for by itself or that otherwise comes to its knowledge, the power to exercise any of the Court of Appeal's powers under sections 427, 430, 431, and 432, or a Court of Session's power under section 344, with an equally-divided-Bench question routed to section 433's third-Judge procedure. Sub-sections (2)-(5) then supply the guardrails: no order to a party's prejudice without a hearing opportunity, no converting an acquittal into a conviction, no revision where an appeal lay and was not brought, and — conversely — a discretion to treat a mistakenly-filed revision application as an appeal where the applicant genuinely believed no appeal lay. Section 443 solves a coordination problem: when co-convicts from the same trial split their revision applications between the High Court and a Sessions Judge, it lets the High Court decide which forum should finally dispose of all of them, with a one-way-only transfer mechanism and its own finality rule once a Sessions Judge disposal happens.
:::

::: story Co-convicts who went to two different courts
Three persons convicted at the same trial each want to challenge aspects of the same judgment by way of revision. Two of them apply to the Sessions Judge; the third, believing the matter more suitable for the High Court, applies directly there. Section 443(1) gives the High Court the power to decide, having regard to the general convenience of the parties and the importance of the questions involved, which of the two courts should finally dispose of all the applications — and if it decides it should handle everything itself, it directs that the Sessions-Judge-pending applications be transferred up; if it decides the Sessions Judge should handle everything, it sends its own applications down. Separately, in an unrelated matter, the High Court examines a record on revision and considers reversing an acquittal outright into a conviction; section 442(3) forecloses that option entirely — whatever else the High Court's revisional power might permit, converting acquittal to conviction is not among them.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 442 — High Court's powers of revision
"442. (1) In the case of any proceeding the record of which has been called for by itself or which otherwise comes to its knowledge, the High Court [[?may]], in its discretion, exercise any of the powers conferred on a Court of Appeal by sections 427, 430, 431 and 432 or on a Court of Session by section 344, and, when the Judges composing the Court of revision are equally divided in opinion, the case [[!shall]] be disposed of in the manner provided by section 433.
(2) No order under this section [[!shall]] be made to the prejudice of the accused or other person unless he has had an opportunity of being heard either personally or by advocate in his own defence.
(3) Nothing in this section [[!shall]] be deemed to authorise a High Court to convert a finding of acquittal into one of conviction.
(4) Where under this Sanhita an appeal lies and no appeal is brought, no proceeding by way of revision [[!shall]] be entertained at the instance of the party who could have appealed.
(5) Where under this Sanhita an appeal lies but an application for revision has been made to the High Court by any person and the High Court is satisfied that such application was made under the erroneous belief that no appeal lies thereto and that it is necessary in the interests of justice so to do, the High Court [[?may]] treat the application for revision as a petition of appeal and deal with the same accordingly."
:::

::: proviso Section 443 — Power of High Court to withdraw or transfer revision cases
"443. (1) Whenever one or more persons convicted at the same trial makes or make application to a High Court for revision and any other person convicted at the same trial makes an application to the Sessions Judge for revision, the High Court [[!shall]] decide, having regard to the general convenience of the parties and the importance of the questions involved, which of the two Courts should finally dispose of the applications for revision and when the High Court decides that all the applications for revision should be disposed of by itself, the High Court [[!shall]] direct that the applications for revision pending before the Sessions Judge be transferred to itself and where the High Court decides that it is not necessary for it to dispose of the applications for revision, it [[!shall]] direct that the applications for revision made to it be transferred to the Sessions Judge.
(2) Whenever any application for revision is transferred to the High Court, that Court [[!shall]] deal with the same as if it were an application duly made before itself.
(3) Whenever any application for revision is transferred to the Sessions Judge, that Judge [[!shall]] deal with the same as if it were an application duly made before himself.
(4) Where an application for revision is transferred by the High Court to the Sessions Judge, no further application for revision [[!shall]] lie to the High Court or to any other Court at the instance of the person or persons whose applications for revision have been disposed of by the Sessions Judge."
:::

### In plain English

Section 442 is the High Court's main revisional engine, and it is powerful — the High Court can reach for the same tools a Court of Appeal or a Court of Session would have, and if its own Bench splits evenly, the third-Judge mechanism in section 433 breaks the tie. But four guardrails keep this from becoming an unbounded do-over: someone has to be heard before an order goes against them, an acquittal can never become a conviction through revision no matter how wrong the acquittal looks, a party who had an appeal available and simply did not use it cannot fall back on revision instead — except in the narrow case where the High Court is genuinely satisfied the applicant mistakenly believed no appeal existed and it is in the interests of justice to simply treat the revision as if it had been filed as an appeal all along. Section 443 solves a much more practical problem: when several people convicted together scatter their revision applications across two different courts, someone has to decide who handles the whole thing, and the Act gives that job to the High Court, guided by convenience and the importance of the legal questions — with a one-way finality rule once the Sessions Judge ends up deciding a transferred batch.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct On revision, the High Court concludes that a trial court's acquittal was plainly against the weight of the evidence and would have been reversed on appeal had the State appealed. No State appeal was in fact filed. Can the High Court use its revisional power under section 442 to convert the acquittal into a conviction?

::: ruling No. Section 442(3) is an absolute bar: nothing in section 442 authorises the High Court to convert a finding of acquittal into one of conviction, regardless of how strongly the High Court may believe the acquittal was wrong on the evidence. The High Court's revisional power, however broad in other respects, simply does not extend that far -- and the State's remedy for a wrongly decided acquittal lies in an appeal under section 419, not in inviting the High Court to achieve the same result through revision.
:::
::::

:::: instinct A convicted person files an application for revision directly to the High Court, genuinely believing that no appeal lay against the order in question. In fact, an appeal did lie, but the person simply did not know this and so did not file one within the appeal period. Is the High Court powerless to help because section 442(4) bars revision where an appeal lay and was not brought?

::: ruling Not necessarily. Section 442(5) is a specific exception built for exactly this situation: where an appeal lay but a revision application was made instead, and the High Court is satisfied both that the application was made under the erroneous belief that no appeal lay and that treating it as an appeal is necessary in the interests of justice, the High Court may treat the revision application as a petition of appeal and deal with it accordingly. This is not automatic -- the applicant must actually satisfy the High Court on both the genuineness of the erroneous belief and the interests-of-justice requirement -- but section 442(4)'s bar is not the end of the road on these facts.

## Sections 444–445 — Whether a party has a right to be heard on revision, and certifying the decision back down to the lower court

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute This final cluster closes out the Chapter with two housekeeping provisions that matter enormously in practice despite their brevity. Section 444 answers a question practitioners get wrong surprisingly often: does a party have a right to be heard, personally or by advocate, before a court exercising revisional power? The answer is no, save where some other provision of the Sanhita expressly says otherwise — but the court retains a discretion to hear any party if it thinks fit. Section 445 is the closing mechanical step of the entire revision process: once a case has actually been revised by the High Court or a Sessions Judge, the decision or order has to be certified, in the manner provided by section 429, down to the court that recorded or passed the original finding, sentence, or order — which must then make conforming orders and, if necessary, amend its own record accordingly.
:::

::: story A revision decided without oral argument, and then sent back down
A person applies for revision, and the High Court decides to dispose of the matter entirely on the papers, without calling either side for oral argument. The applicant later complains that this was procedurally unfair. Section 444 answers the complaint directly: outside of provisions that expressly create a hearing right, no party has any right to be heard, personally or by advocate, before a court exercising revisional power — the court could have heard the parties if it thought fit, but choosing not to is not itself an irregularity. Once the High Court's revision decision is finalised, section 445 takes over: the decision or order must be certified, in the manner provided by section 429, to the court that originally recorded the finding, sentence, or order under review, and that court must then make whatever orders are conformable to what has been certified — amending its own record if that becomes necessary to give effect to the decision.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 444 — Option of Court to hear parties
"444. Save as otherwise expressly provided by this Sanhita, no party has any right to be heard either personally or by an advocate before any Court exercising its powers of revision; but the Court [[?may]], if it thinks fit, when exercising such powers, hear any party either personally or by an advocate."
:::

::: proviso Section 445 — High Court's order to be certified to lower Court
"445. When a case is revised under this Chapter by the High Court or a Sessions Judge, it or he [[!shall]], in the manner provided by section 429, certify its decision or order to the Court by which the finding, sentence or order revised was recorded or passed, and the Court to which the decision or order is so certified [[!shall]] thereupon make such orders as are conformable to the decision so certified, and, if necessary, the record [[!shall]] be amended in accordance therewith."
:::

### In plain English

Section 444 is a genuine trap for anyone who assumes revision works like an ordinary hearing: it does not, by default. Unless some other provision expressly says a party must be heard, nobody has a right to appear and argue before a court exercising revisional power — the court can hear a party if it wants to, but choosing to decide on the papers alone is entirely within its discretion and is not, by itself, an irregularity. That said, this default rule does not swallow provisions elsewhere in the Chapter that do create hearing rights — section 442(2)'s requirement that no prejudicial order issue without a hearing opportunity is the clearest example, and it operates as one of the 'expressly provided' exceptions section 444 itself contemplates. Section 445 is the final mechanical step once a revision is actually decided: the decision has to travel back down, formally certified in the way section 429 prescribes, to whichever court originally made the finding, sentence, or order under review — and that court is not free to improvise; it must make orders that conform to what has been certified, amending its own record if that is what conformity requires.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct A Sessions Judge disposes of a revision application without giving either the applicant or the other side any opportunity for oral hearing, deciding the matter purely on the written record. The applicant argues this violated natural justice. Absent any provision expressly requiring a hearing for this kind of application, does the argument succeed?

:::: instinct The High Court decides a revision application and modifies the sentence originally passed by a Magistrate. ::: ruling Something has gone wrong in the process, though not necessarily at the Magistrate's court's end. If that certification never actually reached the Magistrate's court, the Magistrate's court cannot be faulted for failing to act on a decision it was never formally given; the appropriate step is to follow up with the High Court's registry to ensure the section 445 certification is actually issued and transmitted, after which the Magistrate's court is obliged to make conforming orders and amend its record if necessary.

#### Strategy and drafting

- When filing a revision application, explicitly request an oral hearing and state reasons why the court should exercise its section 444 discretion in the applicant's favour -- do not assume a hearing will be scheduled automatically.
- When opposing a revision application on procedural grounds, check whether the applicant's 'right to be heard' argument actually identifies an express statutory hook, or is instead a generalised fairness claim that section 444's default rule forecloses.
