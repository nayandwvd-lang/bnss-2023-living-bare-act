# Chapter XXVII — Provisions as to Accused Persons of Unsound Mind

## Sections 367–368 — Determining unsoundness of mind at the inquiry stage and at the trial stage

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Two mirror-image provisions govern the same question at two different procedural moments. Section 367 applies when a Magistrate holding an inquiry has reason to believe the person before him is of unsound mind and consequently incapable of making his defence — the Magistrate must have him examined by the civil surgeon, and, if unsoundness is found, referred on to a psychiatrist or clinical psychologist for a further opinion on whether the incapacity is such that he cannot enter a defence. Section 368 asks exactly the same question at the trial stage, before a Magistrate or a Court of Session, once the trial itself has already begun. Both provisions converge on the same fork: if a prima facie case is not made out against the accused even without questioning him, he is discharged and dealt with under section 369 rather than have the inquiry or trial simply postponed; if a prima facie case is made out, the proceeding is postponed for the psychiatrist's or clinical psychologist's recommended treatment period, and the accused is dealt with under section 369 in the meantime. An intellectual-disability finding under section 367(5) takes a narrower path — straight to closure of the inquiry, without the prima-facie-case fork at all.
:::

::: story The inquiry that never gets to a charge, and the trial that stops mid-way
A Magistrate holding an inquiry into a complaint notices that the person before him is unable to follow the questions being put to him and appears disoriented as to time and place. Rather than proceeding to frame anything, the Magistrate first has him examined by the civil surgeon under section 367(1). The civil surgeon finds unsoundness of mind and refers him on to a psychiatrist at the nearest Government hospital. The psychiatrist reports the accused incapable of entering a defence. The Magistrate then does something section 367(4) requires before postponing anything further: examines the prosecution's evidence on the record and hears the accused's advocate, without questioning the accused himself. No prima facie case emerges — the Magistrate discharges the accused outright under section 369, instead of postponing an inquiry that would otherwise have dragged on indefinitely. Contrast a trial already underway: three prosecution witnesses have been examined before the Sessions Judge notices the accused's evident incapacity. Section 368 governs from that point exactly as section 367 would have governed at the inquiry stage — except now it is trial evidence already on record, not a charge yet to be framed, that the Judge examines in deciding whether a prima facie case survives.
:::

> **367. (1)** When a Magistrate holding an inquiry has reason to believe that the person against whom the inquiry is being held is a person of unsound mind and consequently incapable of making his defence, the Magistrate [[!shall]] inquire into the fact of such unsoundness of mind, and [[!shall]] cause such person to be examined by the civil surgeon of the district or such other medical officer as the State Government may direct, and thereupon [[!shall]] examine such surgeon or other medical officer as a witness, and [[!shall]] reduce the examination to writing.
>
> **(2)** If the civil surgeon finds the accused to be a person of unsound mind, he [[!shall]] refer such person to a psychiatrist or clinical psychologist of Government hospital or Government medical college for care, treatment and prognosis of the condition, and the psychiatrist or clinical psychologist, as the case may be, [[!shall]] inform the Magistrate whether the accused is suffering from unsoundness of mind or intellectual disability:
>
> Provided that if the accused is aggrieved by the information given by the psychiatrist or clinical psychologist to the Magistrate, he [[?may]] prefer an appeal before the Medical Board, consisting of (a) the head of the psychiatry unit in the nearest Government hospital, and (b) a faculty member in psychiatry in the nearest Government medical college.
>
> **(3)** Pending such examination and inquiry, the Magistrate [[?may]] deal with such person in accordance with the provisions of section 369.
>
> **(4)** If the Magistrate is informed that the person is a person of unsound mind, the Magistrate [[!shall]] further determine whether the unsoundness of mind renders the accused incapable of entering defence; and if the accused is found so incapable, the Magistrate [[!shall]] record a finding to that effect, and [[!shall]] examine the record of evidence produced by the prosecution and, after hearing the advocate of the accused but without questioning the accused, if no prima facie case is made out against the accused, he [[!shall]], instead of postponing the enquiry, discharge the accused and deal with him under section 369:
>
> Provided that if the Magistrate finds a prima facie case is made out, he [[!shall]] postpone the proceeding for such period as, in the opinion of the psychiatrist or clinical psychologist, is required for the treatment of the accused, and order the accused to be dealt with under section 369.
>
> **(5)** If the Magistrate is informed that the person is a person with intellectual disability, the Magistrate [[!shall]] further determine whether the intellectual disability renders the accused incapable of entering defence; and if the accused is found so incapable, the Magistrate [[!shall]] order closure of the inquiry and deal with the accused under section 369.
>
> **368. (1)** If at the trial of any person before a Magistrate or Court of Session, it appears that such person is of unsound mind and consequently incapable of making his defence, the Magistrate or Court [[!shall]], in the first instance, try the fact of such unsoundness of mind and incapacity, and if satisfied of the fact after considering such medical and other evidence as may be produced, [[!shall]] record a finding to that effect and [[!shall]] postpone further proceedings in the case.
>
> **(2)** If during trial the Magistrate or Court finds the accused to be of unsound mind, he or it [[!shall]] refer such person to a psychiatrist or clinical psychologist for care and treatment, and the psychiatrist or clinical psychologist [[!shall]] report to the Magistrate or Court whether the accused is suffering from unsoundness of mind:
>
> Provided that if the accused is aggrieved by that information, he [[?may]] prefer an appeal before the same Medical Board described in section 367(2).
>
> **(3)** If the Magistrate or Court is informed that the person is a person of unsound mind, the Magistrate or Court [[!shall]] further determine whether the unsoundness of mind renders the accused incapable of entering defence; and if the accused is found so incapable, the Magistrate or Court [[!shall]] record a finding to that effect and [[!shall]] examine the record of evidence produced by the prosecution and, after hearing the advocate of the accused but without questioning the accused, if no prima facie case is made out, he or it [[!shall]], instead of postponing the trial, discharge the accused and deal with him under section 369:
>
> Provided that if a prima facie case is made out, the Magistrate or Court [[!shall]] postpone the trial for such period as, in the opinion of the psychiatrist or clinical psychologist, is required for the treatment of the accused.
>
> **(4)** If a prima facie case is made out and the accused is incapable of entering defence by reason of intellectual disability, the Magistrate or Court [[!shall]] not hold the trial, and [[!shall]] order the accused to be dealt with under section 369.

::: proviso The prima-facie-case fork applies only to unsoundness of mind — an intellectual-disability finding under section 367(5) or 368(4) skips straight to closure, without that fork
Sections 367(4) and 368(3) build in an intermediate check before postponing anything: the Magistrate or Court must look at the prosecution's evidence already on record and, after hearing the accused's advocate but without questioning the accused, ask whether a prima facie case survives at all. If it does not, the accused is discharged outright rather than left in indefinite postponement. Sections 367(5) and 368(4) do not carry that same fork for intellectual disability — the text moves directly to closure of the inquiry, or a bar on holding the trial, once incapacity is found. A practitioner should not assume the two incapacity findings are interchangeable procedurally; they are not.
:::

::: proviso The Medical Board appeal is the accused's route to challenge the psychiatrist's or clinical psychologist's opinion — it does not sit in the Magistrate's or Court's own hands
Both sections 367(2) and 368(2) give the accused, not the prosecution, a right of appeal to a two-member Medical Board — the head of psychiatry at the nearest Government hospital and a psychiatry faculty member at the nearest Government medical college — if he is aggrieved by the psychiatrist's or clinical psychologist's information to the Magistrate or Court. Nothing in the text gives the prosecution a parallel right to appeal a finding that favours the accused.
:::

### In plain English

These two sections ask the identical question at two different moments — before a charge (section 367) and after a trial has started (section 368) — and they answer it the same way. First, get a medical opinion: civil surgeon, then psychiatrist or clinical psychologist if needed, with the accused free to appeal that opinion to a small medical board if he disagrees with it. Second, once incapacity from unsoundness of mind is confirmed, do not simply freeze the case — check whether the prosecution actually has a case at all by reading the record and hearing the defence advocate, without ever putting questions to the incapacitated accused himself. If there is no case, end it there with a discharge. If there is a case, pause it for as long as treatment is expected to take. Intellectual disability is treated differently: once found to render the accused incapable of entering a defence, the inquiry is simply closed, or the trial simply not held — there is no intermediate prima-facie-case check built into the text for that finding.

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

⚠️ *Research gap: no citation meets this guide's confidence bar for this specific procedural cluster. A reader relying on this cluster in practice should independently locate and verify the leading Supreme Court authority on the CrPC predecessor provisions (sections 328-329) before citing one — this guide treats the gap as a research gap, not a citation to be guessed.*

#### Sword and shield

As a **shield**, defence counsel confronted with a client who cannot follow proceedings should press for the section 367/368 inquiry at the earliest possible moment — an incapacitated accused who is nonetheless made to stand trial without this inquiry having been triggered is a live ground of challenge later. As a **sword**, the prosecution's strongest response to a section 367/368 application made purely to delay proceedings is to insist the Magistrate or Court test the "reason to believe" or "appears" threshold rigorously before ordering any medical examination at all — the provision is not a mechanism for an accused to indefinitely stall a proceeding on an unsubstantiated claim of incapacity.

#### The limitation clock

No independent limitation period runs under sections 367-368; the postponement they authorise is expressly tied to "such period as, in the opinion of the psychiatrist or clinical psychologist, is required for the treatment of the accused" — a medically determined period, not a fixed statutory one.

#### Interlocking matrix

Both sections feed directly into section 369 (release on bail or detention pending disposal, built in the next cluster of this chapter) for what happens to the accused once either discharged or found incapable and the proceeding postponed or closed. Section 367(4)'s discharge track echoes the discharge architecture already built under section 262 (warrant-cases) and section 281 (summons-cases stop-proceedings power) in earlier chapters, though the trigger here is incapacity rather than groundlessness of the charge. The Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 supplies the institutional backdrop for the psychiatrist/clinical-psychologist references throughout, though this guide has not independently verified those cross-references against that Act's text.

#### Strategy and drafting

**Model checklist for a section 367/368 application:**

1. State the specific facts giving the Magistrate or Court "reason to believe" (inquiry stage) or making it "appear" (trial stage) that the person is of unsound mind or has an intellectual disability — a bare assertion invites rejection.
2. Confirm whether the medical opinion obtained addresses unsoundness of mind, intellectual disability, or both — the two findings lead down different procedural tracks from that point.
3. If the psychiatrist's or clinical psychologist's opinion is adverse to the accused's own expectation, advise on the Medical Board appeal route under the proviso to section 367(2)/368(2) before treating the opinion as final.
4. Where incapacity from unsoundness of mind is found, press for the prima-facie-case check under section 367(4)/368(3) to actually be conducted on the record — a discharge opportunity is lost if the Magistrate or Court moves straight to postponement without it.

## Sections 369–371 — Release pending disposal, resumption of the proceeding, and reappearance before the Magistrate or Court

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Once incapacity is found under section 367 or section 368, the accused does not simply disappear into custody — section 369 requires the Magistrate or Court to actually consider release. Bail is the default position, even for offences where bail would not ordinarily be available, provided the unsoundness or disability does not require in-patient treatment and a friend or relative undertakes regular out-patient psychiatric care and prevention of harm. Only where bail cannot be granted, or no such undertaking is forthcoming, does detention for regular psychiatric treatment follow — and even then, sub-section (3) requires a further, separate determination: given the nature of the act committed and the extent of the incapacity, can release still be ordered on sufficient security, or must the accused instead be transferred to a residential care facility? Sections 370 and 371 then govern what happens once the person recovers: the Magistrate or Court may resume the proceeding at any time after the person ceases to be of unsound mind, a supervising officer's certificate of capacity is itself receivable in evidence, and on reappearance the Magistrate or Court makes a fresh capacity assessment — proceeding if capable, or cycling back through section 367/368 and section 369 if still incapable.
:::

::: story The undertaking that kept a man out of custody, and the certificate that brought a case back to life
An accused found incapable of making his defence by reason of unsoundness of mind does not need in-patient care — his condition is manageable with regular out-patient treatment. His brother undertakes, in writing, to obtain that treatment from the nearest medical facility and to prevent him from harming himself or anyone else. Section 369(1)'s proviso is satisfied, and the Magistrate releases him on bail despite the underlying charge being one where bail would not otherwise be automatic. Two years later, the surety produces the accused before the officer the Magistrate had appointed for the purpose. That officer certifies the accused is now capable of making his defence. Under section 370(2), that certificate is itself receivable in evidence — the Magistrate does not need to reopen the entire medical inquiry from scratch — and the long-postponed trial resumes.
:::

> **369. (1)** Whenever a person is found under section 367 or section 368 to be incapable of entering defence by reason of unsoundness of mind or intellectual disability, the Magistrate or Court, as the case may be, [[!shall]], whether the case is one in which bail may be taken or not, order release of such person on bail:
>
> Provided that the accused is suffering from unsoundness of mind or intellectual disability which does not mandate in-patient treatment and a friend or relative undertakes to obtain regular out-patient psychiatric treatment from the nearest medical facility and to prevent him from doing injury to himself or to any other person.
>
> **(2)** If the case is one in which, in the opinion of the Magistrate or Court, bail cannot be granted, or if an appropriate undertaking is not given, he or it [[!shall]] order the accused to be kept in such a place where regular psychiatric treatment can be provided, and [[!shall]] report the action taken to the State Government:
>
> Provided that no order for the detention of the accused in a public mental health establishment [[!shall]] be made otherwise than in accordance with such rules as the State Government may have made under the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017.
>
> **(3)** Whenever a person is found under section 367 or section 368 to be incapable of entering defence by reason of unsoundness of mind or intellectual disability, the Magistrate or Court, keeping in view the nature of the act committed and the extent of unsoundness of mind or intellectual disability, [[!shall]] further determine if the release of the accused can be ordered:
>
> Provided that —
>
> (a) if on the basis of medical opinion or opinion of a specialist, the Magistrate or Court decides to order discharge of the accused, as provided under section 367 or section 368, such release [[?may]] be ordered, if sufficient security is given that the accused shall be prevented from doing injury to himself or to any other person;
>
> (b) if the Magistrate or Court is of the opinion that discharge of the accused cannot be ordered, the transfer of the accused to a residential facility for persons with unsoundness of mind or intellectual disability [[?may]] be ordered wherein the accused may be provided care and appropriate education and training.
>
> **370. (1)** Whenever an inquiry or a trial is postponed under section 367 or section 368, the Magistrate or Court [[?may]] at any time after the person concerned has ceased to be of unsound mind, resume the inquiry or trial and require the accused to appear or be brought before such Magistrate or Court.
>
> **(2)** When the accused has been released under section 369, and the sureties for his appearance produce him to the officer whom the Magistrate or Court appoints in this behalf, the certificate of such officer that the accused is capable of making his defence [[!shall]] be receivable in evidence.
>
> **371. (1)** If, when the accused appears or is again brought before the Magistrate or Court, the Magistrate or Court considers him capable of making his defence, the inquiry or trial [[!shall]] proceed.
>
> **(2)** If the Magistrate or Court considers the accused to be still incapable of making his defence, the Magistrate or Court [[!shall]] act according to the provisions of section 367 or section 368, as the case may be, and if the accused is found to be of unsound mind and consequently incapable of making his defence, [[!shall]] deal with such accused in accordance with the provisions of section 369.

::: proviso Section 369(1) makes bail the default, overriding the ordinary bailability of the offence — but only if the treatment-and-prevention undertaking is actually given
The opening words of section 369(1) — "whether the case is one in which bail may be taken or not" — mean the ordinary bailable/non-bailable classification of the offence does not control this decision. But the release is not unconditional: the proviso requires both that the condition does not mandate in-patient treatment, and that a friend or relative undertakes regular out-patient treatment and prevention of harm. Absent that undertaking, sub-section (2) takes over and detention for treatment follows instead.
:::

::: proviso Sub-section (3) is a second, independent determination — it does not automatically track whatever was decided under sub-sections (1)-(2)
A practitioner should not assume that once release or detention has been decided under sub-sections (1)-(2), the matter is settled. Sub-section (3) requires the Magistrate or Court to separately weigh the nature of the act committed and the extent of the incapacity, and choose between a security-backed release (proviso (a)) and transfer to a residential care-and-training facility (proviso (b)). The two sub-sections address related but distinct questions: (1)-(2) ask whether bail or treatment-detention is appropriate in the first instance; (3) asks, given everything now known about the act and the condition, whether release can still be ordered at all.
:::

### In plain English

A finding of incapacity is not an off-ramp into indefinite custody — the Sanhita builds in a strong presumption toward bail, backed by a family undertaking to manage treatment and prevent harm, and it overrides the offence's ordinary bail classification to do so. Detention for treatment is the fallback, not the default. Even then, the statute does not treat the case as closed: a further, independent look at the nature of the act and the extent of the incapacity decides whether a security-backed release is still possible, or whether a residential care facility is the better course. And none of this is meant to be permanent — the Magistrate or Court can resume the case the moment the person recovers, a supervising officer's certificate makes that easy to establish, and reappearance triggers a fresh, honest capacity check rather than an automatic resumption of business as usual.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct An accused is found incapable of making his defence by reason of unsoundness of mind, in a case where the underlying offence is non-bailable. Section 369(1) opens with "whether the case is one in which bail may be taken or not," and the proviso's conditions — no in-patient treatment required, and a relative's undertaking to obtain treatment and prevent harm — are both met on these facts. The Magistrate or Court must order release on bail.

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

⚠️ *Research gap: no citation meets this guide's confidence bar for this cluster. A reader relying on the bail-as-default architecture of section 369 in practice should independently locate and verify the leading authority on the CrPC predecessor provisions (sections 330-332) before citing one.*

#### Sword and shield

As a **shield**, defence counsel should have the friend-or-relative undertaking under section 369(1)'s proviso drafted and ready before the incapacity finding is even made — a Magistrate cannot act on an undertaking that has not yet been offered, and delay here means avoidable time in treatment-detention. As a **sword**, the prosecution's realistic point of resistance is not the bail decision itself but the section 369(3) determination — where the nature of the act is serious, press for the residential-facility route under proviso (b) rather than a security-backed release under proviso (a), and put the medical or specialist opinion supporting that position on the record.

#### The limitation clock

No independent limitation period runs under sections 369-371. Postponement under section 367/368, read with resumption under section 370, is open-ended and tied to the accused's medical recovery, not to a filing deadline; nothing in these sections is capable of lapsing merely with the passage of time.

#### Interlocking matrix

Section 369 operates only once section 367 or section 368 has produced a finding of incapacity — it has no independent trigger of its own. Section 369(2)'s proviso cross-refers to rules the State Government may make under the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 (⚠️ outside this guide's verified corpus). Section 370(1)'s resumption power and section 371's fresh-assessment mechanism together close the loop opened by sections 367-368, returning the case either to ordinary trial footing or back through the same incapacity-determination cycle.

#### Strategy and drafting

**Model checklist for a section 369 bail application following an incapacity finding:**

1. Secure the friend-or-relative undertaking in writing before the hearing, specifying the treatment facility and the concrete steps to prevent harm to the accused or others.
2. Distinguish clearly, on the record, between the section 369(1)-(2) bail-or-detention decision and the separate section 369(3) release-or-transfer determination — do not let the two be conflated into a single ruling.
3. Where in-patient treatment may be required, obtain and file the specific medical or specialist opinion the Magistrate or Court needs under section 369(3) before the hearing, rather than leaving that assessment to be made without one.
4. On recovery, apply promptly for resumption under section 370(1) and tender the supervising officer's certificate under section 370(2) — but be ready to support it with argument, since section 371(1) requires the Magistrate's own satisfaction on capacity, not mere production of the certificate.
