# Chapter IV — Powers of Superior Officers of Police and Aid to the Magistrates and the Police

## Sections 30–32 — A superior officer's rank power across his jurisdiction, and the public's duty to lend a hand

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Two short mechanical sections and one civic-duty section: who else can exercise a station officer's powers, and when the ordinary public is legally required to pitch in.
Chapter IV is short by design — three sections that answer basic operational questions the rest of the Sanhita assumes are already settled: does a superior-ranked officer lose his powers the moment he steps outside a particular police station's walls, is an ordinary member of the public ever legally obligated to help catch a fleeing suspect or stop a public disturbance, and can someone who is not a police officer at all still lawfully assist in executing a warrant. Sections 30 to 32 answer all three, cleanly and briefly.
:::

::: story The bystander who almost got it wrong
A Sub-Inspector chases a robbery suspect fleeing on foot through a crowded market. Winded, and about to lose the suspect at a busy crossing, the officer shouts to a nearby shopkeeper to block the man's path. The shopkeeper hesitates, unsure whether helping a police officer catch a runaway person is even his business, or whether he could be blamed for laying hands on a stranger. Section 31 settles the shopkeeper's hesitation in the officer's favour: the public is bound to assist a police officer reasonably demanding aid in exactly this situation — taking or preventing the escape of a person the officer is authorised to arrest.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 30 — Powers of superior officers of police
"30. Police officers superior in rank to an officer in charge of a police station [[?may]] exercise the same powers, throughout the local area to which they are appointed, as [[?may]] be exercised by such officer within the limits of his station."
:::

::: proviso Section 31 — Public when to assist Magistrates and police
"31. Every person is bound to assist a Magistrate or police officer reasonably demanding his aid—
(a) in the taking or preventing the escape of any other person whom such Magistrate or police officer is authorised to arrest; or
(b) in the prevention or suppression of a breach of the peace; or
(c) in the prevention of any injury attempted to be committed to any public property."
:::

::: proviso Section 32 — Aid to person, other than police officer, executing warrant
"32. When a warrant is directed to a person other than a police officer, any other person [[?may]] aid in the execution of such warrant, if the person to whom the warrant is directed be near at hand and acting in the execution of the warrant."
:::

### In plain English

Section 30 is a simple rule about a chain of command: a Superintendent, for example, can do everything a Station House Officer can do, but only inside the area the Superintendent himself is posted to — rank is not a passport to act anywhere in the State. A Superintendent posted to one district has no section 30 power to walk into a police station in a different district merely because he outranks its officer in charge; the territorial limit is tied to the appointment, not to seniority alone. Section 31 puts a real, enforceable duty on ordinary people, but only for three specific things: helping catch or stop the escape of someone the officer is already authorised to arrest, helping stop or prevent a breach of the peace, and helping prevent damage to public property — it does not create a general duty to help police with anything they ask. Section 32 is a narrow, practical rule for the situation where a warrant is addressed to a private person rather than a police officer: someone else nearby can lawfully pitch in, so long as the person the warrant was actually addressed to is present and is himself acting on it.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct The shopkeeper who refuses to help
A police officer, out of breath and chasing a pickpocketing suspect, asks a nearby shopkeeper to physically block the suspect's path. The shopkeeper refuses, saying it is not his job. The officer later wants to know whether the shopkeeper committed any wrong by refusing.
::: ruling What actually happens
Section 31 makes the shopkeeper legally bound to assist in exactly this situation — preventing the escape of a person the officer is authorised to arrest is one of the three listed categories. Refusal without lawful excuse is itself capable of amounting to an offence under the relevant provision of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 dealing with omission to assist a public servant when legally bound to do so; the shopkeeper's belief that helping police was optional is simply wrong on the text of section 31.
:::
::::

:::: instinct The out-of-district Superintendent
A Superintendent of Police posted to District A visits District B and, citing his rank, attempts to direct the investigation being run by the officer in charge of a police station in District B, over that officer's objection.
::: ruling What actually happens
Section 30 does not assist the visiting Superintendent. The section's power is expressly limited to "the local area to which they are appointed" — his own appointment is to District A, not District B, so his superior rank alone carries no section 30 authority to displace or direct the District B officer in charge within District B's police station.
:::
::::

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

::: info Research gap — no case promoted
No single Supreme Court authority is promoted to a winning ratio for this cluster. Sections 30 to 32 are narrow, mechanical provisions on rank, civic duty and warrant-execution assistance that are rarely the subject of dedicated appellate litigation in their own right — they typically surface only as a subordinate point within a larger dispute about the validity of an arrest or a warrant's execution.
:::

#### Sword and shield

As a **sword**, a prosecuting authority can invoke section 31 to establish that a bystander's refusal to assist in an arrest, breach-of-peace prevention, or protection of public property was itself unlawful, supporting a separate charge for omission to assist. As a **shield**, a person who assisted in executing someone else's warrant under section 32 can point to the section as authority for having lawfully acted, provided the actual addressee of the warrant was present and participating. A person accused of exceeding his authority under section 30 can equally raise the territorial limit as a shield, showing the act complained of was, in fact, within his own appointed local area.

#### The limitation clock

No independent limitation period attaches to ss. 30–32; any objection to an officer having acted outside section 30's territorial limit, or to the validity of assistance rendered under section 32, should be raised at the point the resulting action — arrest, seizure, or warrant execution — is first challenged.

#### Interlocking matrix

| Situation | Governing provision | What follows |
|---|---|---|
| Senior-ranked officer acting within his own appointed area | Section 30 | Same powers as the local officer in charge, confined to that area |
| Public asked to assist arrest, prevent breach of peace, or protect public property | Section 31 | Legally bound to assist within these three categories only |
| Non-police person aiding execution of a warrant addressed to another non-police person | Section 32 | Lawful, provided the actual addressee is near at hand and acting on the warrant |

#### Strategy and drafting

**Drafting traps.**

1. Do not assume a senior officer's rank alone justifies action outside his own appointed local area — check the appointment, not just the rank, before relying on section 30.
2. Do not treat section 31's duty to assist as a general one — it is confined to arrest-related escape prevention, breach-of-peace prevention, and protection of public property; do not stretch it to any request for help.
3. Do not overlook that section 32 requires the actual warrant-addressee to be near at hand and acting on the warrant — a stranger cannot execute someone else's warrant alone merely by claiming to assist.

**Timing.** Raise a section 30 territorial-limit objection as early as possible, ideally at the first hearing where the senior officer's action is relied upon, rather than after the underlying investigative step has already produced evidence.

**Model checklist for reviewing a superior-officer or civic-assistance action:**

1. Confirm the senior officer's own appointment covers the local area where he purported to exercise station-officer powers.
2. Confirm any claimed section 31 duty falls within one of the three listed categories, not a broader request.
3. Confirm that, where non-police assistance in warrant execution is claimed under section 32, the actual addressee was present and acting on the warrant.

## Sections 33–34 — The citizen's duty to report grave offences, and the village-level duty to keep the authorities informed

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Two duties to speak up: one binds every person who knows of a specified serious offence, the other binds village officials and residents on a wider range of local information.
Sections 33 and 34 impose affirmative duties to inform the authorities — not permissions, duties — but they are calibrated differently. Section 33 targets any person, anywhere, who is aware of the commission or intended commission of a closed list of especially serious Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita offences, and shifts the burden of proving a reasonable excuse for silence onto that person. Section 34 targets village-level officials and residents specifically, and covers a broader menu of local information — from the presence of a receiver of stolen property to an unexplained death — reflecting the village as the smallest unit where such information is likely to first surface.
:::

::: story The neighbour who knew and said nothing
A resident of a small town learns, from a chance remark by an acquaintance, that a violent offence covered by section 33's list is about to be committed nearby. He decides it is not his concern and says nothing to anyone. When the offence occurs and the earlier remark comes to light, he is asked to explain his silence. Section 33 places the burden squarely on him: once aware of the intended commission of a listed offence, silence without a reasonable excuse is itself made a distinct wrong, and it is for him — not the prosecution — to establish the excuse.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 33 — Public to give information of certain offences
"33. (1) Every person, aware of the commission of, or of the intention of any other person to commit, any offence punishable under any of the following sections of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, namely:—
(i) sections 103 to 105 (both inclusive);
(ii) sections 111 to 113 (both inclusive);
(iii) sections 140 to 144 (both inclusive);
(iv) sections 147 to 154 (both inclusive) and section 158;
(v) sections 178 to 182 (both inclusive);
(vi) sections 189 and 191;
(vii) sections 274 to 280 (both inclusive);
(viii) section 307;
(ix) sections 309 to 312 (both inclusive);
(x) sub-section (5) of section 316;
(xi) sections 326 to 328 (both inclusive); and
(xii) sections 331 and 332,
[[!shall]], in the absence of any reasonable excuse, the burden of proving which excuse shall lie upon the person so aware, forthwith give information to the nearest Magistrate or police officer of such commission or intention.
(2) For the purposes of this section, the term 'offence' includes any act committed at any place out of India which would constitute an offence if committed in India."
:::

::: proviso Section 34 — Duty of officers employed in connection with affairs of a village to make certain report
"34. (1) Every officer employed in connection with the affairs of a village and every person residing in a village [[!shall]] forthwith communicate to the nearest Magistrate or to the officer in charge of the nearest police station, whichever is nearer, any information which he may possess respecting—
(a) the permanent or temporary residence of any notorious receiver or vendor of stolen property in or near such village;
(b) the resort to any place within, or the passage through, such village of any person whom he knows, or reasonably suspects, to be a robber, escaped convict or proclaimed offender;
(c) the commission of, or intention to commit, in or near such village any non-bailable offence or any offence punishable under section 189 and section 191 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023;
(d) the occurrence in or near such village of any sudden or unnatural death or of any death under suspicious circumstances or the discovery in or near such village of any corpse or part of a corpse, in circumstances which lead to a reasonable suspicion that such a death has occurred or the disappearance from such village of any person in circumstances which lead to a reasonable suspicion that a non-bailable offence has been committed in respect of such person;
(e) the commission of, or intention to commit, at any place out of India near such village any act which, if committed in India, would be an offence punishable under any of the following sections of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, namely, 103, 105, 111, 112, 113, 178 to 181 (both inclusive), 305, 307, 309 to 312 (both inclusive), clauses (f) and (g) of section 326, 331 or 332;
(f) any matter likely to affect the maintenance of order or the prevention of crime or the safety of person or property respecting which the District Magistrate, by general or special order made with the previous sanction of the State Government, has directed him to communicate information.
(2) In this section,—
(i) 'village' includes village lands;
(ii) the expression 'proclaimed offender' includes any person proclaimed as an offender by any Court or authority in any territory in India to which this Sanhita does not extend, in respect of any act which if committed in the territories to which this Sanhita extends, would be an offence punishable with imprisonment for ten years or more or with imprisonment for life or with death under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023;
(iii) the words 'officer employed in connection with the affairs of the village' means a member of the panchayat of the village and includes the headman and every officer or other person appointed to perform any function connected with the administration of the village."
:::

### In plain English

Section 33 is a targeted, serious duty: if a person actually knows a listed grave offence — covering categories such as offences against the State, certain offences against the human body, and offences affecting public tranquillity — has been or is about to be committed, silence is not a neutral option. The person has to come forward, and if challenged, has to prove why not speaking up was reasonable; it is not the State's job to prove the silence was unreasonable. Both section 33(2) and section 34(1)(e) reach beyond India's borders too — an offence that would qualify under the listed sections if committed in India triggers the same duty even where it is committed, or intended to be committed, abroad. Section 34 is broader in subject-matter but narrower in audience: it binds village officials and residents specifically, and asks them to pass on a wider range of practical local intelligence — known criminals in the area, suspicious deaths, and anything the District Magistrate has specifically directed be reported — to whichever of the Magistrate or the nearest police station is closer.

### What changed from the CrPC

::: info Verify before citing
BNSS sections 33 and 34 are provisionally mapped to CrPC sections 39 and 40 respectively. This mapping is unverified against the CrPC bare-act text and must be independently confirmed before use in any filing.
:::

Both sections track their presumed CrPC ancestors closely in structure and substance — a closed list of serious offences triggering a public duty to inform, and a separate, broader village-level reporting duty. No structural change is apparent from the text itself; the drafter has not identified any digitisation-related or timeline-related addition in this cluster, unlike many of the investigation-stage chapters elsewhere in the Sanhita.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct The overheard conversation on a train
A passenger overhears two strangers discussing, in some detail, a plan to commit an offence falling within section 33's list, but does not know either person's identity or where the offence is planned to occur. The passenger says nothing, reasoning that without names or a location, there is nothing useful to report.
::: ruling What actually happens
Section 33(1) does not condition the duty to inform on the informant possessing complete, actionable details — it is triggered by awareness of the intention to commit a listed offence. The passenger's assessment that the information was not useful enough to report is not, by itself, a reasonable excuse; the section places the burden on the passenger to establish that the incompleteness of the information genuinely justified the silence, not on the prosecution to show the information would have been useful.
:::
::::

:::: instinct The village headman who stayed silent about a suspicious death
A body is discovered near a village under circumstances suggesting an unnatural death. The village headman, aware of the discovery, does not report it to the nearest Magistrate or police station, believing it is a matter for the family to handle privately.
::: ruling What actually happens
Section 34(1)(d) squarely covers the discovery of a corpse in circumstances raising a reasonable suspicion of an unnatural or suspicious death, and section 34(1) makes the duty to report this to the nearest Magistrate or police station mandatory for a person employed in connection with the affairs of the village, which includes a headman. The private character of the family's grief does not displace the reporting duty; the headman's silence is not a matter of discretion.
:::

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

::: info Research gap — no case promoted
No single Supreme Court authority is promoted to a winning ratio for this cluster. Prosecutions squarely built on a bare failure to inform under section 33 or section 34 are uncommon in reported appellate jurisprudence, which more often addresses these sections as background context to a separate substantive charge.
:::

#### Sword and shield

As a **sword**, an investigating agency can rely on section 33 to argue that a witness's prior silence about a listed offence, once shown to have been aware, was itself unlawful and undermines any later claim of innocent non-involvement. As a **shield**, a person accused under section 33 or 34 can raise a genuinely reasonable excuse — for example, a credible and immediate fear for personal safety — since the section expressly contemplates that an excuse can defeat the duty, once proved.

#### The limitation clock

No independent limitation period attaches to ss. 33–34; the more pressing timing question in practice is how promptly information was communicated once the person became aware of it, since both sections use the word "forthwith" as the operative standard rather than fixing a numbered period.

#### Strategy and drafting

**Drafting traps.**

1. Do not treat section 33 as a general duty to report all offences — confirm the offence actually falls within the closed list of Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita sections named in section 33(1) before alleging a breach.
2. Do not overlook that the burden of proving a reasonable excuse under section 33 sits on the person who stayed silent — a defence strategy built on the prosecution having to disprove an excuse is built on the wrong premise.
3. Do not assume section 34 binds only formally appointed village officials — it also binds every person residing in the village, a wider class than the drafting sometimes suggests.

**Timing.** Where a section 33 or section 34 breach is alleged, establish as early as possible exactly when the person became aware of the relevant information, since "forthwith" is measured from that point, not from some later, more convenient date.

**Model checklist for reviewing an alleged failure to inform:**

1. Confirm the offence in question actually falls within section 33(1)'s closed list, or within one of section 34(1)'s six categories.
2. Confirm the point in time the person is shown to have become aware of the relevant information.
3. Identify and evaluate any reasonable excuse for the delay or silence, bearing in mind which side carries the burden of proving it.
