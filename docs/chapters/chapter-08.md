# Chapter VIII — Reciprocal Arrangements for Assistance in Certain Matters and Procedure for Attachment and Forfeiture of Property

## Section 111 — The five definitions that anchor this Chapter's cross-border and forfeiture machinery

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute This Chapter does two distinct jobs under one roof: it builds a cross-border mutual-assistance channel for Indian courts and foreign "contracting States" to help each other gather evidence and execute process, and it builds a self-contained, court-driven attachment-and-forfeiture regime for property traceable to crime, whether that property sits in India or abroad. Section 111 is the glossary that makes both halves work without constant re-explanation. "Contracting State" fixes which foreign countries this Chapter's assistance provisions actually reach — only those with a Central Government arrangement, by treaty or otherwise. "Proceeds of crime" and "property" are defined broadly and deliberately overlap — property includes not just the asset itself but the deeds and instruments evidencing title to it, and expressly includes property obtained through proceeds of crime, closing the obvious loophole of laundering proceeds into a different asset. "Identifying" and "tracing" are the two investigative verbs the rest of the Chapter builds on — identifying is about proving the crime-to-property link, tracing is about following the property's nature, source, movement, and ownership.
:::

::: story Two disputes that turn entirely on how these words are read
In one matter, an accused argues that funds sitting in a foreign bank account cannot be "proceeds of crime" because they were converted from the originally stolen currency into a different asset before being deposited. Section 111(c)'s definition of proceeds of crime as property derived "directly or indirectly" from criminal activity defeats that argument — indirect derivation is expressly covered, and section 111(d)'s definition of property expressly includes "property obtained through proceeds of crime," so the conversion changes nothing. In a separate matter, an investigating agency seeks assistance from a foreign country with which India has no treaty and no other arrangement. Because that country does not meet section 111(a)'s definition of "contracting State," none of this Chapter's letter-of-request or warrant-transmission machinery is available for that country — assistance, if it exists at all, has to be sought through some other channel entirely.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 111 — Definitions
"111. In this Chapter, unless the context otherwise requires,—
(a) 'contracting State' means any country or place outside India in respect of which arrangements have been made by the Central Government with the Government of such country through a treaty or otherwise;
(b) 'identifying' includes establishment of a proof that the property was derived from, or used in, the commission of an offence;
(c) 'proceeds of crime' means any property derived or obtained directly or indirectly, by any person as a result of criminal activity (including crime involving currency transfers) or the value of any such property;
(d) 'property' means property and assets of every description whether corporeal or incorporeal, movable or immovable, tangible or intangible and deeds and instruments evidencing title to, or interest in, such property or assets derived or used in the commission of an offence and includes property obtained through proceeds of crime;
(e) 'tracing' means determining the nature, source, disposition, movement, title or ownership of property."
:::

### In plain English

Read this section before anything else in the Chapter, because every later provision leans on it. The practical stakes are highest in the proceeds-of-crime and property definitions: they are written to reach money and assets that have been moved, converted, or laundered through several hands, not just the original stolen item sitting untouched. "Contracting State" is the gatekeeper for the whole mutual-assistance half of the Chapter — no arrangement with a country, no letters of request to or from it, no warrant transmission to or from it, regardless of how cooperative that country's authorities might personally be willing to be.

This correspondence is provisional and unverified against the CrPC bare-act text, and the exact section-to-section mapping across the Chapter must be independently confirmed before use in any filing.
:::

The definitions in section 111 read as tracking the presumed CrPC Chapter VII-A definitional scheme closely in substance, including the deliberately broad "directly or indirectly" formulation for proceeds of crime. Because this entire Chapter is understood to be a comparatively recent addition to the CrPC framework rather than part of its original core, practitioners should treat the whole Chapter, not merely its renumbering, as an area demanding careful independent verification.

:::: instinct A country has an active law-enforcement cooperation practice with India, built entirely on informal police-to-police contacts, with no treaty and no Central Government notification of any arrangement. Is it a contracting State under section 111(a)?

::: ruling This is doubtful on the text. Section 111(a) requires "arrangements have been made by the Central Government with the Government of such country through a treaty or otherwise" -- informal police-to-police cooperation, without any arrangement made at the Central Government level, does not obviously meet that threshold, however effective the cooperation may be in practice.
:::
::::

:::: instinct Property originally stolen is sold, and the sale proceeds are used to purchase shares in a company. The accused argues the shares are not "proceeds of crime" because they were never themselves stolen. Is that argument sound under section 111(c)?

::: ruling No. Section 111(c) defines proceeds of crime as property derived or obtained "directly or indirectly" as a result of criminal activity, and section 111(d)'s definition of property expressly includes property "obtained through proceeds of crime." The shares, purchased with the sale proceeds of stolen property, fall within both definitions.

#### Sword and shield

As a sword, the breadth of "proceeds of crime" and "property" under section 111 lets the prosecution or investigating agency pursue an asset through several conversions or transfers without having to establish an unbroken physical chain back to the original stolen item. As a shield, a respondent facing an attachment or forfeiture claim should test whether the Central Government arrangement relied on for a "contracting State" claim actually exists and covers the relevant country, since the entire cross-border half of this Chapter collapses without it.

#### The limitation clock

Section 111 fixes no limitation period of its own; it is a definitions section that governs the scope, not the timing, of the provisions that follow.

#### Interlocking matrix

Every subsequent section in this Chapter depends on section 111's definitions — sections 112 to 114 depend on "contracting State," sections 115 to 122 depend on "property" and "proceeds of crime," and sections 116's "tracing" and "identifying" verbs frame the police officer's mandate under that section.

#### Strategy and drafting

Any application invoking this Chapter's cross-border machinery should affirmatively plead the existence and scope of the Central Government's arrangement with the relevant country — a bare assertion that a country is a "contracting State" invites a straightforward challenge if the arrangement is not identified. Any application for attachment or forfeiture should track the property through its conversions explicitly, showing the indirect-derivation chain required by section 111(c) and (d), rather than resting on an assumption that a later-acquired asset is self-evidently proceeds of crime.

## Sections 112–113 — Sending a letter of request abroad for evidence, and executing one received from abroad

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute When evidence needed for an Indian investigation sits outside India, section 112 gives an Indian Criminal Court a formal channel to reach it: on an application by the investigating officer or a superior officer, the Court may issue a letter of request to a competent foreign court or authority, asking it to examine a witness, record a statement, or require production of a document or thing, and to forward everything collected back to the issuing Court. Whatever comes back is deemed to be evidence collected during the investigation itself — no separate authentication battle needed to bring it into the case. Section 113 is the mirror-image inbound process: when a foreign court or authority sends India a letter of request for examination of a person or production of material in connection with an offence under investigation abroad, the Central Government may, if it thinks fit, either route it to a Chief Judicial Magistrate or Judicial Magistrate to summon the person and record the statement, or send it to a police officer to investigate as if the offence had been committed in India — with everything collected forwarded back through the Central Government to the requesting authority.
:::

::: story Evidence trapped abroad, and a reciprocal request landing in India
An investigating officer needs to examine a witness residing abroad and obtain documents in that witness's possession. On the officer's application, an Indian Criminal Court issues a letter of request under section 112(1) to the competent court in that country, asking it to examine the witness, record the statement, and forward the documents and the recorded statement back to the issuing Court — once received, that material is deemed evidence collected in the investigation under section 112(3), without any further step needed to treat it as such. In a separate matter, a foreign authority investigating an offence committed in its own territory sends India a letter of request under section 113(1) seeking examination of a person located in India. The Central Government, thinking it fit, forwards the request to a Judicial Magistrate, who summons the person, records the statement, and the Magistrate forwards the recorded statement back to the Central Government for transmission to the requesting foreign authority.
:::

### In plain English

These two sections are the workhorse machinery for genuinely cross-border evidence gathering — the everyday version of mutual legal assistance, well short of extradition or arrest. Section 112 keeps things simple for the Indian side: apply, get the letter of request issued, and whatever comes back slots straight into the investigation as evidence, no separate battle over its status. Section 113 is India playing the receiving end of somebody else's investigation, and the Central Government gets a genuine choice about how much machinery to bring to bear — a light-touch Magistrate-led examination for a narrow request, or a full police investigation treating the foreign offence as if it happened on Indian soil, for something that needs deeper digging.

### What changed from the CrPC

::: info Verify before citing

:::

Both sections read as carrying over the presumed CrPC Chapter VII-A letter-of-request scheme largely intact, including the deeming provision in section 112(3) and the Central Government's dual-route discretion in section 113(1). As with the rest of this Chapter, the section numbers are the renumbering most in need of independent confirmation.

:::: instinct An investigating officer, without seeking the involvement of any Criminal Court, directly writes to a foreign police agency asking it to examine a witness and send back a statement. ::: ruling No. Section 112 requires a letter of request issued by a Criminal Court, following an application by the investigating officer or a superior officer -- it is the Court's letter of request, not an informal direct communication from the investigating officer, that triggers the deeming provision in section 112(3). A statement obtained through an informal channel outside this structure does not automatically become deemed evidence under this section.
:::

Is that approach correct?

::: ruling The section directs that the officer "investigate into the offence in the same manner ... as if the offence had been committed within India" -- this is a direction about the manner of investigation, not an assertion that the underlying conduct must independently qualify as an offence under Indian substantive law. Whether the absence of dual criminality affects the request at a policy or treaty level is a separate question from how section 113 itself frames the investigating officer's procedural mandate, and should be checked against the terms of the underlying arrangement with the contracting State concerned.

#### Strategy and drafting

An application under section 112(1) should identify with reasonable precision what evidence is believed to be available abroad and why — a vague, speculative application invites delay or refusal at the letter-of-request stage itself. ## Section 114 — Warrants and summonses that reach into, or arrive from, a contracting State

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Section 114 extends the reciprocal machinery from mere evidence-gathering to compulsory process itself — warrants for arrest, and summonses or warrants requiring attendance or production of a document or thing. Where an Indian Court wants such a warrant executed in a contracting State, it sends it in duplicate, in the prescribed form, through the Central Government's designated channel, to the appropriate foreign court, judge, or Magistrate, who causes it to be executed. A closely related but distinct power lets a Court, on application by an investigating officer or a superior officer, issue a summons or warrant to secure the attendance in India of a person located in a contracting State, again transmitted in duplicate through the prescribed channel. The reverse direction works the same way: a warrant received from a contracting State is executed in India exactly as if it had come from another Indian Court within its own local limits. The section also addresses the position of prisoners transferred under this process — conditions can be imposed by the Indian Court or Central Government on a prisoner transferred out of India, and the Indian Court must ensure that conditions attached to a prisoner transferred into India are complied with, keeping that prisoner in custody subject to whatever conditions the Central Government directs.
:::

::: story Securing a witness's attendance from abroad, and executing an inbound arrest warrant
An investigating officer applies to an Indian Court for the attendance of a person located in a contracting State, satisfying the Court that the person's attendance is genuinely required for the investigation. The Court issues a summons in duplicate, in the prescribed form, transmitted through the Central Government's designated channel to the appropriate authority in that State. In a separate matter, a Court, Judge, or Magistrate in a contracting State issues a warrant for the arrest of a person believed to be in India, requiring that person to attend or produce a document before that foreign Court. On receipt, the Indian Court executes the warrant exactly as if it were a warrant received from another Court within India, applying the ordinary domestic arrest-warrant procedure.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 114 — Assistance in securing transfer of persons
"114. (1) Where a Court in India, in relation to a criminal matter, desires that a warrant for arrest of any person to attend or produce a document or other thing issued by it [[!shall]] be executed in any place in a contracting State, it [[!shall]] send such warrant in duplicate in such form to such Court, Judge or Magistrate through such authority, as the Central Government may, by notification, specify in this behalf and that Court, Judge or Magistrate, as the case may be, [[!shall]] cause the same to be executed.
(2) If, in the course of an investigation or any inquiry into an offence, an application is made by the investigating officer or any officer superior in rank to the investigating officer that the attendance of a person who is in any place in a contracting State is required in connection with such investigation or inquiry and the Court is satisfied that such attendance is so required, it [[!shall]] issue a summons or warrant, in duplicate, against the said person to such Court, Judge or Magistrate, in such form as the Central Government may, by notification, specify in this behalf, to cause the same to be served or executed.
(3) Where a Court in India, in relation to a criminal matter, has received a warrant for arrest of any person requiring him to attend or attend and produce a document or other thing in that Court or before any other investigating agency, issued by a Court, Judge or Magistrate in a contracting State, the same [[!shall]] be executed as if it is the warrant received by it from another Court in India for execution within its local limits.
(4) Where a person transferred to a contracting State pursuant to sub-section (3) is a prisoner in India, the Court in India or the Central Government [[?may]] impose such conditions as that Court or Government deems fit.
(5) Where the person transferred to India pursuant to sub-section (1) or sub-section (2) is a prisoner in a contracting State, the Court in India [[!shall]] ensure that the conditions subject to which the prisoner is transferred to India are complied with and such prisoner [[!shall]] be kept in such custody subject to such conditions as the Central Government may direct in writing."
:::

### In plain English

If section 112 and 113 are about getting information across a border, section 114 is about getting people, or compulsory process against people, across one. It is built with the same duplicate-and-prescribed-channel discipline running through the whole Chapter, so the paperwork side of cross-border enforcement is standardised. The prisoner provisions in sub-sections (4) and (5) exist because transferring someone who is already in custody raises a distinct problem — conditions have to travel with the person, and the receiving side (India, under sub-section (5)) is made responsible for actually keeping to those conditions, not just formally acknowledging them.

## Sections 115–116 — The Court's power to order attachment or forfeiture of offence-derived property, and the police officer tasked with tracing it

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Sections 115 and 116 open the second half of this Chapter — the domestic and cross-border attachment-and-forfeiture regime for property linked to crime. Section 115(1) gives an Indian Court, on reasonable grounds to believe that property was derived or obtained, directly or indirectly, from an offence, the power to order its attachment or forfeiture under the detailed procedure in sections 116 to 122. Where such property is suspected to be in a contracting State, the Court can issue a letter of request to a foreign court or authority to execute that order abroad — and the traffic runs the other way too: where a foreign contracting State sends India a letter of request to attach or forfeit property here that is linked to an offence committed there, the Central Government may forward it to an Indian Court for execution under the same sections 116-to-122 procedure, or under any other applicable law. Section 116 supplies the investigative muscle behind all of this — once the process is triggered, the Court directs a police officer not below the rank of Sub-Inspector to trace and identify the property, through whatever inquiry, investigation, or survey is needed into persons, places, assets, documents, or bank and financial institution records, carried out under the Court's own directions.
:::

::: story A domestic forfeiture order reaching property abroad, and tracing at the ground level
An Indian Court, satisfied on reasonable grounds that property was obtained indirectly from an offence, makes an order of attachment under section 115(1), invoking the procedure in sections 116 to 122. Learning that part of the property is held in a contracting State, the Court issues a letter of request under section 115(2) to the competent authority there for execution of the attachment. Separately, to build the evidentiary foundation for that order in the first place, the Court had earlier directed a Sub-Inspector under section 116(1) to trace and identify the property — the officer conducts inquiries into bank records and property registries, and a survey of relevant premises, all carried out strictly in accordance with the Court's own directions under section 116(3).
:::

## Section 117 — The interim seizure or attachment order, and its thirty-day confirmation deadline

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Tracing property takes time, and property can disappear in the meantime. Section 117 closes that gap: where an officer conducting a tracing inquiry or investigation under section 116 has reason to believe the property is likely to be concealed, transferred, or otherwise disposed of, that officer may order it seized — or, where seizure is not practicable, order its attachment, meaning it cannot be transferred or dealt with except with the officer's own prior permission, with a copy of the order served on the person concerned. This is a fast, officer-level power, but it is deliberately not self-sustaining: any such order has no effect at all unless it is confirmed by the Court within thirty days of being made. Miss that window, and the interim order simply lapses.
:::

::: story A seizure that beats a disposal attempt, and one that lapses through delay
An officer tracing property under section 116 learns that the property owner is actively negotiating its sale to a third party. Acting under section 117(1), the officer immediately orders the property seized, heading off the disposal before it can be completed, and ensures the order is placed before the Court for confirmation well within the thirty-day window fixed by section 117(2). In a separate matter, an officer makes an attachment order under section 117(1) for a different property but, through administrative delay, the order is not placed before the Court for confirmation until the thirty-fifth day. Because section 117(2) makes an unconfirmed order ineffective after thirty days, the attachment has already lapsed by the time confirmation is sought, and the property is no longer restrained under that order.
:::

## Section 118 — The Administrator who manages seized or forfeited property

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Property does not manage itself once it is seized or attached, and section 118 supplies the office that does the managing. The Court may appoint the District Magistrate of the area where the property is situated, or any other officer the District Magistrate nominates, to act as Administrator. The Administrator's job is to receive and manage property covered by a seizure-or-attachment order under section 117(1), or a later order under section 120, in whatever manner and subject to whatever conditions the Central Government specifies — and, once property is actually forfeited to the Central Government, to take whatever measures the Central Government directs to dispose of it.
:::

::: story Keeping a seized business running, and disposing of what is finally forfeited
Property seized under section 117 includes a functioning commercial enterprise that needs ongoing management to preserve its value pending the outcome of proceedings. The Court appoints the District Magistrate of the area as Administrator under section 118(1), who receives and manages the enterprise in the manner and subject to the conditions the Central Government has specified. Once, at a later stage, part of that same property is found to be proceeds of crime and stands forfeited to the Central Government under section 120, the same Administrator, now acting under section 118(3), takes whatever measures the Central Government directs to actually dispose of the forfeited property.
:::

## Sections 119–121 — The show-cause notice, the forfeiture finding, and the fine offered in lieu of losing the property outright

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute This is the due-process core of the forfeiture regime. Once the tracing inquiry under section 116 gives the Court reason to believe property is proceeds of crime, section 119 requires a show-cause notice to the affected person, giving thirty days to explain the source of the property and the evidence relied on, and to show why it should not be declared proceeds of crime and forfeited — with a copy also served on anyone else shown as holding the property on the affected person's behalf. Section 120 is where the Court actually decides: after considering the explanation and the material available, and after giving a genuine hearing opportunity, the Court records a finding on whether the property is proceeds of crime — proceeding ex parte if the person does not appear within the thirty days — and where only some of several properties can be specifically identified as tainted, the Court may still make a best-judgment finding as to which ones. A confirmed finding of proceeds of crime means automatic forfeiture to the Central Government, free of all encumbrances, with company shares specifically required to be re-registered in the Central Government's name regardless of the Companies Act or the company's own Articles. Section 121 softens the outcome only at the margins: where just part of the property's source has not been proved to the Court's satisfaction, the Court must offer the affected person the option of paying a fine equal to that part's market value instead of losing it, after a hearing, and paying that fine within the allowed time gets the forfeiture declaration revoked and the property released.
:::

::: story A notice answered, and a notice ignored
The Court, having reason to believe certain property is proceeds of crime, issues a show-cause notice under section 119(1), giving the affected person thirty days to explain the source of the funds and to show cause against forfeiture. The person responds within time, providing documentation of a legitimate source for most of the property but no satisfactory explanation for one component. Applying section 121, the Court offers the person the option of paying a fine equal to that component's market value instead of forfeiting it outright. In a separate matter, a different affected person, properly served with a similar notice, simply does not appear or respond within the thirty days. The Court proceeds ex parte under section 120(1)'s proviso, records a finding based on the evidence available before it, and the property in question stands forfeited to the Central Government free from all encumbrances under section 120(3).
:::

### In plain English

This is where the forfeiture regime's fairness is actually tested. The thirty-day show-cause notice under section 119 is the affected person's real chance to explain a legitimate source before anything is taken — and the requirement to also notify anyone else holding property on that person's behalf means the regime does not quietly bypass people with a genuine stake just because the notice was addressed to someone else. Section 120's ex parte safety valve keeps the process from being permanently stalled by a person who simply will not engage, but the "reasonable opportunity of being heard" language is doing real work for everyone who does show up. The automatic, encumbrance-free forfeiture in section 120(3), and the blunt override of company law in section 120(4), show how completely a confirmed finding transfers the property — which is exactly why section 121's fine-in-lieu option matters: it is the one place in this sequence where a partial failure of proof does not have to mean total loss, provided the person can pay the property's market value instead.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct A show-cause notice under section 119(1) is served on the affected person but does not specify any particular thirty-day deadline, simply stating that a response is required "promptly." The person responds after twenty-five days. Can the Court still proceed ex parte on the basis that the response was not prompt enough?

::: ruling This is doubtful. Section 119(1) requires the notice to call upon the person "within a period of thirty days specified in the notice" -- a notice that does not specify the thirty-day period as required by the section arguably has not properly triggered the show-cause mechanism at all, and treating a twenty-five-day response as late under an unspecified "promptly" standard sits uneasily with the sections express thirty-day framework.
:::
::::

:::: instinct The Court finds that some but not all of several properties named in a show-cause notice are proceeds of crime, but cannot say with precision which specific ones, given how the funds were commingled. Can the Court still record a forfeiture finding?

::: ruling Yes. Section 120(2) expressly addresses exactly this situation -- where the Court is satisfied some of the properties are proceeds of crime but cannot specifically identify which, it may specify, to the best of its judgment, which properties are proceeds of crime and record a finding accordingly. Precision to the point of certainty is not required once the Court has reached that best-judgment stage.

#### Sword and shield

As a sword, the ex parte route in section 120(1)'s proviso, and the best-judgment power in section 120(2), together mean the forfeiture regime cannot be stalled indefinitely by an affected person's silence or by the practical difficulty of tracing commingled funds to the last rupee. As a shield, section 121's fine-in-lieu option is a powerful, underused argument for the affected person — wherever only part of a property's source is genuinely unproved, the Court is required, not merely permitted, to offer the market-value-fine alternative, and failing to press for that offer where the facts support it leaves a real remedy on the table.

#### The limitation clock

Thirty days from the date specified in the show-cause notice under section 119(1) for the affected person to respond and show cause — the same thirty-day period functions as both the deadline for a substantive response and the trigger for the ex parte route under section 120(1)'s proviso if missed. Section 121(3)'s fine must be paid "within such time as may be allowed in that behalf" by the Court, a separate, case-specific deadline.

#### Interlocking matrix

Section 119 interlocks with section 116, since the show-cause notice is triggered by the Court's reason to believe arising from that tracing inquiry. Section 120(4)'s override of the Companies Act, 2013 interlocks directly with that Act's own share-transfer and registration provisions, which practitioners handling corporate assets should check to understand the practical mechanics of compliance. Section 121 interlocks with section 120(3), since the fine-in-lieu option only arises once a forfeiture declaration under section 120 already exists.

#### Strategy and drafting

A response to a section 119 show-cause notice should be documentary and specific — naming the exact source of income or assets and attaching the evidence relied on — since section 120(1) directs the Court to consider "the explanation, if any," making the quality of that explanation the single most consequential input into the eventual finding. Where a client can prove the source of most, but not all, of the notified property, counsel should affirmatively invoke section 121 rather than waiting for the Court to raise it, since the section frames the fine-in-lieu offer as something the Court "shall" make once the partial-proof condition is met.

## Sections 122–124 — Transfers made to dodge forfeiture are void, and the Central Government's channel and notification powers

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute The Chapter closes with three provisions that protect and operationalise everything before them. Section 122 is the anti-evasion rule: once a seizure-or-attachment order under section 117(1) has been made, or a show-cause notice under section 119 has been issued, any transfer of the property covered by that order or notice is simply ignored for the purposes of these proceedings — and if the property is later actually forfeited under section 120, that intervening transfer is deemed null and void outright, not merely disregarded. Section 123 is the administrative channel provision: every letter of request, summons, or warrant flowing between India and a contracting State under this Chapter, in either direction, is transmitted in whatever form and manner the Central Government specifies by notification. Section 124 gives the Central Government a final calibration tool — by notification in the Official Gazette, it may direct that this Chapter's application to a particular contracting State with which reciprocal arrangements exist is subject to whatever conditions, exceptions, or qualifications the notification specifies, letting the relationship with each country be tailored rather than forced into one uniform mould.
:::

::: story A last-minute sale that changes nothing, and a country-specific carve-out
After a show-cause notice under section 119 has already been issued naming certain property, the affected person hurriedly sells it to a relative, hoping to place it beyond the Court's reach. Section 122 ignores that transfer for the purposes of the pending proceedings entirely, and when the property is ultimately found to be proceeds of crime and forfeited under section 120, the sale to the relative is deemed null and void, leaving the Central Government's title unaffected by the attempted evasion. Separately, the Central Government, having reciprocal arrangements with a particular country, issues a notification under section 124 directing that this Chapter's application to that country is subject to a specified qualification — for instance, limiting the categories of offence for which mutual assistance under the Chapter will be extended to or accepted from that country.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 122 — Certain transfers to be null and void
"122. Where after the making of an order under sub-section (1) of section 117 or the issue of a notice under section 119, any property referred to in the said order or notice is transferred by any mode whatsoever such transfers [[!shall]], for the purposes of the proceedings under this Chapter, be ignored and if such property is subsequently forfeited to the Central Government under section 120, then, the transfer of such property [[!shall]] be deemed to be null and void."
:::

::: proviso Section 123 — Manner in which letters of request, summons or warrant to be transmitted
"123. Every letter of request, summons or warrant, received by the Central Government from, and every letter of request, summons or warrant, to be transmitted to a contracting State under this Chapter [[!shall]] be transmitted to a contracting State or, as the case may be, sent to the concerned Court in India in such form and in such manner as the Central Government may, by notification, specify in this behalf."
:::

::: proviso Section 124 — Chapter not in derogation of any other law
"124. The Central Government [[?may]], by notification in the Official Gazette, direct that the application of this Chapter in relation to a contracting State with which reciprocal arrangements have been made, [[!shall]] be subject to such conditions, exceptions or qualifications as are specified in the said notification."

### In plain English

These closing sections make sure the regime built by sections 115 to 121 cannot simply be defeated by paperwork. Section 122 removes any incentive for a quick sale once proceedings are already in motion — the transfer just does not count, and if forfeiture eventually happens, it is treated as if the transfer never occurred at all. Section 123 is pure administrative plumbing, but it matters practically, since a letter of request or warrant sent in the wrong form or through the wrong channel risks simply not being recognised by the receiving authority. Section 124 is the release valve that keeps the whole reciprocal-arrangements idea workable in practice — not every country's legal system or diplomatic relationship with India is identical, and this section lets the Central Government adjust the Chapter's operation country by country without needing fresh legislation each time.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct Property is transferred to a bona fide purchaser for full market value, without any knowledge of a pending section 119 show-cause notice covering it, before the property is ultimately forfeited under section 120. Does the purchaser's good faith protect the transfer from section 122?

::: ruling This is doubtful on the text. Section 122 states that a transfer made after the section 117(1) order or the section 119 notice "shall ... be ignored" and, on forfeiture, "shall be deemed to be null and void" -- the section draws no express distinction based on the transferee's knowledge or good faith, so a purchaser's lack of awareness of the pending notice does not obviously take the transfer outside the sections reach.
:::

:::: instinct A letter of request is sent to a contracting State using a different form than the one specified in the Central Government's notification under section 123, but the foreign authority processes it without objection. Does the deviation from the notified form affect the letter's validity under section 123?

::: ruling Section 123 requires transmission "in such form and in such manner as the Central Government may, by notification, specify" -- on the text, this is a compliance requirement placed on the transmitting side, and the fact that the receiving foreign authority chose to process a non-conforming request does not, by itself, establish that section 123 was actually complied with. Practitioners should still confirm the notified form was used, rather than relying on the foreign authority's willingness to overlook a deviation.

#### Sword and shield

As a sword, section 122 gives the prosecution or investigating agency a clean, text-based answer to any argument that a transfer executed after proceedings began should be respected — the transfer simply does not count once the property is forfeited. As a shield, a party facing a section 122 challenge should look for a transfer that genuinely predates both the section 117(1) order and the section 119 notice, since the section's reach is expressly tied to those two triggering events and does not extend to earlier, unconnected transfers.

#### The limitation clock

None of these three sections fixes a limitation period of its own; section 122's application is triggered by the timing of the transfer relative to the section 117(1) order or section 119 notice, not by any independent clock.

#### Interlocking matrix

Section 122 interlocks directly with sections 117(1), 119, and 120, since its operation depends on the timing of those three events relative to the challenged transfer. Section 123 interlocks with sections 112, 113, 114, and 115(2)-(3), the provisions that generate the letters of request, summonses, and warrants section 123 governs the transmission of. Section 124 interlocks with section 111(a)'s definition of "contracting State," since a country-specific notification under section 124 presupposes reciprocal arrangements already exist with that country.

#### Strategy and drafting

Before advising a client on the safety of acquiring property that may be connected to a pending or anticipated investigation, check specifically whether a section 117(1) order or a section 119 notice already exists or is imminent — section 122's harsh, no-good-faith-exception treatment makes this due diligence step considerably more important than it would be for an ordinary property transaction. When challenging or relying on cross-border process under this Chapter, always locate and cite the specific Central Government notification under section 123 (governing form and manner) and, where relevant, under section 124 (governing country-specific conditions) — the bare text of sections 111 to 122 does not itself supply those operational details.
