# Chapter XXXIX — Miscellaneous

## Sections 520–521 — How a High Court tries a case itself, and how a person subject to military law gets routed to a Court-martial

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute This chapter gathers together the provisions that do not belong anywhere else in the Sanhita but that every practitioner eventually needs — procedural odds and ends, rule-making powers, disqualification rules, and the repeal-and-savings clause that closes the whole Sanhita out. This first cluster covers two of them. Section 520 answers a narrow but important question: when a High Court tries an offence itself otherwise than by using its section 447 transfer-to-itself power, what procedure does it follow? The answer is that it observes the same procedure a Court of Session would observe trying the same case — the High Court does not get some special, unwritten procedure of its own. Section 521 addresses the interface between ordinary criminal courts and the military justice system: the Central Government may make rules, consistent with the Sanhita and the Armed Forces enactments, fixing which persons subject to army, naval or air-force law are to be tried by an ordinary Sanhita court versus a Court-martial, and where a Magistrate has a person before him who could be tried either way, the Magistrate must have regard to those rules and, in the proper case, hand the person over to the relevant commanding officer instead of proceeding himself.
:::

::: story A serving officer charged with an offence triable either way
A person subject to the Army Act, 1950 is brought before a Magistrate on a charge for which he could be tried either by an ordinary criminal court under the Sanhita or by a Court-martial. Central Government rules made under section 521(1) specify which route applies to a case of this kind. The Magistrate must have regard to those rules, and section 521(1) requires that, in the proper case, the Magistrate deliver the accused — together with a statement of the offence — to the commanding officer of the unit to which he belongs, or to the commanding officer of the nearest army, naval or air-force station, so that a Court-martial can try him instead. Separately, where a commanding officer makes a written application for help apprehending a person accused of such an offence, section 521(2) requires every Magistrate receiving that application to use his utmost endeavours to apprehend and secure that person — the ordinary criminal justice machinery is expected to actively assist the military justice system, not merely defer to it on paper.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 520 — Trials before High Courts
"520. When an offence is tried by the High Court otherwise than under section 447, it [[!shall]], in the trial of the offence, observe the same procedure as a Court of Sessions would observe if it were trying the case."
:::

::: proviso Section 521 — Delivery to commanding officers of persons liable to be tried by Court-martial
"521. (1) The Central Government [[?may]] make rules consistent with this Sanhita and the Air Force Act, 1950, the Army Act, 1950, the Navy Act, 1957, and any other law, relating to the Armed Forces of the Union, for the time being in force, as to cases in which persons subject to army, naval or air-force law, or such other law, [[!shall]] be tried by a Court to which this Sanhita applies, or by a Court-martial; and when any person is brought before a Magistrate and charged with an offence for which he is liable to be tried either by a Court to which this Sanhita applies or by a Court-martial, such Magistrate [[!shall]] have regard to such rules, and [[!shall]] in proper cases deliver him, together with a statement of the offence of which he is accused, to the commanding officer of the unit to which he belongs, or to the commanding officer of the nearest army, naval or air-force station, as the case may be, for the purpose of being tried by a Court-martial.
Explanation.—In this section—
(a) 'unit' includes a regiment, corps, ship, detachment, group, battalion or company;
(b) 'Court-martial' includes any Tribunal with the powers similar to those of a Court-martial constituted under the relevant law applicable to the Armed Forces of the Union.
(2) Every Magistrate [[!shall]], on receiving a written application for that purpose by the commanding officer of any unit or body of soldiers, sailors or airmen stationed or employed at any such place, use his utmost endeavours to apprehend and secure any person accused of such offence.
(3) A High Court [[?may]], if it thinks fit, direct that a prisoner detained in any jail situate within the State be brought before a Court-martial for trial or to be examined touching any matter pending before the Court-martial."
:::

### In plain English

Section 520 is a short, easily overlooked provision with an important practical consequence: nothing about a High Court trying a case makes the trial procedurally different from an ordinary Sessions trial — the same rules of evidence, examination and record-keeping apply, just at a different forum. Section 521 is about a genuinely separate justice system running in parallel with the ordinary criminal courts — the military justice system — and it builds the bridge between the two. Central Government rules decide, as a matter of policy, which categories of military-law offences go to ordinary courts and which go to Court-martial; the Magistrate's job is not to make that policy call himself but to apply the rules that already exist, and where they say the person should go to a Court-martial, to actually hand him over with a statement of the offence rather than simply proceeding with an ordinary trial. The duty to help apprehend a person on a commanding officer's request is a separate, standalone obligation — cooperation with the military justice system runs both ways.

#### Sword and shield

Section 520 rarely generates its own litigation — it is mostly relevant as a backdrop assumption when challenging some other aspect of a High Court trial's procedure, since it forecloses any argument that the High Court was free to invent its own procedure. Section 521 is more often a shield for the defence in a military-law case — where the applicable Central Government rules point to Court-martial jurisdiction, an ordinary-court trial that proceeded without regard to those rules is vulnerable to challenge on that ground.

#### The limitation clock

Neither section fixes an independent limitation period.

#### Interlocking matrix

Section 521 interlocks with the Air Force Act, 1950, the Army Act, 1950, and the Navy Act, 1957, and with whatever Central Government rules have actually been made under section 521(1) — a section 521 argument is incomplete without identifying the specific applicable rule, not just the general existence of the rule-making power.

## Sections 522–523 — The forms every court can rely on, and the High Court's power to license petition-writers

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Two administrative-machinery provisions. Section 522 addresses the Second Schedule's prescribed forms — subject to the High Court's own rule-making power under article 227 of the Constitution, those forms, with whatever variations the circumstances of a particular case require, may be used for the purposes they are meant for, and if used, are sufficient for that purpose. This is a practical safe-harbour: using the Schedule form correctly is not something a court can second-guess as inadequate. Section 523 gives every High Court, with the State Government's previous approval, a rule-making power specifically over petition-writers — persons permitted to draft petitions for litigants in the criminal courts subordinate to that High Court. The rules can cover who may act as a petition-writer, how licences are issued, how their business and fees are regulated, and what penalty applies for breaking the rules, plus a residual catch-all for anything the State Government's own rules require or permit to be covered. All such rules must be published in the Official Gazette.
:::

::: story A form used with minor case-specific variations
A party uses a Second Schedule form for a particular application, adjusting a few details to fit the specific facts of the case, as the circumstances require. Section 522 confirms that using the form this way — varied as the case requires, but still recognisably the prescribed form — is sufficient for the purpose it is meant to serve, subject only to whatever the High Court has itself prescribed under its article 227 rule-making power. In a separate matter, a person who is not currently licensed under any High Court rule attempts to act as a petition-writer for litigants in a Magistrate's court. Section 523 makes clear that whether such a person may act in that capacity at all is a question governed by rules the relevant High Court has made, with the State Government's previous approval — including who may be permitted to act as a petition-writer, and what penalty attaches to acting without the required licence.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 522 — Forms
"522. Subject to the power conferred by article 227 of the Constitution, the forms set forth in the Second Schedule, with such variations as the circumstances of each case require, [[?may]] be used for the respective purposes therein mentioned, and if used [[!shall]] be sufficient."
:::

::: proviso Section 523 — Power of High Court to make rules
"523. (1) Every High Court [[?may]], with the previous approval of the State Government, make rules—
(a) as to the persons who may be permitted to act as petition-writers in the Criminal Courts subordinate to it;
(b) regulating the issue of licences to such persons, the conduct of business by them, and the scale of fees to be charged by them;
(c) providing a penalty for a contravention of any of the rules so made and determining the authority by which such contravention may be investigated and the penalties imposed;
(d) any other matter which is required to be, or may be, provided by rules made by the State Government.
(2) All rules made under this section [[!shall]] be published in the Official Gazette."
:::

### In plain English

Section 522 is a small but genuinely useful safe-harbour: if you use the prescribed Second Schedule form, adapted sensibly to your case's specific facts, nobody can later say the form itself was inadequate — the Sanhita has already decided it is sufficient. Section 523 is about who gets to help litigants draft their petitions in the criminal courts below a High Court, and it deliberately leaves the details to each High Court's own rule-making, subject to State Government approval and Gazette publication — so the practical rules on petition-writers are a matter of local practice you have to actually look up for the specific High Court in question, not something the Sanhita itself spells out.

## Sections 524–526 — Redesignating a magisterial function, and who may not try, hear, or preside over a case

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Three provisions about who is entitled to exercise which magisterial or judicial function. Section 524 lets a State — by Legislative Assembly resolution, State Government notification, and after consultation with the High Court — redirect references in sections 127, 128, 129, 164 and 166 to an Executive Magistrate so that they are instead construed as references to a Judicial Magistrate of the first class, effectively reallocating those specific functions within the State. Section 525 is a personal-interest disqualification rule: no Judge or Magistrate may, except with the permission of the court to which an appeal lies from his own court, try or commit for trial a case to which he is a party or in which he is personally interested, and no Judge or Magistrate may hear an appeal from his own judgment or order — with an Explanation carving out mere public-capacity involvement or a site inspection made in connection with the case from counting as disqualifying personal interest. Section 526 is a narrower conflict rule: an advocate who practices in a particular Magistrate's court may not sit as Magistrate in that same court, or in any court within that court's local jurisdiction.
:::

::: story A Magistrate who inspected the crime scene
A Magistrate who is trying a case had, earlier in the proceedings, personally visited the place where the offence is alleged to have occurred, in order to better understand the layout described in the evidence. The defence argues this site visit makes the Magistrate personally interested in the case, disqualifying him under section 525. The Explanation to section 525 answers this directly — a Judge or Magistrate is not deemed to be personally interested merely because he has viewed the place where the offence is alleged to have occurred, or another place material to the case, and made an inquiry in connection with it. The disqualification argument fails on these facts alone. In a separate matter, an advocate who regularly practices before a particular Magistrate's court is later appointed to sit as Magistrate — but in that very same court. Section 526 bars exactly this: a practising advocate in a given Magistrate's court cannot sit as Magistrate in that court, or in any court within its local jurisdiction, regardless of how competent or well-regarded the advocate may otherwise be.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 524 — Power to alter functions allocated to Executive Magistrate in certain cases
"524. If the Legislative Assembly of a State by a resolution so permits, the State Government [[?may]], after consultation with the High Court, by notification, direct that references in sections 127, 128, 129, 164 and 166 to an Executive Magistrate [[!shall]] be construed as references to a Judicial Magistrate of the first class."
:::

::: proviso Section 525 — Cases in which Judge or Magistrate is personally interested
"525. No Judge or Magistrate [[!shall]], except with the permission of the Court to which an appeal lies from his Court, try or commit for trial any case to or in which he is a party, or personally interested, and no Judge or Magistrate [[!shall]] hear an appeal from any judgment or order passed or made by himself.
Explanation.—A Judge or Magistrate [[!shall]] not be deemed to be a party to, or personally interested in, any case by reason only that he is concerned therein in a public capacity, or by reason only that he has viewed the place in which an offence is alleged to have been committed, or any other place in which any other transaction material to the case is alleged to have occurred, and made an inquiry in connection with the case."
:::

::: proviso Section 526 — Practising advocate not to sit as Magistrate in certain Courts
"526. No advocate who practices in the Court of any Magistrate [[!shall]] sit as a Magistrate in that Court or in any Court within the local jurisdiction of that Court."
:::

### In plain English

Section 524 is a State-level administrative lever, not something that changes automatically — unless a specific State has gone through the Legislative Assembly resolution, State Government notification, and High Court consultation process, the five listed sections continue to mean what they ordinarily say about Executive Magistrates. Section 525 protects the basic fairness expectation that nobody should be a judge in their own cause — a Judge or Magistrate with a real personal stake in a case cannot try it or hear an appeal from his own decision, though the Explanation is careful to draw the line at genuine personal interest, not at ordinary official involvement or an evidentiary site visit, which are routine parts of doing the job rather than signs of bias. Section 526 protects a narrower but equally important expectation — the professional relationships built up while practising as an advocate in a particular court should not follow that same person onto the bench in that same court, because the risk of at least the appearance of familiarity-driven bias is too high.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct A Magistrate is asked to hear an appeal from a judgment he himself passed in an earlier proceeding, and no permission from any appellate court has been sought. Can he hear the appeal?

::: ruling No. Section 525 expressly bars a Judge or Magistrate from hearing an appeal from any judgment or order passed or made by himself -- unlike the try-or-commit branch of the section, this particular bar is not qualified by any appellate-permission exception in the text, and the Magistrate cannot properly hear that appeal.
:::
::::

A posting to an unrelated court in a different district, with no jurisdictional overlap with his former practice, falls outside the specific scope of that bar.

#### Sword and shield

Section 525 is a sword available to a party seeking to have a case reassigned away from a Judge or Magistrate with a genuine personal stake, and correspondingly a shield for a Judge or Magistrate resisting a recusal demand grounded only in ordinary public-capacity involvement or a site visit. Section 526 is a sword for a party challenging the very composition of the court on structural-conflict grounds, independent of any showing of actual bias in the specific case.

#### The limitation clock

None of the three sections fixes an independent limitation period; objections under sections 525 or 526 should ordinarily be raised at the earliest practicable stage once the disqualifying fact is known, since a late-raised objection risks the same could-have-been-raised-earlier scrutiny seen elsewhere in this guide's discussion of procedural objections generally.

#### Interlocking matrix

Section 524 interlocks directly with sections 127, 128, 129, 164 and 166 — a party relying on a State's redesignation notification must confirm both that the notification exists and that it actually covers the specific function in question. Sections 525 and 526 interlock with the general law on bias and recusal, and with whatever specific appellate structure determines which court's permission under section 525 would need to be sought.

#### Strategy and drafting

When raising a section 524 point, plead the existence and date of the specific State notification, not merely the general possibility that a State might have made one. When raising a section 525 or section 526 objection, raise it the moment the disqualifying fact becomes known, and frame the objection around the precise textual category involved — genuine personal interest or party status under section 525, or the specific practising-advocate conflict under section 526 — rather than a generalised claim of unfairness, since each section's protection is defined by its own specific terms.

## Sections 527–528 — A public servant barred from buying what he sells, and the High Court's inherent powers preserved

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Two short but structurally important provisions. Section 527 is a narrow conflict-of-interest bar: a public servant who has any duty to perform in connection with the sale of property under the Sanhita may not himself purchase or bid for that property — the person running the sale cannot also be a bidder in it. Section 528 is one of the most consequential sentences in the entire Sanhita for practical litigation purposes: nothing in the Sanhita is to be deemed to limit or affect the inherent powers of the High Court to make orders necessary to give effect to any order under the Sanhita, to prevent abuse of the process of any court, or otherwise to secure the ends of justice. This is the High Court's residual, catch-all authority — a safety valve for situations the Sanhita's specific provisions do not anticipate or adequately address.
:::

::: story A public servant tempted to bid on his own sale
A public servant responsible for conducting the sale of attached property under the Sanhita wishes to purchase that same property himself, believing he can offer a fair price. Section 527 bars this outright — a public servant with any duty in connection with the sale may not purchase or bid for the property, regardless of how fair the offered price might be, because the conflict between running the sale and bidding in it is itself the problem the section eliminates. In a separate matter, a party finds itself in a procedural situation the Sanhita's specific provisions do not squarely address, but where the process of a subordinate court is plainly being abused. The party approaches the High Court, which invokes section 528 to make whatever order is necessary to prevent that abuse and secure the ends of justice — a power that exists precisely because no single specific provision could anticipate every situation genuinely requiring judicial intervention.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 527 — Public servant concerned in sale not to purchase or bid for property
"527. A public servant having any duty to perform in connection with the sale of any property under this Sanhita [[!shall]] not purchase or bid for the property."
:::

::: proviso Section 528 — Saving of inherent powers of High Court

### In plain English

Section 527 removes temptation at its source — rather than trying to police whether a public servant conducting a sale bid unfairly low or used inside knowledge, the Sanhita simply forbids that person from bidding at all, closing off the conflict entirely. Section 528 is the opposite kind of provision — instead of a narrow, specific rule, it is a broad acknowledgment that the High Court's inherent authority to do justice was never something the Sanhita's specific sections could fully capture, and it makes explicit that nothing in the Sanhita cuts that authority down. In practice, this means the High Court retains a genuine, if carefully bounded, power to step in and make things right where the specific rules run out — but that power is meant for genuine gaps, not as a shortcut around remedies the Sanhita already provides.

## Sections 529–531 — The High Court's duty of continuous oversight, electronic-mode proceedings, and the Sanhita's own repeal-and-savings clause

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute The chapter, and the Sanhita itself, closes with three provisions of very different character. Section 529 imposes an affirmative duty on every High Court to exercise its superintendence over the Courts of Session and Courts of Judicial Magistrates subordinate to it so as to ensure expeditious and proper disposal of cases — this is a duty of active oversight, not a passive power. Section 530 is a genuinely modern addition: all trials, inquiries and proceedings under the Sanhita — including issuance, service and execution of summons and warrants, examination of complainants and witnesses, recording of evidence, and appellate or other proceedings — may be held in electronic mode, by electronic communication or by audio-video electronic means. Section 531 is the Sanhita's own repeal-and-savings clause: it repeals the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 outright, but immediately qualifies that repeal with savings for pending matters (to be disposed of under the old Code as if the Sanhita had not commenced), for things already done under the old Code (deemed done under the corresponding Sanhita provisions), for sanctions and consents already given, and for periods of limitation that had already expired under the old Code before the Sanhita's commencement.
:::

::: story A trial conducted entirely through video-conferencing
A trial court decides to conduct the entire trial — issuance and service of summons, examination of witnesses, and recording of evidence — through audio-video electronic means, given the logistics of the case. Section 530 expressly authorises exactly this: all of the listed categories of proceeding, and indeed all trials, inquiries and proceedings under the Sanhita generally, may be held in electronic mode. In a separate matter, an appeal that was already pending under the old Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 on the date the Sanhita commenced continues after that date. Section 531(2)(a) answers what law governs it — the appeal is disposed of in accordance with the provisions of the old Code, as if the Sanhita had never come into force, notwithstanding that the Code has now been formally repealed by section 531(1).
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 529 — Duty of High Court to exercise continuous superintendence over Courts
"529. Every High Court [[!shall]] so exercise its superintendence over the Courts of Session and Courts of Judicial Magistrates subordinate to it as to ensure that there is an expeditious and proper disposal of cases by the Judges and Magistrates."
:::

::: proviso Section 530 — Trial and proceedings to be held in electronic mode
"530. All trials, inquires and proceedings under this Sanhita, including—
(i) issuance, service and execution of summons and warrant;
(ii) examination of complainant and witnesses;
(iii) recording of evidence in inquiries and trials; and
(iv) all appellate proceedings or any other proceeding,
[[?may]] be held in electronic mode, by use of electronic communication or use of audio-video electronic means."
:::

::: proviso Section 531 — Repeal and savings
"531. (1) The Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 is hereby repealed.
(2) Notwithstanding such repeal—
(a) if, immediately before the date on which this Sanhita comes into force, there is any appeal, application, trial, inquiry or investigation pending, then, such appeal, application, trial, inquiry or investigation [[!shall]] be disposed of, continued, held or made, as the case may be, in accordance with the provisions of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, as in force immediately before such commencement (hereinafter referred to as the said Code), as if this Sanhita had not come into force;
(b) all notifications published, proclamations issued, powers conferred, forms provided by rules, local jurisdictions defined, sentences passed and orders, rules and appointments, not being appointments as Special Magistrates, made under the said Code and which are in force immediately before the commencement of this Sanhita, [[!shall]] be deemed, respectively, to have been published, issued, conferred, specified, defined, passed or made under the corresponding provisions of this Sanhita;
(c) any sanction accorded or consent given under the said Code in pursuance of which no proceeding was commenced under that Code, [[!shall]] be deemed to have been accorded or given under the corresponding provisions of this Sanhita and proceedings [[?may]] be commenced under this Sanhita in pursuance of such sanction or consent.
(3) Where the period specified for an application or other proceeding under the said Code had expired on or before the commencement of this Sanhita, nothing in this Sanhita [[!shall]] be construed as enabling any such application to be made or proceeding to be commenced under this Sanhita by reason only of the fact that a longer period therefor is specified by this Sanhita or provisions are made in this Sanhita for the extension of time."

### In plain English

Section 529 is a reminder that a High Court's supervisory role over the courts below it is not optional or passive — it is an affirmative duty to actively manage for expeditious and proper case disposal, which matters practically whenever there is a pattern of delay in a particular subordinate court that a litigant wants to draw the High Court's attention to. Section 530 is the Sanhita's explicit embrace of remote and electronic proceedings across the board — not just for one narrow category of hearing, but for summons, witness examination, evidence recording, and appeals alike — reflecting how criminal justice practice has actually evolved. Section 531 is the mechanical hinge on which the entire transition from the old Code to the new Sanhita turns: the old Code is gone as a matter of law, but nothing that was already happening under it — a pending case, a thing already done, a sanction already given — simply vanishes or has to restart; it continues under the old rules as if nothing changed, except that a limitation period that had already run out under the old Code does not get revived by the Sanhita's own, possibly longer, timelines.

### What changed from the CrPC

::: info Verify before citing
BNSS section 529 is provisionally associated with the CrPC's high-court-superintendence-duty provision. Section 530 (electronic-mode trials and proceedings) appears to be a substantially new BNSS provision reflecting post-2020 developments in remote judicial proceedings, with no clear one-to-one CrPC predecessor, and should be treated as a genuine BNSS-original addition rather than a renumbered carry-forward pending independent confirmation. Section 531 is the Sanhita's own repeal-and-savings clause and, by its nature, has no CrPC predecessor at all — the CrPC's own equivalent clause repealed the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1898, not the 1973 Code. All section-number associations in this cluster must be independently verified before use in any filing.
:::

Section 529's superintendence duty and section 531's transitional-savings structure — preserving pending matters, deemed-continuity of past acts, and the anti-revival rule for already-expired limitation periods — both track well-established Indian legislative-transition drafting conventions. Section 530's explicit, comprehensive embrace of electronic-mode proceedings across summons, examination, evidence recording and appeals is the more genuinely novel provision in this cluster and is worth flagging to a CrPC-trained practitioner as new territory rather than assuming it simply restates settled CrPC-era practice on video-conferencing, which developed more piecemeal and largely through judicial orders rather than a single consolidated statutory provision.

#### Sword and shield

Section 529 is a sword a litigant can use to press the High Court into supervisory action against a pattern of delay in a subordinate court. Section 530 is largely a shield against objections to electronic-mode proceedings — a party challenging the validity of a video-conferenced hearing purely on the ground that it was conducted electronically has that challenge foreclosed by the section's express authorisation. Section 531 cuts both ways depending on which side benefits from old-Code continuity versus Sanhita governance in a given transitional matter — identifying which regime actually applies is itself the first and often decisive move.

#### The limitation clock

Section 531(3) is the direct limitation interaction in this cluster — it expressly bars any revival of a claim or proceeding whose old-Code limitation period had already expired before the Sanhita's commencement, regardless of any longer period or extension mechanism the Sanhita itself might otherwise offer.

#### Interlocking matrix

Section 531 interlocks with literally every provision of the Sanhita that has a CrPC predecessor, since it is the master switch determining whether the old Code or the new Sanhita governs any given pending or historical matter. Section 530 interlocks with the specific procedural provisions governing summons, witness examination, evidence recording and appeals throughout the rest of the Sanhita, since it is a mode-of-conduct provision layered on top of those substantive rules rather than a replacement for them.

#### Strategy and drafting

When a matter straddles the Sanhita's commencement date, resolve the section 531 threshold question first and explicitly — which regime governs — before making any substantive argument, since arguing the substance under the wrong regime's provisions can be fatal regardless of the argument's underlying merit. When objecting to or defending an electronic-mode proceeding, root the argument in the specific adequacy of the electronic means used in that case rather than a blanket objection to electronic proceedings as such, since section 530 has already foreclosed the latter. When pressing a section 529 supervisory complaint, document the specific pattern of delay concretely rather than raising a generalised grievance, since the duty is to ensure expeditious and proper disposal, which is best evidenced by specific, comparable case-management data.
