# Chapter I — Preliminary

## Sections 1–2 — What this Sanhita is called, where it applies, when it starts running, and the vocabulary that governs every page after this one

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Chapter I does two jobs before the Sanhita does anything substantive: section 1 fixes the Sanhita's name, its territorial reach (including a carve-out for Nagaland and the Sixth Schedule tribal areas, with three named Chapters switched on regardless), and its date of commencement by notification; section 2 fixes the vocabulary — 26 defined terms plus a cross-reference to the Information Technology Act, 2000 and the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 for anything not defined here — that every later Chapter assumes the reader already knows.
Nothing in Chapters II onward makes sense without this vocabulary. "Cognizable offence," "bailable offence," "warrant-case," "inquiry" versus "investigation," "police report" versus "complaint" — these pairs recur on almost every page that follows, and each pair marks a fork in the road: whether police can arrest without a warrant, whether a Magistrate can grant bail as of right, whether a trial proceeds on the fast summons-case track or the slower warrant-case track. Section 2 is where BNSS quietly made one of its most consequential structural changes too: unlike the CrPC, section 2 never separately defines a "metropolitan area" or "Metropolitan Magistrate" — because BNSS abolished that entire tier of courts. That single omission ripples through every later Chapter that used to route matters differently in a metropolitan area.
:::

::: story The FIR that hinged on one word in section 2
A complainant walks into a police station to report a theft. The investigating officer initially treats the matter as requiring a Magistrate's prior permission before registration, on the mistaken view that only a court-filed complaint can trigger action. But section 2(g)'s definition of "cognizable offence" — one for which a police officer "may, in accordance with the First Schedule or under any other law for the time being in force, arrest without warrant" — settles the question independently of how the information arrived. Theft is shown as cognizable in the First Schedule. The information itself, however informally received, is enough to trigger the police officer's independent power to investigate without needing anyone's permission first. The label attached to the paperwork does not change what the First Schedule already fixed.
:::

### In plain English

Section 1 is short and mostly mechanical: the Sanhita's name, the fact that it does not automatically extend to Nagaland or the Sixth Schedule tribal areas of Assam (except for the three Chapters — IX, XI and XII — that always apply, which happen to be the preventive-security, public-order, and police-preventive-action Chapters, precisely the ones a State would want available everywhere regardless of the general carve-out), and the fact that "when it starts" is left to a future notification rather than fixed in the text itself.

Section 2 is the dictionary the rest of the book assumes you already own. A few pairs matter more than the rest and are worth memorising on sight, because they recur constantly and each one is a fork in the road for how a case proceeds: cognizable versus non-cognizable (can the police arrest and investigate without asking anyone first), bailable versus non-bailable (is bail a right or a discretion), summons-case versus warrant-case (which trial procedure applies, keyed purely to the maximum punishment), inquiry versus investigation (is a Magistrate or Court doing the fact-finding, or is a police officer), and complaint versus police report (did this proceeding start with someone approaching a Magistrate directly, or with police forwarding the fruits of an investigation). The definition of "victim" is also worth flagging early: it is not limited to the injured person, but expressly reaches forward to that person's guardian or legal heir, which matters for who can seek compensation, file an appeal against acquittal, or be heard at various later stages.

### What changed from the CrPC

::: info Verify before citing
BNSS section 2 corresponds, section-for-section, to CrPC section 1973's section 2 — both are the "Definitions" provision opening Chapter I of their respective Codes. The specific additions and omissions below are believed accurate based on a direct reading of the two lists, but should be independently cross-checked against the CrPC text before being relied upon in drafting or argument, particularly given how much practitioner habit still runs on CrPC vocabulary.
:::

Two changes stand out. First, BNSS adds definitions the CrPC never had: "audio-video electronic means" and "electronic communication" are wholly new, reflecting the Sanhita's general push toward electronic recording, video-conferenced proceedings, and electronic service — vocabulary the 1973 Code simply had no occasion to define. BNSS also now defines "bail" and "bail bond" as freestanding terms; the CrPC defined "bailable offence" but left "bail" itself undefined, a gap that generated real interpretive debate over the decades that section 2(b) now closes by codifying release-from-custody-on-conditions as the operative concept.

Second, and more consequential for practitioners trained on the CrPC: BNSS section 2 contains no definition of "metropolitan area" and no definition of "Metropolitan Magistrate," terms the CrPC defined and used throughout. This is not an oversight — BNSS abolished the Metropolitan Magistrate tier of courts entirely, folding what used to be a separate metropolitan-area track into the ordinary Judicial Magistrate structure. A CrPC-trained advocate who reflexively reaches for "Metropolitan Magistrate" language in a city that used to have one needs to recalibrate: BNSS routes that same work through a Judicial Magistrate of the first class. The CrPC's separate definition of "pleader" also does not appear to carry forward as a defined term in section 2.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct The theft report that was called a "complaint" by mistake
A shopkeeper walks into a police station and orally reports a theft. The desk officer's paperwork, filled out carelessly, labels the report a "complaint." The accused's advocate later argues that because it was labelled a complaint rather than an FIR, only a Magistrate — not the police — had the power to act on it, and the police investigation should be treated as unauthorised. Is the advocate correct?

::: ruling
No. Section 2(h) defines "complaint" specifically as an allegation made to a Magistrate, with a view to the Magistrate taking action — and it expressly excludes a police report. An oral report of a cognizable offence made to a police officer is neither a complaint nor dependent on being correctly labelled; what matters is the substance captured by section 2(g)'s definition of "cognizable offence," which independently authorises the police to investigate without anyone's prior permission. A clerical mislabelling of the paperwork does not convert a police-station report into a Magistrate's complaint, and does not strip the police of the investigative power section 2(g) already gives them.
:::
::::

:::: instinct The victim who was not the person actually injured
A person is killed in an accident caused by a rash act. The deceased's elderly mother wants to be heard at the trial and seeks compensation. The accused argues she has no standing because she was not the person who suffered the "loss or injury" — her son was, and he is no longer alive to assert any right. Is the accused correct?

::: ruling
No. Section 2(y)'s definition of "victim" expressly includes "the guardian or legal heir of such victim," precisely to cover this situation. The deceased's mother, as a legal heir, steps into the definition of "victim" in her own right for the purposes of this Sanhita — she is not a secondary or derivative participant but falls squarely within the statutory definition, with whatever standing and rights the Sanhita's victim-related provisions elsewhere confer.
:::
::::

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

::: info Research gap — no case promoted
Section 2 is a definitions provision, and while individual defined terms (cognizable offence, victim, investigation versus inquiry) have each generated substantial case law under the identically-worded CrPC provision, no single Supreme Court authority interpreting BNSS section 2 itself is confidently recalled for promotion here. Any argument turning on a specific defined term should be researched against the case law on that specific term rather than against section 2 as a whole.
:::

#### Sword and shield

As a **sword**, correctly invoking the cognizable-offence definition under section 2(g) lets a complainant get police action moving immediately, without needing to first approach a Magistrate — informal information is enough if the underlying offence is shown as cognizable. As a **shield**, a defence advocate can challenge an entire chain of proceedings by showing that a threshold classification was wrong at the outset — for instance, that an offence treated as a warrant-case was, on the correct reading of the maximum sentence, actually a summons-case, entitling the accused to the simpler and faster procedure that should have applied from the start.

#### The limitation clock

Section 1 and section 2 do not themselves carry a limitation period — section 1(3) instead creates a different kind of clock: the entire Sanhita's operative date is suspended until the Central Government notifies a commencement date, and different provisions can in practice be brought into force on different notified dates.

#### Strategy and drafting

**Drafting traps.**

1. Do not assume a term undefined in section 2 is undefined altogether — section 2(2) pulls in the Information Technology Act, 2000 and the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 definitions for anything not defined here, so check both before assuming a gap.
2. Do not draft on CrPC-era assumptions about "metropolitan area" or "Metropolitan Magistrate" — those categories do not exist in BNSS, and pleadings referencing them risk looking like they were drafted for the wrong statute.
3. Do not conflate "inquiry" and "investigation" in drafting — section 2(k) and 2(l) draw a precise line (Magistrate-or-Court versus police-or-authorised-person) that later Chapters rely on to allocate very different powers.

**Timing.** Confirm the applicable commencement notification under section 1(3) before assuming any specific BNSS provision is in force for a given date — the Sanhita's provisions were brought into force in stages, and a proceeding governed by conduct predating the relevant commencement date may still fall to be assessed under the CrPC.

**Model checklist for a first read of any new matter:**

1. Confirm territorial applicability under section 1(2) if the matter arises in Nagaland or a Sixth Schedule tribal area.
2. Classify the offence as cognizable or non-cognizable, bailable or non-bailable, and warrant-case or summons-case, before advising on any procedural step.
3. Identify whether the record so far consists of a complaint, a police report, or something else, since this determines which later Chapter's procedure actually governs.
4. Check whether an undefined term is covered by the Information Technology Act, 2000 or the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 cross-reference in section 2(2) before assuming the Sanhita is silent on it.

## Sections 3–5 — Reading "Magistrate" correctly wherever it appears in other laws, which procedure governs which offences, and the safety net that protects special and local laws

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Sections 3 through 5 finish Chapter I's housekeeping. Section 3 tells every other law in the country how to read the bare word "Magistrate," or "Magistrate of the first class," or "Magistrate of the second class," when that other law uses those words without further qualification — and separately sorts Magisterial functions into a judicial bucket (evidence-appreciation, decisions exposing someone to punishment or custody) that only a Judicial Magistrate may exercise, and an administrative bucket (licensing, sanctioning or withdrawing prosecutions) that an Executive Magistrate may exercise. Section 4 fixes which procedure governs which offences: BNS, 2023 offences follow the Sanhita's own procedure without qualification; offences under any other law follow the same procedure too, but subject to whatever special manner-or-place rules that other law separately prescribes. Section 5 is the general saving clause — nothing in the Sanhita overrides a special or local law's own jurisdiction, power, or procedure unless the Sanhita says so specifically.
Together these three sections answer a question that recurs constantly in mixed-statute practice: when a special enactment (a forest law, an excise law, a food-safety law) uses procedural vocabulary of its own, how much of the BNSS machinery actually applies? The answer is: all of it, except wherever the special law has made its own specific arrangement — and section 3's judicial/administrative sorting rule decides, for functions under that other law, which flavour of Magistrate is even competent to exercise them.
:::

::: story The licensing Magistrate who was asked to decide a bail-like question
A local excise law confers a function on "the Magistrate" to suspend a trader's licence for a stated period. The same trader is also facing prosecution, and his advocate tries to get the licensing Magistrate — an Executive Magistrate designated under the excise law — to also rule on a custody-related question arising from the criminal case, since "the Magistrate" is already seized of related facts. Section 3(2) forecloses this shortcut directly: licence suspension is administrative or executive in nature, squarely within an Executive Magistrate's competence under section 3(2)(b); but any question involving the appreciation of evidence or a decision exposing the trader to punishment, penalty, or custody falls under section 3(2)(a), and is exercisable only by a Judicial Magistrate. The excise law's grant of power to "the Magistrate" does not import a general judicial competence the Executive Magistrate does not otherwise have.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 3 — Construction of references
"3. (1) Unless the context otherwise requires, any reference in any law, to a Magistrate without any qualifying words, Magistrate of the first class or a Magistrate of the second class [[!shall]], in relation to any area, be construed as a reference to a Judicial Magistrate of the first class or Judicial Magistrate of the second class, as the case may be, exercising jurisdiction in such area.
(2) Where, under any law, other than this Sanhita, the functions exercisable by a Magistrate relate to matters,—
(a) which involve the appreciation or shifting of evidence or the formulation of any decision which exposes any person to any punishment or penalty or detention in custody pending investigation, inquiry or trial or would have the effect of sending him for trial before any Court, they [[!shall]], subject to the provisions of this Sanhita, be exercisable by a Judicial Magistrate; or
(b) which are administrative or executive in nature, such as, the granting of a licence, the suspension or cancellation of a licence, sanctioning a prosecution or withdrawing from a prosecution, they [[!shall]], subject to the provisions of clause (a) be exercisable by an Executive Magistrate."
:::

::: proviso Section 4 — Trial of offences under Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 and other laws
"4. (1) All offences under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 [[!shall]] be investigated, inquired into, tried, and otherwise dealt with according to the provisions hereinafter contained.
(2) All offences under any other law [[!shall]] be investigated, inquired into, tried, and otherwise dealt with according to the same provisions, but subject to any enactment for the time being in force regulating the manner or place of investigating, inquiring into, trying or otherwise dealing with such offences."

### In plain English

Section 3 exists because Parliament and State legislatures have, over the decades, written hundreds of laws that casually refer to "the Magistrate" without saying which kind. Section 3(1) supplies the default answer — read it as a Judicial Magistrate of the first or second class — while section 3(2) then splits Magisterial functions generally into two buckets regardless of which law created them: judicial-flavoured functions (weighing evidence, exposing someone to punishment or custody) go to a Judicial Magistrate, and administrative-flavoured functions (granting or suspending a licence, sanctioning or withdrawing a prosecution) go to an Executive Magistrate — with the judicial bucket always winning if a function has features of both.

Section 4 is the simplest housekeeping rule in the chapter: BNS, 2023 offences are tried under the Sanhita's ordinary procedure, full stop; offences under any other law are tried under the same Sanhita procedure too, unless that other law has made its own special arrangement about how or where the offence is investigated, inquired into, or tried, in which case the special arrangement is layered on top. Section 5 is the general safety net underneath everything: unless the Sanhita specifically says it is overriding a special or local law, it is not — the special or local law's own jurisdiction, powers, and procedure survive by default.

### What changed from the CrPC

::: info Verify before citing
BNSS sections 3, 4, and 5 correspond, on a straightforward section-by-section read, to CrPC sections 3, 4, and 5 respectively — again preserving the CrPC's own section numbering for this stretch of Chapter I. The correspondence should be independently verified against the CrPC text before being relied upon in drafting or argument.
:::

On a provision-by-provision comparison, the operative substance of all three sections — the construction-of-references rule with its judicial/administrative split, the BNS-offences-versus-other-law-offences rule, and the general saving clause — tracks the CrPC predecessor closely. The most notable knock-on change is not in the text of these sections themselves but in what "Judicial Magistrate" now means given section 2's silence on "Metropolitan Magistrate": any older law that still refers to "the Magistrate" in a former metropolitan area now resolves, through section 3(1), to an ordinary Judicial Magistrate of the first class rather than to a Metropolitan Magistrate, because that separate category no longer exists in the Sanhita's own vocabulary.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct The special law that set its own limitation period
A State forest law creates an offence and separately prescribes that prosecutions under it must be launched within six months, a shorter period than any general BNSS limitation rule that might otherwise apply. The accused argues that BNSS, being the later and general Sanhita, overrides the forest law's shorter period. Is the accused correct?

::: ruling
No, not merely by virtue of BNSS being later or general. Section 5 preserves any special or local law's own jurisdiction, power, or procedure unless the Sanhita contains a specific provision to the contrary. Section 4(2) reinforces this for the trial of offences under other laws specifically: they are dealt with according to the Sanhita's provisions, but subject to any enactment regulating the manner or place of dealing with such offences. Absent a specific BNSS provision displacing the forest law's own limitation period, that shorter period continues to govern.
:::
::::

:::: instinct The Executive Magistrate asked to decide guilt under a local licensing law
A local trade law empowers "the Magistrate" both to cancel a trader's licence and, on the same facts, to determine whether the trader committed an offence carrying imprisonment under that law. An Executive Magistrate, having already handled the licence cancellation as an administrative matter, proceeds to also determine the imprisonment question himself. Is that correct?

::: ruling
No. Licence cancellation is squarely administrative or executive under section 3(2)(b), within the Executive Magistrate's competence. But determining whether an offence carrying imprisonment has been committed necessarily involves appreciating evidence and making a decision exposing the trader to punishment — squarely within section 3(2)(a), which clause (b) is itself made subject to. That determination is exercisable only by a Judicial Magistrate; the Executive Magistrate handling the licence question does not thereby acquire competence over the punishment question as well.
:::

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

::: info Research gap — no case promoted
No specific Supreme Court authority interpreting BNSS sections 3 to 5 themselves is confidently recalled for promotion here. The judicial/administrative distinction in section 3(2) and the saving-clause principle in section 5 both have a substantial body of case law under the identically-structured CrPC provisions; that line of authority should be researched and a specific citation sourced and verified before being pleaded.

#### Sword and shield

As a **sword**, a party can use section 3(2)(a) to insist that a decision genuinely involving evidence-appreciation or exposure to punishment or custody be handled by a Judicial Magistrate, even where the enabling special law loosely says only "the Magistrate." As a **shield**, a party relying on a special or local law's own procedural protections — a shorter limitation period, a different forum, a special defence — can invoke section 5 to resist an argument that the general BNSS scheme has silently displaced them.

#### The limitation clock

Sections 3 to 5 do not themselves create a limitation period. Section 4(2)'s "subject to any enactment... regulating the manner or place" language, read with section 5's saving clause, means that any limitation period fixed by a special or local law for offences under that law continues to apply in full, undisturbed by any general BNSS timeline.

#### Interlocking matrix

| Situation | Governing provision | What follows |
|---|---|---|
| Another law refers to "the Magistrate" without specifying which kind | Section 3(1) | Read as a Judicial Magistrate of the first or second class, as the area requires |
| Function under another law involves evidence-appreciation or exposure to punishment/custody | Section 3(2)(a) | Exercisable only by a Judicial Magistrate |
| Function under another law is administrative or executive (licensing, prosecution sanction) | Section 3(2)(b) | Exercisable by an Executive Magistrate, subject to clause (a) |
| Offence charged under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 | Section 4(1) | Sanhita's own procedure applies without further qualification |
| Offence charged under any other law | Section 4(2) | Sanhita's procedure applies, subject to that other law's special manner-or-place rules |
| Special or local law's own jurisdiction, power, or procedure said to conflict with the Sanhita | Section 5 | Special or local law prevails absent a specific contrary BNSS provision |

#### Strategy and drafting

**Drafting traps.**

1. Do not assume every function labelled "the Magistrate" under a special law is automatically exercisable by whichever Magistrate happens to be handling the file — apply section 3(2)'s judicial/administrative split explicitly, and flag any function with both features as falling under clause (a).
2. Do not argue that BNSS silently overrides a special or local law's own procedure merely because BNSS is the later and more general enactment — section 5 requires a specific contrary provision, not general displacement by implication.
3. When a client is prosecuted under a special or local enactment, always check that enactment's own manner-and-place rules under section 4(2) before assuming the Sanhita's default procedure applies unmodified.

**Timing.** Where a special or local law prescribes its own limitation period, calendar that period independently of any general BNSS timeline — section 5 and section 4(2) together mean the special period controls, and missing it because of a mistaken assumption that BNSS's general scheme applies instead can be fatal to a prosecution or a defence.

**Model checklist when a special or local enactment is in play:**

1. Identify every function that enactment confers on "the Magistrate," and classify each as judicial or administrative under section 3(2) before assuming a particular Magistrate is competent to exercise it.
2. Check whether the enactment prescribes its own manner, place, jurisdiction, limitation period, or procedure, and treat those as surviving under sections 4(2) and 5 unless the Sanhita specifically overrides them.
3. Where a function seems to straddle both administrative and judicial character, resolve the doubt in favour of Judicial Magistrate competence, consistent with section 3(2)(b) being subject to clause (a).
