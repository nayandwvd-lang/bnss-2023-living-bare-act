# Chapter XXXIV — Execution, Suspension, Remission and Commutation of Sentences

## Sections 453–456 — Carrying a death sentence into effect, and the pregnancy bar

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute A death sentence does not execute itself, and this cluster is the Sessions Court's marching orders once the appellate and confirmation stages are over. Section 453 covers the ordinary route: once the Court of Session receives the High Court's order confirming (or otherwise disposing of) a section 407 reference, it must issue a warrant or take whatever other steps are necessary to carry that order into effect. Section 454 covers the less common route: where the High Court itself passes a death sentence, on appeal or in revision, the Court of Session again acts on receiving that order, issuing the warrant. Section 455 is the postponement rule that runs alongside both: where a further appeal lies to the Supreme Court, or where the convict intends to seek a certificate or special leave to appeal, the High Court must order execution postponed until that avenue is exhausted or the time to invoke it has run. Section 456 is a narrow, absolute rule: a woman sentenced to death who is found to be pregnant has her sentence commuted to imprisonment for life — not postponed, not suspended, but commuted outright.
:::

::: story A warrant issued, then recalled, because a Supreme Court appeal is still live
A Court of Session receives the High Court's order confirming a death sentence and, under section 453, begins the process of issuing a warrant to carry it into effect. Before the warrant is executed, the convict's counsel brings to the Sessions Court's attention that an appeal under article 134(1)(a) of the Constitution — as of right, since the High Court reversed an acquittal and imposed a death sentence in appeal — lies to the Supreme Court, and that the High Court's own section 455(1) postponement order is still in force pending expiry of the appeal period. The Sessions Court, on verifying the postponement order, withholds execution notwithstanding the confirmation, because section 455 places the postponement obligation on the High Court and makes it binding regardless of what section 453 would otherwise require. Separately, in an unrelated matter, a woman under sentence of death is medically confirmed to be pregnant; the High Court does not merely postpone her execution pending delivery — section 456 requires it to commute the sentence to imprisonment for life outright, a permanent change in the sentence itself, not a pause in carrying out the original one.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 453 — Execution of order passed under section 409

"453. When in a case submitted to the High Court for the confirmation of a sentence of death, the Court of Session receives the order of confirmation or other order of the High Court thereon, it [[!shall]] cause such order to be carried into effect by issuing a warrant or taking such other steps as may be necessary."
:::

::: proviso Section 454 — Execution of sentence of death passed by High Court

"454. When a sentence of death is passed by the High Court in appeal or in revision, the Court of Session [[!shall]], on receiving the order of the High Court, cause the sentence to be carried into effect by issuing a warrant."
:::

::: proviso Section 455 — Postponement of execution of sentence of death in case of appeal to Supreme Court

"455. (1) Where a person is sentenced to death by the High Court and an appeal from its judgment lies to the Supreme Court under sub-clause (a) or sub-clause (b) of clause (1) of article 134 of the Constitution, the High Court [[!shall]] order the execution of the sentence to be postponed until the period allowed for preferring such appeal has expired, or if, an appeal is preferred within that period, until such appeal is disposed of.
(2) Where a sentence of death is passed or confirmed by the High Court, and the person sentenced makes an application to the High Court for the grant of a certificate under article 132 or under sub-clause (c) of clause (1) of article 134 of the Constitution, the High Court [[!shall]] order the execution of the sentence to be postponed until such application is disposed of by the High Court, or if a certificate is granted on such application, until the period allowed for preferring an appeal to the Supreme Court on such certificate has expired.
(3) Where a sentence of death is passed or confirmed by the High Court, and the High Court is satisfied that the person sentenced intends to present a petition to the Supreme Court for the grant of special leave to appeal under article 136 of the Constitution, the High Court [[!shall]] order the execution of the sentence to be postponed for such period as it considers sufficient to enable him to present such petition."
:::

::: proviso Section 456 — Commutation of sentence of death on pregnant woman

"456. If a woman sentenced to death is found to be pregnant, the High Court [[!shall]] commute the sentence to imprisonment for life."
:::

### In plain English

Once a death sentence clears the confirmation and appellate process, someone still has to physically set the machinery of execution in motion — that is the Sessions Court's job, whether the order it is acting on came from a confirmation reference (section 453) or from the High Court's own appellate or revisional decision (section 454). But the process is not allowed to race ahead of a convict's remaining constitutional appeal rights: sections 455(1)-(3) require the High Court itself to build in a pause — for the ordinary Supreme Court appeal period, for a pending certificate application, or for time to file a special leave petition — before execution can proceed. Section 456 sits apart from all of this timing machinery: it is not about postponing execution for a period, but about permanently changing the sentence itself, because a pregnant woman under sentence of death is, by this section, no longer a person who can be executed at all — her sentence becomes imprisonment for life.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct A High Court confirms a death sentence and, believing the convict has no further appellate avenue available (mistakenly, as it turns out — an article 134(1)(a) appeal as-of-right does lie, since the High Court reversed a trial-court acquittal), does not pass any section 455 postponement order. The Court of Session, receiving the confirmation order under section 453, proceeds to issue a warrant. Is the Sessions Court's action lawful?

::: ruling No, though the immediate failure lies with the High Court rather than the Sessions Court. Section 455(1) makes the postponement order mandatory ("shall") wherever an article 134(1)(a) or (b) appeal lies — it is not discretionary, and the High Court's mistaken belief that no appeal lies does not exempt it from the obligation where an appeal in fact lies as of right. Once the High Court's omission is identified, the correct course is for the convict to bring the article 134(1)(a) appeal right to the attention of the Court of Session or the High Court immediately, and for execution to be halted pending a corrected postponement order — a section 453 warrant issued in the absence of a required section 455 postponement, on facts where the postponement should have been ordered, does not cure the underlying constitutional appeal right.
:::
::::

:::: instinct A woman under sentence of death is pregnant at the time sentence is passed by the trial court, but by the time the case reaches the High Court for confirmation, she has already delivered. Does section 456 still apply, since she "was" pregnant at some point in the proceedings?

::: ruling No. Section 456 turns on the person being "found to be pregnant" — read naturally, this requires a pregnancy that exists at the relevant time the High Court is considering the matter, not a historical fact about an earlier stage of the proceedings. Once delivery has occurred, there is no pregnancy for the High Court to find, and the commutation trigger is not met on that basis. This does not foreclose other grounds for commutation or clemency that may independently arise — including the welfare of a newborn or infant child, which may be relevant under other provisions or in a mercy petition under section 472 — but section 456 itself is not engaged by a pregnancy that has already ended by the time the High Court considers the case.
:::
::::

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

::: info Research gap — no case promoted

The CrPC's substantial case law on postponement of execution pending Supreme Court appeal rights, and on the pregnancy-commutation rule, remains directly relevant in substance — but every citation must be independently re-verified against the BNSS's renumbered provisions before it is pleaded in any court.
:::

#### Sword and shield

For the **defence**, section 455 is a critical safety mechanism to invoke proactively rather than wait for the High Court to remember it — the moment a death sentence is confirmed or passed in appeal, defence counsel should immediately place on record whether an article 134(1)(a)/(b) appeal lies, or whether a certificate or special leave petition is contemplated, and seek an explicit postponement order rather than assuming one will issue automatically. Section 456 is an absolute shield for a pregnant convict — it requires no application or discretion once the fact of pregnancy is established; counsel's task is to get the medical finding before the High Court promptly. For the **State**, sections 453-454 are the operative duty once all appellate avenues are genuinely exhausted — but proceeding to execution without first confirming, on the record, that no section 455 postponement is outstanding is a serious procedural exposure.

#### The limitation clock

Section 455 itself creates the relevant "clock" in this cluster: postponement runs until the ordinary Supreme Court appeal period expires (or a filed appeal is disposed of), until a pending certificate application is disposed of (with a further postponement for any resulting appeal period), or for such period as the High Court considers sufficient for a special leave petition to be filed. There is no independent limitation period on the Sessions Court's sections 453/454 duty to act once the postponement conditions are cleared — but that duty cannot be exercised prematurely while any of section 455's postponement conditions remain live.

#### Interlocking matrix

- **Section 407-409 (Chapter XXX)** — the confirmation reference whose outcome section 453 acts on; read this cluster as the execution-stage continuation of that chapter.
- **Article 132, 134, 136 of the Constitution** — the substantive appeal, certificate, and special leave rights section 455's postponement duty exists to protect; verify which sub-clause is actually engaged on the facts (as-of-right appeal versus certificate versus special leave) since each triggers a differently worded postponement obligation.
- **Section 472 (this Chapter, cluster 5)** — the mercy petition process, which runs on a separate timeline commencing only after the appellate/postponement process in this cluster has concluded.
- **Section 474 (this Chapter, cluster 6)** — commutation powers of the appropriate Government generally; section 456's pregnancy-commutation is a standalone, court-ordered commutation distinct from that executive power.

#### Strategy and drafting

- The moment a death sentence is confirmed or passed on appeal, file a written note before the High Court identifying precisely which Supreme Court avenue (article 134(1)(a)/(b) as-of-right appeal, article 132/134(1)(c) certificate, or article 136 special leave) is available or intended, and expressly request the corresponding section 455 postponement order — do not rely on the High Court to raise this unprompted.
- Where a pregnancy commutation under section 456 is in issue, secure and place on record a clear, dated medical finding before the High Court disposes of the confirmation or appeal, since the commutation is tied to a present finding of pregnancy, not a historical one.
- Before any execution step is taken at the Sessions Court level, obtain written confirmation on the record that no section 455 postponement order remains in force and that no Supreme Court appeal, certificate application, or special leave petition is pending — this is a documentary check, not a formality.
- Distinguish, when advising a client, between section 455's temporary postponement (which still ends in execution unless the appeal succeeds) and section 456's outright commutation (a permanent change in sentence) — the two operate on entirely different logics and should not be conflated in submissions.

## Sections 457–460 — Warrants for imprisonment: where, how, and to whom

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Part B is the operational hand-off from "a Court has sentenced this person to imprisonment" to "this person is now lawfully inside a specific jail." Section 458 obliges the sentencing Court to forward a warrant to the jail (and, if the accused is not already there, to forward the accused himself), with a narrow carve-out for the trivial case of imprisonment till the rising of the Court, where no warrant is needed at all; if the accused is not present when sentenced, the Court must issue an arrest warrant instead, and the sentence only commences from the date of that arrest. Section 459 says every such warrant must be directed to the officer in charge of the specific jail or place of confinement — not to the jail in the abstract, not to the police. Section 460 is the narrowest procedural point of all: when a prisoner is to be confined in a jail, the warrant is lodged with the jailor, the person who actually has custody. Section 457, sitting just before this Part in the text, gives the State Government the power to designate what place a person liable to imprisonment is confined in, and handles the mechanics of moving someone between a civil jail and a criminal jail.
:::

::: story A sentence commencing from arrest, not from the date of judgment
A Magistrate convicts an accused person in his absence — the accused, out on bail, failed to attend the pronouncement despite notice, and the Magistrate proceeded under section 392's proviso permitting pronouncement notwithstanding his absence. The sentence is six months' rigorous imprisonment. Because the accused was not present in Court when sentenced, section 458(2) requires the Court to issue a warrant for his arrest, rather than the ordinary forwarding warrant under section 458(1) — and critically, the sentence does not commence from the date of the judgment, but only from the date the accused is actually arrested on that warrant. Three weeks later, the accused is arrested; the warrant, properly directed to the officer in charge of the jail under section 459, accompanies him, and on reaching the jail it is lodged with the jailor under section 460. His six months begins running that day, not three weeks earlier — a fact that matters considerably if he later seeks parole or calculates his release date.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 457 — Power to appoint place of imprisonment

"457. (1) Except when otherwise provided by any law for the time being in force, the State Government [[?may]] direct in what place any person liable to be imprisoned or committed to custody under this Sanhita shall be confined.
(2) If any person liable to be imprisoned or committed to custody under this Sanhita is in confinement in a civil jail, the Court or Magistrate ordering the imprisonment or committal [[?may]] direct that the person be removed to a criminal jail.
(3) When a person is removed to a criminal jail under sub-section (2), he [[!shall]], on being released therefrom, be sent back to the civil jail, unless either—
(a) three years have elapsed since he was removed to the criminal jail, in which case he shall be deemed to have been released from the civil jail under section 58 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908; or
(b) the Court which ordered his imprisonment in the civil jail has certified to the officer in charge of the criminal jail that he is entitled to be released under section 58 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908."
:::

::: proviso Section 458 — Execution of sentence of imprisonment

"458. (1) Where the accused is sentenced to imprisonment for life or to imprisonment for a term in cases other than those provided for by section 453, the Court passing the sentence [[!shall]] forthwith forward a warrant to the jail or other place in which he is, or is to be, confined, and, unless the accused is already confined in such jail or other place, shall forward him to such jail or other place, with the warrant:

Provided that where the accused is sentenced to imprisonment till the rising of the Court, it shall not be necessary to prepare or forward a warrant to a jail, and the accused [[?may]] be confined in such place as the Court may direct.

(2) Where the accused is not present in Court when he is sentenced to such imprisonment as is mentioned in sub-section (1), the Court [[!shall]] issue a warrant for his arrest for the purpose of forwarding him to the jail or other place in which he is to be confined; and in such case, the sentence shall commence on the date of his arrest."
:::

::: proviso Section 459 — Direction of warrant for execution

"459. Every warrant for the execution of a sentence of imprisonment [[!shall]] be directed to the officer in charge of the jail or other place in which the prisoner is, or is to be, confined."
:::

::: proviso Section 460 — Warrant with whom to be lodged

"460. When the prisoner is to be confined in a jail, the warrant [[!shall]] be lodged with the jailor."
:::

### In plain English

This cluster is about paperwork that is not really paperwork — it is the chain of custody that turns a courtroom sentence into a person actually and lawfully held in a specific place. Section 458 is the general rule (forward a warrant, and the person, to the jail) with two real-world complications built in: a token sentence lasting only until the Court rises needs no warrant at all, and a sentence passed on someone who simply is not in the room that day cannot commence until that person is actually caught and arrested — the clock does not run in absentia. Sections 459 and 460 are about precision, not policy: the warrant has to name the actual officer in charge of the actual place of confinement, and once the prisoner is in a jail, the physical warrant has to end up with the jailor specifically, not merely somewhere in the jail's paperwork. Section 457 is a separate, background power — the State Government's authority to designate where categories of prisoners are held, and a narrower rule for moving someone who happens to be sitting in a civil jail (usually for a civil debt or contempt matter) into a criminal jail instead.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct An accused is sentenced to "imprisonment till the rising of the Court" for contempt committed in the course of proceedings — a routine, same-day disposal. The Court's staff, following the Part B pattern used for ordinary term sentences, prepare and forward a formal jail warrant anyway, and the accused is sent to the district jail rather than held in a court lock-up for the remaining hour of the sitting. Is this a defect?

::: ruling It is not the kind of defect that invalidates the sentence, but it is an unnecessary and arguably improper step. The section 458(1) proviso is explicit that for a rising-of-the-Court sentence, "it shall not be necessary to prepare or forward a warrant to a jail, and the accused may be confined in such place as the Court may direct" — the evident purpose being to avoid exactly this kind of formal jail commitment for what is meant to be a brief, in-house measure. Preparing a full jail warrant and sending the accused to the district jail defeats that purpose in substance, even though the proviso's language is permissive ("shall not be necessary," not an outright prohibition) rather than a hard bar. The better practice, and the one consistent with the proviso's design, is to hold the person in a court lock-up or equivalent for the balance of the sitting.
:::
::::

:::: instinct A person is sentenced to three years' imprisonment and, on the day of sentencing, is present in Court, has the warrant forwarded to the jail under section 458(1), but through an administrative delay at the jail, is not actually admitted and confined for four more days. Does the sentence commence on the date of the Court's order, or on the date of actual admission to the jail?

::: ruling Section 458(1) does not contain a commencement rule of its own comparable to section 458(2)'s explicit "the sentence shall commence on the date of his arrest" — that in-absentia commencement rule is specifically tied to the arrest-warrant route under sub-section (2). Where the accused was present and sentenced in Court under sub-section (1), the ordinary position is that the sentence commences from the date of the judgment/order itself (subject to any set-off under section 468 for pre-conviction detention), not from the date the jail administratively processes his admission. An administrative delay in intake at the jail's end is not attributable to the accused and does not shift the commencement date against him; if anything, any resulting delay is a matter for the jail administration to account for, not a basis to extend the sentence's effective term.
:::
::::

#### Sword and shield

For the **defence**, section 458(2)'s in-absentia commencement rule is worth flagging proactively whenever a client is sentenced without being present — it directly affects release-date calculations, and a jail record that mistakenly runs the sentence from the judgment date rather than the arrest date is worth correcting immediately through the sentencing Court. For the **prosecution/jail administration**, sections 459-460's precision requirements are basic custody-chain hygiene: a warrant directed to the wrong officer, or never formally lodged with the jailor, creates a documentary gap that a habeas corpus petition can exploit even where the underlying sentence is entirely sound.

#### The limitation clock

This cluster does not create an independent limitation period; its significance is in fixing commencement dates that feed into other limitation-sensitive calculations — most importantly, the date from which parole eligibility, remission calculations, and (for the 475 fourteen-year minimum in Part E) service-of-sentence periods are reckoned. Getting the section 458(1)/(2) commencement date right at the outset avoids downstream disputes about exactly when a sentence began.

#### Interlocking matrix

- **Section 468 (this Chapter, cluster 4)** — the set-off of pre-conviction detention against the sentence term, which interacts with but is distinct from the section 458 commencement-date rules.
- **Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, section 58** — expressly cross-referenced in section 457(3) for the civil-jail release deeming provision; verify the CPC section's current text before relying on the three-year or certification triggers.
- **Section 453-454 (this Chapter, cluster 1)** — section 458(1) expressly carves out "cases other than those provided for by section 453," so confirm which route (death-reference execution versus ordinary term-imprisonment execution) actually governs the sentence in hand.
- **Habeas corpus jurisdiction (Constitution, articles 226/32)** — the practical remedy where a custody-chain defect under sections 459-460 is used to challenge the legality of continued detention.

#### Strategy and drafting

- Whenever a client is sentenced in absentia, immediately verify and, if necessary, formally place on record the actual date of arrest, since that date — not the judgment date — is the sentence's legal commencement point under section 458(2).
- For a rising-of-the-Court sentence, object on record if the Court's staff attempt to process a full jail-warrant commitment; the proviso to section 458(1) is designed to avoid exactly that, and a client should not spend the balance of a sitting being formally jailed for a token sentence.
- On any custody challenge, request the actual warrant and confirm it is directed to the officer in charge of the specific place of confinement (section 459) and was lodged with the jailor (section 460) — a defect here is a genuine, checkable procedural ground, not a technicality to be assumed away.
- Where a client has spent time in a civil jail and is later moved to a criminal jail under section 457(2), calendar the three-year mark under section 457(3)(a) independently, since it operates as a deeming provision regardless of whether anyone applies for it.
