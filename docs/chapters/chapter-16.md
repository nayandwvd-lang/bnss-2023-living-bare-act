# Chapter XVI — Complaints to Magistrates

## Sections 223–224 — Examining the complainant, and the accused's early right to be heard

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute A private complaint does not become a live prosecution just because someone signed and filed it. Before a Magistrate can take cognizance on a complaint, section 223 requires him to put the complainant — and any witnesses present — on oath and get their account down in writing, and it adds something genuinely unusual: the accused must be given an opportunity of being heard *before cognizance is even taken*, not merely after process later issues.
The ordinary route puts the complainant and witnesses under oath, reduces the substance to writing, and has everyone sign it — a basic truth-testing filter before a case is allowed to exist at all. Two of the three provisos carve out when that examination is unnecessary: a written complaint from a public servant acting in official capacity, or from a Court, needs no examination; and if a case is simply made over to another Magistrate under section 212 after the first Magistrate has already examined everyone, the second Magistrate does not have to repeat it. Section 223(2) then adds a wholly separate, heavier gate for a different situation — a complaint *against* a public servant for something done in the discharge of official duty — requiring both that the public servant be given a chance to respond, and that a report from his superior officer be obtained, before cognizance. Section 224 is the simplest of the two: what happens when the complaint lands with a Magistrate who was never the right one to take it in the first place.
:::

::: story The tenant who never knew, and the officer who got to answer first
A landlord files a complaint aimed at a tenant. Before this Magistrate can take cognizance, section 223(1)'s closing proviso requires that the tenant — the accused — be given an opportunity of being heard, something the old procedure under the CrPC's equivalent stage did not require at all; ordinarily an accused only entered the picture once process was issued and a summons arrived. Here, he gets a voice before the case is even formally taken up. In a separate matter, a complaint alleges that a police officer used excessive force during a raid — an offence allegedly committed in the discharge of his official duties. Section 223(2) means the Magistrate cannot take cognizance merely because the complainant's sworn account was convincing; the officer must first be given the chance to explain the situation as he saw it, and a report on the incident must come in from his superior officer, before cognizance can even be considered.
:::

> **223. (1)** A Magistrate having jurisdiction while taking cognizance of an offence on complaint [[!shall]] examine upon oath the complainant and the witnesses present, if any, and the substance of such examination [[!shall]] be reduced to writing and [[!shall]] be signed by the complainant and the witnesses, and also by the Magistrate:
>
> *Provided that no cognizance of an offence shall be taken by the Magistrate without giving the accused an opportunity of being heard:*
>
> *Provided further that when the complaint is made in writing, the Magistrate need not examine the complainant and the witnesses—*
>
> *(a) if a public servant acting or purporting to act in the discharge of his official duties or a Court has made the complaint; or*
>
> *(b) if the Magistrate makes over the case for inquiry or trial to another Magistrate under section 212:*
>
> *Provided also that if the Magistrate makes over the case to another Magistrate under section 212 after examining the complainant and the witnesses, the latter Magistrate need not re-examine them.*
>
> **(2)** A Magistrate [[!shall]] not take cognizance on a complaint against a public servant for any offence alleged to have been committed in course of the discharge of his official functions or duties unless—
>
> (a) such public servant is given an opportunity to make assertions as to the situation that led to the incident so alleged; and
>
> (b) a report containing facts and circumstances of the incident from the officer superior to such public servant is received.
>
> **224.** If the complaint is made to a Magistrate who is not competent to take cognizance of the offence, he [[!shall]]—
>
> (a) if the complaint is in writing, return it for presentation to the proper Court with an endorsement to that effect;
>
> (b) if the complaint is not in writing, direct the complainant to the proper Court.

::: proviso The accused's pre-cognizance hearing right runs through the whole section
Section 223(1)'s first proviso is not confined to the examination-of-complainant mechanics around it — "no cognizance of an offence shall be taken by the Magistrate without giving the accused an opportunity of being heard" is a freestanding condition on cognizance itself. This marks a real departure from the traditional model in which an accused had no formal role at the cognizance stage and first entered the process once summons issued; here, the accused's voice is invited in earlier, before the case is even formally taken up.
:::

::: proviso Section 223(2) runs in the opposite direction from the proviso 223(1)(a) exemption
Section 223(1)'s second proviso, clause (a), exempts examination where the *complainant* is a public servant or a Court. Section 223(2) is about the reverse situation entirely — where the *accused* is a public servant, and the offence is alleged to have occurred in the discharge of his official duties. The two provisions are not in tension; they simply look in opposite directions, and a single case could in principle trigger both if, for instance, one public servant complained against another about conduct in the discharge of the latter's duties.
:::

### In plain English

Think of section 223 as a two-layer filter before a private complaint can become a real case. Layer one, the default: put the complainant and any witnesses under oath, write down what they say, get it signed — unless the complaint already came from a public servant or a Court in writing, or the case is simply being handed to another Magistrate who's already had that done. Layer two, only for complaints against a public servant about something done in the course of his job: the public servant gets to respond first, and his superior has to weigh in with a report, before the Magistrate can even take cognizance. And no matter which layer applies, the accused — of any kind — has to be given a chance to be heard before cognizance, a notably early entry point for the defence compared to older procedure. Section 224 is unrelated to any of this — it is simply the traffic-directing rule for a complaint that landed with a Magistrate who was never the right one to receive it.

### What changed from the CrPC

⚠️ *Provisional mapping, pending dedicated verification pass: BNSS section 223 appears to track CrPC section 200 (examination of complainant) and BNSS section 224 appears to track CrPC section 201 (procedure by Magistrate not competent to take cognizance of the case). Two features of section 223 stand out as likely significant changes and are flagged rather than asserted: (1) the first proviso to section 223(1), requiring the accused be given an opportunity of being heard before cognizance itself, does not appear to have a direct equivalent in the CrPC's section 200 examination stage, where the accused traditionally had no formal role until process issued — if confirmed, this is a material procedural departure; (2) section 223(2)'s hearing-and-superior-report precondition for complaints against a public servant for official-duty conduct also does not appear to trace to an equivalent CrPC provision at this stage, and appears to be a new, and reportedly debated, BNSS-era addition. Both points require independent confirmation before being asserted as settled in a filing.*

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct A Magistrate takes cognizance on a written complaint filed by a police officer concerning facts within the scope of her official duties, without personally examining her or any witnesses on oath. The defence argues cognizance is invalid for skipping the section 223(1) examination.
::: ruling No examination was required, but check the accused's hearing right separately
Section 223(1)'s second proviso, clause (a), exempts exactly this situation — a written complaint made by a public servant acting or purporting to act in the discharge of official duties needs no examination of the complainant or witnesses. The defence's objection on that specific ground fails. It remains necessary, however, to confirm the accused was separately given an opportunity of being heard before cognizance, since that requirement is not tied to, or excused by, the examination exemption.
:::
::::

:::: instinct A complaint is filed against a police officer for conduct during an official raid. The Magistrate takes cognizance relying solely on the complainant's compelling sworn testimony under section 223(1), without giving the officer any chance to respond and without obtaining any report from his superior officer.
::: ruling Cognizance is improperly taken
Section 223(2) is a separate, additional precondition that applies specifically because the accused is a public servant and the alleged offence occurred in the discharge of his official duties. Satisfying section 223(1)'s examination requirement does not substitute for section 223(2)'s own twin conditions — the officer's opportunity to respond and the superior officer's report. Both must independently be satisfied before cognizance is proper here.
:::

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

⚠️ *No case citation is promoted for this cluster. Section 223(1)'s traditional examination-of-complainant mechanics are well-trodden CrPC-era ground, but section 223(1)'s pre-cognizance hearing proviso and section 223(2)'s hearing-and-report gate both appear to be new BNSS-era features without an identified, confidently-recalled defining Supreme Court authority construing them as they now stand — flagged as a research gap under "The authorities" rather than guessed.*

#### Sword and shield

As a **shield**, section 223(1)'s pre-cognizance hearing proviso gives every accused an unusually early voice in the process, and section 223(2) gives public servants accused of official-duty conduct a further, specific layer of protection against being hauled into cognizance on an unanswered, unreported allegation. As a **sword**, the defence can challenge cognizance taken without satisfying whichever precondition actually applied — the examination requirement, the accused's hearing opportunity, or, for a public-servant accused, both limbs of section 223(2) independently.

#### The limitation clock

No independent limitation period is fixed by this cluster.

#### Interlocking matrix

Section 223(1)'s third proviso interlocks directly with section 212 (Chapter XV, making-over of cases). Section 223(2)'s hearing-and-report gate sits alongside, and is independent of, section 218's sanction requirement (Chapter XV, flagship) for complaints against a public servant regarding official-duty conduct — a practitioner handling such a complaint should check both gates separately, not assume satisfying one satisfies the other. Section 224 interlocks with the general territorial and jurisdictional framework of Chapter XIV.

#### The authorities

⚠️ *None promoted for this cluster; not independently verified for this rendering.*

#### Strategy and drafting

**Model checklist for reviewing this cluster's provisions:**

1. Confirm whether the section 223(1) examination requirement was satisfied, or correctly exempted under one of its provisos, before treating cognizance as validly taken.
2. Separately confirm the accused was given an opportunity of being heard before cognizance — this is not excused merely because the examination itself was exempted.
3. Where the accused is a public servant and the alleged offence concerns official-duty conduct, check section 223(2)'s hearing-and-report gate and section 218's sanction requirement (Chapter XV) as two independent preconditions, not one.
4. Where a complaint was misdirected to an incompetent Magistrate, confirm the correct section 224 procedure (return with endorsement for a written complaint, direction to the proper Court for an oral one) was followed rather than an improvised alternative.

## Sections 225–226 — Postponing process: inquiry, investigation, and dismissal for want of sufficient ground

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Examining the complainant under section 223 does not mean process against the accused issues automatically. Section 225 lets — and in one specific circumstance requires — the Magistrate to pause, inquire further himself, or direct an investigation, precisely so that a complaint is properly tested before an accused is even summoned. Section 226 is the natural next step: if that testing leaves the Magistrate unconvinced, he must dismiss the complaint and say briefly why.
Section 225(1) gives the Magistrate a general discretion to postpone the issue of process and either inquire himself or direct an investigation "for the purpose of deciding whether or not there is sufficient ground for proceeding" — but that discretion hardens into a duty the moment the accused resides beyond the Magistrate's own jurisdiction. Two provisos then restrict how this plays out for offences exclusively triable by a Court of Session: no outside investigation may be directed at all, and if the Magistrate instead holds his own inquiry, he must call for and examine all of the complainant's witnesses, not merely some. A separate proviso bars directing any investigation at all, for a complaint not made by a Court, unless the complainant and any witnesses present have already been examined under section 223. Section 226 then closes the loop: no sufficient ground, after all this, means dismissal — with reasons briefly recorded, not a bare rejection.
:::

::: story The distant accused, and the Sessions-triable complaint
A complaint names an accused who lives in another state entirely. Section 225(1) does not leave postponement to the Magistrate's taste here — because the accused resides beyond the area of this Magistrate's own jurisdiction, postponing the issue of process and either inquiring personally or directing an investigation is mandatory, not discretionary, before that distant accused can be dragged into the proceedings at all. In a different case, the offence complained of is one triable exclusively by the Court of Session. The Magistrate cannot simply direct an outside police-style investigation here — section 225(1)'s first proviso rules that out entirely. His only option is to inquire himself, and if he does, section 225(2)'s proviso obliges him to call upon the complainant to produce, and then examine on oath, every one of his witnesses — not a convenient subset.
:::

> **225. (1)** Any Magistrate, on receipt of a complaint of an offence of which he is authorised to take cognizance or which has been made over to him under section 212, [[?may]], if he thinks fit, and [[!shall]], in a case where the accused is residing at a place beyond the area in which he exercises his jurisdiction, postpone the issue of process against the accused, and either inquire into the case himself or direct an investigation to be made by a police officer or by such other person as he thinks fit, for the purpose of deciding whether or not there is sufficient ground for proceeding:
>
> *Provided that no such direction for investigation shall be made,—*
>
> *(a) where it appears to the Magistrate that the offence complained of is triable exclusively by the Court of Session; or*
>
> *(b) where the complaint has not been made by a Court, unless the complainant and the witnesses present (if any) have been examined on oath under section 223.*
>
> **(2)** In an inquiry under sub-section (1), the Magistrate [[?may]], if he thinks fit, take evidence of witnesses on oath:
>
> *Provided that if it appears to the Magistrate that the offence complained of is triable exclusively by the Court of Session, he [[!shall]] call upon the complainant to produce all his witnesses and examine them on oath.*
>
> **(3)** If an investigation under sub-section (1) is made by a person not being a police officer, he [[!shall]] have for that investigation all the powers conferred by this Sanhita on an officer in charge of a police station except the power to arrest without warrant.
>
> **226.** If, after considering the statements on oath (if any) of the complainant and of the witnesses and the result of the inquiry or investigation (if any) under section 225, the Magistrate is of opinion that there is no sufficient ground for proceeding, he [[!shall]] dismiss the complaint, and in every such case he [[!shall]] briefly record his reasons for so doing.

::: proviso Postponement is discretionary in general, mandatory in one specific circumstance
Section 225(1)'s own wording carries both a "may" and a "shall" side by side: the Magistrate *may* postpone if he thinks fit in the general run of cases, but *shall* postpone where the accused resides beyond his jurisdiction. This is not a stylistic quirk — it means an accused's distance from the Magistrate's own area converts what would otherwise be a discretionary screening step into a mandatory one.
:::

### In plain English

Section 225 is the Magistrate's screening pause between examining a complainant and actually summoning an accused. Ordinarily it's his call whether to pause and dig deeper — inquire himself, or send it out for investigation. But if the accused lives outside his own jurisdiction, that pause stops being optional; the statute requires it, so a distant accused isn't dragged into proceedings on an unexamined complaint. For offences that can only be tried by a Court of Session, the Magistrate loses the outside-investigation option entirely and, if he inquires himself instead, has to hear from every witness the complainant offers, not a convenient sample. And an investigation can never be directed at all — for a private complaint — unless the section 223 oath-examination has already happened. Section 226 is simply the honest ending to all of this: if, after everything, there still isn't sufficient ground, the complaint gets dismissed, and the Magistrate has to say briefly why.

### What changed from the CrPC

⚠️ *Provisional mapping, pending dedicated verification pass: BNSS section 225 appears to track CrPC section 202 (postponement of issue of process) and BNSS section 226 appears to track CrPC section 203 (dismissal of complaint). The mandatory-postponement trigger for an out-of-jurisdiction accused, the Sessions-exclusive-offence provisos, and the reasoned-dismissal requirement all appear substantively continued from the CrPC scheme in this reading, but the correspondence has not been independently cross-checked clause-by-clause. Flagged for verification before relying on the exact CrPC-to-BNSS correspondence in a filing.*

Section 225(1) makes postponement — and either an inquiry or a directed investigation — mandatory, not discretionary, precisely because the accused resides beyond the Magistrate's own jurisdiction. The apparent credibility of the complaint does not excuse skipping this step; the statute requires it regardless of how convincing the complaint looks on its face.

Section 225(1)'s proviso (a) specifically forbids directing an investigation where the offence complained of is exclusively triable by the Court of Session. The Magistrate's only available route in that situation is to inquire into the case himself — and if he does, section 225(2)'s proviso then requires him to call upon the complainant to produce, and examine on oath, all of his witnesses.

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

Vijay Dhanuka v. Najima Mamtaj, (2014) 14 SCC 638. The Court held that the CrPC's equivalent postponement-and-inquiry provision is mandatory, not discretionary, once it is shown the accused resides outside the area in which the Magistrate exercises jurisdiction — squarely mirroring the "shall" that section 225(1) uses for exactly that circumstance. **Practitioner takeaway**: where process has issued against an out-of-jurisdiction accused without any inquiry or investigation under section 225, that omission is not a mere irregularity — it is a defect going to the validity of the process itself.

#### Sword and shield

As a **shield**, section 225's mandatory-postponement rule protects a distant accused from being summoned into proceedings before the complaint has been screened at all. As a **sword**, the defence can challenge process issued without the required inquiry or investigation where the accused's out-of-jurisdiction residence triggered the mandatory limb of section 225(1), citing Vijay Dhanuka; the prosecution's countervailing point is that section 226 requires only that the Magistrate be satisfied there is *sufficient ground* — not proof beyond reasonable doubt — to let the complaint proceed.

#### Strategy and drafting

**Model checklist for reviewing this cluster's provisions:**

1. Before accepting that process issued validly, check the accused's residence against the Magistrate's jurisdiction — an out-of-jurisdiction accused converts postponement from discretionary to mandatory.
2. For an exclusively Sessions-triable offence, confirm no outside investigation was directed, and, if an inquiry was held instead, confirm all of the complainant's witnesses were in fact produced and examined.
3. Before treating any directed investigation as valid, confirm the section 223 oath-examination had already occurred, where the complaint was not made by a Court.
4. Where a complaint is dismissed under section 226, check the order for briefly recorded reasons — a bare dismissal without reasons does not satisfy the section.
