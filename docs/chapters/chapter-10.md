# Chapter X — Order for Maintenance of Wives, Children and Parents

## Sections 144–145 — The maintenance order itself, and the procedure for getting one

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute A summary, welfare-driven remedy that lets a Magistrate order monthly maintenance for a wife, child, or parent who cannot maintain themselves — without the delay of a full civil suit.
Section 144 is one of the highest-traffic provisions in the entire Sanhita. It exists because civil maintenance litigation is slow, and a wife, child, or parent left without means cannot wait years for a decree. The remedy is summary and welfare-oriented: a Magistrate of the first class, on proof that a person with sufficient means has neglected or refused to maintain a dependant falling into one of four defined categories, can order a monthly allowance — and, crucially, can order interim maintenance and litigation expenses while the main application is still pending, with that interim application itself subject to a sixty-day disposal target. Section 145 supplies the procedure: which district the proceedings can be brought in, how evidence is taken, and what happens if the person against whom the order is sought simply refuses to show up.
:::

::: story The interim application that could not wait years for the main hearing
A wife unable to maintain herself files an application under section 144 against a husband who has sufficient means but has stopped supporting her. The main maintenance application is contested and will take time to resolve. She also asks for interim maintenance and litigation expenses under the second proviso to section 144(1), to survive in the meantime. The third proviso answers the timing concern directly: an application for interim maintenance and expenses "shall, as far as possible, be disposed of within sixty days from the date of the service of notice of the application" — a target aimed squarely at preventing the interim relief itself from becoming another multi-year proceeding.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 144 — Order for maintenance of wives, children and parents
"144. (1) If any person having sufficient means neglects or refuses to maintain—
(a) his wife, unable to maintain herself; or
(b) his legitimate or illegitimate child, whether married or not, unable to maintain itself; or
(c) his legitimate or illegitimate child (not being a married daughter) who has attained majority, where such child is, by reason of any physical or mental abnormality or injury unable to maintain itself; or
(d) his father or mother, unable to maintain himself or herself,
a Magistrate of the first class [[?may]], upon proof of such neglect or refusal, order such person to make a monthly allowance for the maintenance of his wife or such child, father or mother, at such monthly rate as such Magistrate thinks fit and to pay the same to such person as the Magistrate may from time to time direct:
Provided that the Magistrate [[?may]] order the father of a female child referred to in clause (b) to make such allowance, until she attains her majority, if the Magistrate is satisfied that the husband of such female child, if married, is not possessed of sufficient means:
Provided further that the Magistrate [[?may]], during the pendency of the proceeding regarding monthly allowance for the maintenance under this sub-section, order such person to make a monthly allowance for the interim maintenance of his wife or such child, father or mother, and the expenses of such proceeding which the Magistrate considers reasonable, and to pay the same to such person as the Magistrate may from time to time direct:
Provided also that an application for the monthly allowance for the interim maintenance and expenses of proceeding under the second proviso [[!shall]], as far as possible, be disposed of within sixty days from the date of the service of notice of the application to such person.
Explanation.—For the purposes of this Chapter, 'wife' includes a woman who has been divorced by, or has obtained a divorce from, her husband and has not remarried.
(2) Any such allowance for the maintenance or interim maintenance and expenses of proceeding [[!shall]] be payable from the date of the order, or, if so ordered, from the date of the application for maintenance or interim maintenance and expenses of proceeding, as the case may be.
(3) If any person so ordered fails without sufficient cause to comply with the order, any such Magistrate [[?may]], for every breach of the order, issue a warrant for levying the amount due in the manner provided for levying fines, and [[?may]] sentence such person, for the whole or any part of each month's allowance for the maintenance or the interim maintenance and expenses of proceeding, as the case may be, remaining unpaid after the execution of the warrant, to imprisonment for a term which may extend to one month or until payment if sooner made:
Provided that no warrant [[!shall]] be issued for the recovery of any amount due under this section unless application be made to the Court to levy such amount within a period of one year from the date on which it became due:
Provided further that if such person offers to maintain his wife on condition of her living with him, and she refuses to live with him, such Magistrate [[?may]] consider any grounds of refusal stated by her, and [[?may]] make an order under this section notwithstanding such offer, if he is satisfied that there is just ground for so doing.
Explanation.—If a husband has contracted marriage with another woman or keeps a mistress, it shall be considered to be just ground for his wife's refusal to live with him.
(4) No wife [[!shall]] be entitled to receive an allowance for the maintenance or the interim maintenance and expenses of proceeding, from her husband under this section if she is living in adultery, or if, without any sufficient reason, she refuses to live with her husband, or if they are living separately by mutual consent.
(5) On proof that any wife in whose favour an order has been made under this section is living in adultery, or that without sufficient reason she refuses to live with her husband, or that they are living separately by mutual consent, the Magistrate [[!shall]] cancel the order."
:::

::: proviso Section 145 — Procedure
"145. (1) Proceedings under section 144 [[?may]] be taken against any person in any district—
(a) where he is; or
(b) where he or his wife resides; or
(c) where he last resided with his wife, or as the case may be, with the mother of the illegitimate child; or
(d) where his father or mother resides.
(2) All evidence in such proceedings [[!shall]] be taken in the presence of the person against whom an order for payment of maintenance is proposed to be made, or, when his personal attendance is dispensed with, in the presence of his advocate, and [[!shall]] be recorded in the manner prescribed for summons-cases:
Provided that if the Magistrate is satisfied that the person against whom an order for payment of maintenance is proposed to be made is wilfully avoiding service, or wilfully neglecting to attend the Court, the Magistrate [[?may]] proceed to hear and determine the case ex parte and any order so made may be set aside for good cause shown on an application made within three months from the date thereof subject to such terms including terms as to payment of costs to the opposite party as the Magistrate may think just and proper.
(3) The Court in dealing with applications under section 144 [[!shall]] have power to make such order as to costs as may be just."
:::

### In plain English

Section 144 is deliberately built for speed and welfare rather than doctrinal precision — it is not a wealth-transfer mechanism modelled on inheritance or contract, but a floor beneath dependants who genuinely cannot support themselves. The four categories are exhaustive: a wife unable to maintain herself (with the Explanation extending "wife" to a still-unremarried divorced woman), a minor child (legitimate or illegitimate), an adult child unable to maintain itself because of physical or mental abnormality or injury, and a parent unable to maintain himself or herself. The two later provisos to section 144(1) matter as much as the main power itself: interim maintenance exists precisely because a contested main application can take months or years, and the sixty-day disposal target on the interim application is the section's own acknowledgment that even interim relief can become slow enough to defeat its purpose if left unchecked. Section 144(3)'s enforcement mechanism treats the unpaid amount like a fine to be levied by warrant, backed by a genuinely coercive but capped sanction — up to one month's imprisonment per default, or until payment, whichever comes first — subject to a one-year limitation on applying to levy any particular default. Section 144(4) and (5) are the section's built-in limits on a wife's entitlement, tied to conduct (adultery, unreasonable refusal to live with the husband, mutual-consent separation) rather than need, and the Explanation to section 144(3)'s second proviso deliberately tips the balance back toward the wife where the husband's own conduct — bigamy or keeping a mistress — is the real reason for her refusal to return. Section 145 supplies the procedural scaffolding: proceedings can be brought in any of four possible districts (a genuinely wide venue choice favouring the dependant), evidence must ordinarily be taken in the presence of the respondent or his advocate and recorded as in a summons-case, and the ex parte proviso exists as a safety valve against a respondent who simply refuses to engage with the process — balanced by the three-month window to have such an order set aside for good cause.

The core structure of the presumed CrPC ancestor provision appears preserved, with one addition of real practical significance: the second and third provisos to section 144(1), introducing an explicit interim-maintenance-and-expenses power together with a sixty-day disposal target for applications seeking it. If the presumed CrPC ancestor did not contain an equivalent express interim-maintenance mechanism with a disposal timeline, this would represent a genuine substantive addition rather than a renumbering, and should be flagged and confirmed independently before being relied upon as a point of departure in any filing.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct The interim application that sat for eight months
A wife applies for interim maintenance and litigation expenses under the second proviso to section 144(1). Notice is served on the husband, but the application remains undecided eight months later. She asks whether the delay itself gives her any right to relief.
::: ruling What actually happens
The third proviso to section 144(1) sets a disposal target of sixty days from service of notice, using the language "as far as possible" — a directory, not an absolute, timeline. Its breach does not by itself entitle the applicant to any specific automatic relief or invalidate the eventual order; what it does give her is a concrete basis to press the Court, and, where the delay is unreasonable, a ground to seek expedited disposal through supervisory remedies. The sixty-day figure is best used as leverage for urgency, not as a self-executing entitlement.
:::
::::

:::: instinct The husband who blamed his wife for refusing to return
A husband, proceeded against under section 144, offers to maintain his wife on condition that she return to live with him. She refuses, citing his having kept a mistress. He argues her refusal disentitles her under section 144(4).
::: ruling What actually happens
The Explanation to the second proviso of section 144(3) answers this directly: if a husband has contracted marriage with another woman or keeps a mistress, that is deemed just ground for the wife's refusal to live with him. Her refusal on this specific ground does not trigger the section 144(4) disqualification; if anything, the Magistrate is directed to weigh her stated ground and may still make the order despite the husband's offer, precisely because the offer's good faith is itself in question.

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

::: info Verify before citing
**Case:** *Rajnesh* v. *Neha*, reported at (2021) 2 SCC 324 (Supreme Court of India, two-judge bench).
**Facts (one line):** A protracted, multi-forum maintenance dispute prompted the Supreme Court to lay down uniform guidelines addressing overlapping maintenance claims across different statutes, disclosure of assets, and the quantum and date of commencement of maintenance.
**Ratio:** The Court directed that a party seeking or resisting maintenance must file a comprehensive affidavit of disclosure of assets and liabilities in a prescribed format, that overlapping maintenance orders across different proceedings must be accounted for and adjusted against each other, and that maintenance should ordinarily be awarded from the date of the application rather than the date of the order, absent special reasons.
**Practitioner takeaway:** "File the disclosure affidavit as a matter of course in every maintenance proceeding, and seek maintenance expressly from the date of application, not the date of order, unless the facts genuinely call for a later start date."
This citation is drawn from established legal knowledge and is flagged for independent verification against the official reporter before use in any filing, per house citation-honesty rules.
:::

#### Sword and shield

As a **sword**, a dependant within one of section 144(1)'s four categories can secure both final and interim maintenance without waiting for a civil decree, and can invoke section 144(3)'s warrant-and-imprisonment mechanism the moment a defaulting respondent fails to pay without sufficient cause. As a **shield**, a respondent can resist an interim or final order by disproving "sufficient means" or the underlying neglect or refusal, and — specifically against a wife applicant — can raise the section 144(4) disqualifications (adultery, unreasonable refusal to live together, mutual-consent separation), though the Explanation to section 144(3)'s second proviso cuts back that shield sharply where the husband's own bigamy or keeping a mistress is the real cause of the estrangement.

#### The limitation clock

Section 144(3)'s first proviso bars a warrant for levying any particular unpaid amount unless the application to levy it is made within one year from the date that amount became due — a rolling, per-default limitation, not a single limitation running from the original order. The third proviso to section 144(1) supplies the sixty-day directory disposal target on interim-maintenance applications, and section 145(2)'s proviso gives a three-month window to apply to set aside an ex parte order for good cause shown.

#### Interlocking matrix

| Situation | Governing provision | What follows |
|---|---|---|
| Neglect or refusal to maintain a qualifying dependant, sufficient means shown | Section 144(1) | Monthly maintenance allowance ordered |
| Main application pending, immediate need shown | Section 144(1), second and third provisos | Interim maintenance and expenses, targeted for disposal within sixty days of service |
| Default in complying with an order without sufficient cause | Section 144(3) | Warrant to levy as a fine; imprisonment up to one month or until payment, within the one-year window on each default |
| Wife's adultery, unreasonable refusal to live together, or mutual-consent separation | Section 144(4)–(5) | Disentitlement, and cancellation of an existing order on proof |
| Respondent wilfully avoiding service or neglecting to attend | Section 145(2) proviso | Ex parte hearing and order, settable aside within three months for good cause |

#### Strategy and drafting

**Drafting traps.**

1. Do not plead a section 144 application generically — identify precisely which of the four section 144(1) categories the dependant falls into, since the section's structure and the "unable to maintain" standard differ across them.
2. Do not overlook the interim-maintenance provisos when urgency is genuine — a bare final-relief application forces the dependant to wait out the entire main proceeding without support.
3. For a wife respondent facing a section 144(4) disqualification argument built on her refusal to live with the husband, always check whether the Explanation to section 144(3)'s second proviso (bigamy or keeping a mistress) supplies a complete answer before conceding the point.

**Timing.** Seek maintenance expressly from the date of the application rather than the date of the order at the outset, and press for expedited disposal of any interim-maintenance application once the sixty-day directory target under the third proviso to section 144(1) has been exceeded without adequate explanation.

**Model prayer checklist for a section 144 application:**

1. State precisely which section 144(1) category the dependant falls into, and plead the facts establishing "unable to maintain" for that category specifically.
2. Plead the respondent's sufficient means, with particulars.
3. Where urgency exists, expressly pray for interim maintenance and litigation expenses under the second proviso to section 144(1), separately from the final relief.
4. Pray for maintenance from the date of the application, not the date of the order.
5. Where applicable, anticipate and pre-empt a section 144(4) defence by pleading facts (such as the Explanation to section 144(3)'s second proviso) that answer it in advance.

## Sections 146–147 — Changing the allowance later, and making sure the order actually gets enforced

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Maintenance orders are not frozen in time — circumstances change, and section 146 lets the amount be revisited, while section 147 makes sure the paperwork itself travels with the order wherever enforcement is needed.
A section 144 order is not a one-time, unappealable settlement of the question. Section 146 lets the Magistrate revisit the allowance — up or down — on proof of a change in circumstances, lets a civil court's later decision override an inconsistent section 144 order, and sets out precise rules for when a divorced woman's order must be cancelled (remarriage, receipt of a customary or personal-law divorce settlement, or her own voluntary surrender of maintenance rights). Section 147 is comparatively mechanical but practically indispensable: it guarantees a free copy of the order to the person entitled to it, and confirms that the order can be enforced by any Magistrate, anywhere the person against whom it is made happens to be found — not only in the court that made it.
:::

::: story The divorced woman who had already settled the money question once
A woman is divorced by her husband. Under the personal law applicable to the parties, he pays her, at the time of the divorce, the entire lump sum she was entitled to on divorce. A section 144 maintenance order made in her favour after the divorce is then challenged by the husband, who argues the order should be cancelled given the settlement already paid. Section 146(3)(b) supplies the precise mechanism: where the woman has received, whether before or after the date of the section 144 order, the whole sum payable to her under the applicable customary or personal law on divorce, the Magistrate must cancel the order — and if the sum was paid before the order was made, the cancellation runs from the date the order was made itself, not from some later date.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 146 — Alteration in allowance
"146. (1) On proof of a change in the circumstances of any person, receiving, under section 144 a monthly allowance for the maintenance or interim maintenance, or ordered under the same section to pay a monthly allowance for the maintenance, or interim maintenance, to his wife, child, father or mother, as the case may be, the Magistrate [[?may]] make such alteration, as he thinks fit, in the allowance for the maintenance or the interim maintenance, as the case may be.
(2) Where it appears to the Magistrate that in consequence of any decision of a competent Civil Court, any order made under section 144 should be cancelled or varied, he [[!shall]] cancel the order or, as the case may be, vary the same accordingly.
(3) Where any order has been made under section 144 in favour of a woman who has been divorced by, or has obtained a divorce from, her husband, the Magistrate [[!shall]], if he is satisfied that—
(a) the woman has, after the date of such divorce, remarried, cancel such order as from the date of her remarriage;
(b) the woman has been divorced by her husband and that she has received, whether before or after the date of the said order, the whole of the sum which, under any customary or personal law applicable to the parties, was payable on such divorce, cancel such order,—
(i) in the case where such sum was paid before such order, from the date on which such order was made;
(ii) in any other case, from the date of expiry of the period, if any, for which maintenance has been actually paid by the husband to the woman;
(c) the woman has obtained a divorce from her husband and that she had voluntarily surrendered her rights to maintenance or interim maintenance, as the case may be, after her divorce, cancel the order from the date thereof.
(4) At the time of making any decree for the recovery of any maintenance or dowry by any person, to whom a monthly allowance for the maintenance and interim maintenance or any of them has been ordered to be paid under section 144, the Civil Court [[!shall]] take into account the sum which has been paid to, or recovered by, such person as monthly allowance for the maintenance and interim maintenance or any of them, as the case may be, in pursuance of the said order."
:::

::: proviso Section 147 — Enforcement of order of maintenance
"147. A copy of the order of maintenance or interim maintenance and expenses of proceedings, as the case may be, [[!shall]] be given without payment to the person in whose favour it is made, or to his guardian, if any, or to the person to whom the allowance for the maintenance or the allowance for the interim maintenance and expenses of proceeding, as the case may be, is to be paid; and such order [[?may]] be enforced by any Magistrate in any place where the person against whom it is made may be, on such Magistrate being satisfied as to the identity of the parties and the non-payment of the allowance, or as the case may be, expenses, due."
:::

### In plain English

Section 146 recognises that a maintenance order fixes an amount based on circumstances as they stood at one moment, and those circumstances do not stay fixed — the payer's means might improve or collapse, the recipient's needs might change, and a civil court might later decide something (a divorce, a property settlement) that makes the existing criminal-court order obsolete or wrong. Subsection (1) is the general, open-textured power to alter the allowance on proof of changed circumstances; subsection (2) is narrower and mandatory — once a competent civil court's decision is inconsistent with the section 144 order, the Magistrate has no real discretion left, the order must be cancelled or varied to match. Subsection (3) is the most granular part of the section, dealing specifically with divorced women, and each of its three cancellation grounds fixes its own effective date with care: remarriage cancels from the date of remarriage itself; a full customary or personal-law divorce settlement cancels either from the date of the section 144 order (if the settlement was already paid before that order) or from the date maintenance last stopped being actually paid (if the settlement came later); and a voluntary surrender of maintenance rights cancels from the date of that surrender. Subsection (4) closes a potential double-recovery gap by requiring a civil court, when later decreeing maintenance or dowry, to account for whatever has already been paid or recovered under the section 144 order. Section 147, by contrast, is not about changing anything — it is about making sure the order, once made, actually works in practice: a free certified copy for the person it protects, and portable enforcement by any Magistrate wherever the paying person is actually found, so that a respondent cannot defeat the order simply by moving outside the jurisdiction of the court that made it.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct The lump-sum settlement paid after the maintenance order, not before
A woman divorced by her husband obtains a section 144 maintenance order in her favour. Two years later, under the personal law applicable to the parties, her husband pays her the full lump-sum settlement due on divorce. He then seeks cancellation of the maintenance order from its original date, arguing the settlement covers everything.
::: ruling What actually happens
Section 146(3)(b)(ii) governs this exact sequence: where the settlement sum is paid after the section 144 order (rather than before it), cancellation runs "from the date of expiry of the period, if any, for which maintenance has been actually paid by the husband to the woman" — not from the date of the original order. The husband cannot retroactively erase two years of maintenance already due and paid under the order simply because a settlement was later paid; the order's cancellation only takes effect going forward from where actual payment left off.
:::
::::

:::: instinct The order enforced far from the court that made it
A section 144 maintenance order is made by a Magistrate in one district. The person against whom it is made later moves to a distant city, entirely outside that Magistrate's jurisdiction, and stops paying. The person entitled to the maintenance asks whether she must return to the original court to enforce it.
::: ruling What actually happens
Section 147 does not require that. The order may be enforced "by any Magistrate in any place where the person against whom it is made may be" — meaning the recipient can approach a Magistrate in the city where the defaulting person is now actually found, once that Magistrate is satisfied as to the identity of the parties and the fact of non-payment. The order's enforceability travels with the defaulting person; it is not tied to the geography of the original court.

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

::: info Research gap — no case promoted
No single Supreme Court authority is promoted to a winning ratio for this cluster. Sections 146 and 147 are more frequently applied in individual, fact-specific alteration, cancellation, and enforcement proceedings than governed by a single generally applicable Supreme Court ratio.
:::

#### Sword and shield

As a **sword**, a recipient whose circumstances have worsened can seek an upward alteration under section 146(1), and can enforce an existing order in any place the defaulting payer is actually found under section 147, without needing to return to the originating court. As a **shield**, a payer whose means have genuinely diminished can seek a downward alteration under section 146(1), and — where the recipient is a divorced woman — can invoke any of the three section 146(3) cancellation grounds (remarriage, full settlement received, voluntary surrender) to bring an existing order to an end, with the precise effective date fixed by whichever clause applies.

#### The limitation clock

Neither section 146 nor section 147 imposes a fixed limitation period of its own; both operate on the fact of a triggering event (a change in circumstances, a civil court decision, remarriage, receipt of a settlement, voluntary surrender, or simply locating the defaulting payer) rather than on a running clock. The one-year limitation on levying any particular default under section 144(3)'s first proviso remains the operative timing constraint once an order is being enforced under section 147.

#### Strategy and drafting

**Drafting traps.**

1. Do not plead a section 146(3) cancellation ground generically — identify precisely which of clauses (a), (b), or (c) applies, since each fixes a different effective date for cancellation.
2. Do not assume a section 146(3)(b) settlement automatically cancels the order from its original date — check whether the settlement was paid before or after the section 144 order, since the two sub-clauses point to different dates.
3. Do not require a client to return to the originating court to enforce an order against a payer who has since moved — section 147 allows enforcement wherever the payer is actually found.

**Timing.** Raise a section 146(2) variation application promptly once an inconsistent civil court decision becomes final, since the Magistrate's obligation to cancel or vary is not discretionary once that condition is met.

**Model checklist for a section 146 alteration or section 147 enforcement application:**

1. Identify precisely which sub-section or clause of section 146 is being invoked, and plead the specific triggering fact for that clause.
2. For a section 146(3)(b) settlement-based cancellation, plead whether the settlement was paid before or after the section 144 order, to fix the correct effective date.
3. For section 147 enforcement, plead the current location of the person against whom the order was made, and be prepared to establish identity and non-payment before the enforcing Magistrate.
