# Chapter XXXIII — Transfer of Criminal Cases

## Sections 446–447 — The Supreme Court's and the High Court's power to move a case out of its ordinary court

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Not every case stays with the court it started in. This cluster gives the two highest courts in the ordinary hierarchy a power to move a pending case or appeal somewhere else entirely, for reasons that go well beyond simple convenience. Section 446 is the Supreme Court's transfer power — it can move a case or appeal from one High Court to another, or between subordinate criminal courts under different High Courts, but only where this is expedient for the ends of justice, and only on the application of the Attorney-General of India or an interested party, by motion, generally supported by affidavit. Section 447 is the High Court's own, considerably more detailed transfer power, triggered by any of three grounds — a fair and impartial trial being impossible in a subordinate court, a genuinely difficult question of law likely to arise, or an order being required by the Sanhita, convenient for the parties, or otherwise expedient for justice — and it can act on a lower court's report, a party's application, or its own initiative, subject to a proviso requiring a first attempt before the Sessions Judge for same-division transfers, notice and waiting-period safeguards for accused-initiated applications, a stay power over the subordinate court pending disposal, and a compensation sanction against frivolous or vexatious applications at both the Supreme Court and High Court level.
:::

::: story A trial the accused says cannot be fair where it stands
An accused facing trial in a particular district believes, with good reason, that local circumstances make a fair and impartial trial there genuinely impossible — perhaps intense local hostility connected to the alleged offence. The accused applies to the High Court under section 447(1)(a). Because the application is made by an accused person, section 447(4) lets the High Court require a bond for any compensation it might later award, and section 447(5) requires the accused to give the Public Prosecutor written notice with a copy of the grounds, with a mandatory twenty-four-hour gap before the merits are heard. If the High Court, having heard the matter, is satisfied the application was actually frivolous or vexatious, section 447(7) lets it order the accused to compensate whoever opposed the application. In a separate, larger case, a question of unusual legal difficulty appears likely to arise in a trial pending before a subordinate court, and — without waiting for anyone to apply — the High Court decides under section 447(1)(b) and section 447(2) to act on its own initiative and transfer the case to itself, then trying it under the same procedure the original court would have used, per section 447(8).
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 446 — Power of Supreme Court to transfer cases and appeals
"446. (1) Whenever it is made to appear to the Supreme Court that an order under this section is expedient for the ends of justice, it [[?may]] direct that any particular case or appeal be transferred from one High Court to another High Court or from a Criminal Court subordinate to one High Court to another Criminal Court of equal or superior jurisdiction subordinate to another High Court.
(2) The Supreme Court [[?may]] act under this section only on the application of the Attorney-General of India or of a party interested, and every such application [[!shall]] be made by motion, which [[!shall]], except when the applicant is the Attorney-General of India or the Advocate-General of the State, be supported by affidavit or affirmation.
(3) Where any application for the exercise of the powers conferred by this section is dismissed, the Supreme Court [[?may]], if it is of opinion that the application was frivolous or vexatious, order the applicant to pay by way of compensation to any person who has opposed the application such sum as it [[?may]] consider appropriate in the circumstances of the case."
:::

::: proviso Section 447 — Power of High Court to transfer cases and appeals
"447. (1) Whenever it is made to appear to the High Court—
(a) that a fair and impartial inquiry or trial cannot be had in any Criminal Court subordinate thereto; or
(b) that some question of law of unusual difficulty is likely to arise; or
(c) that an order under this section is required by any provision of this Sanhita, or will tend to the general convenience of the parties or witnesses, or is expedient for the ends of justice,
it [[?may]] order—
(i) that any offence be inquired into or tried by any Court not qualified under sections 197 to 205 (both inclusive), but in other respects competent to inquire into or try such offence;
(ii) that any particular case or appeal, or class of cases or appeals, be transferred from a Criminal Court subordinate to its authority to any other such Criminal Court of equal or superior jurisdiction;
(iii) that any particular case be committed for trial to a Court of Session; or
(iv) that any particular case or appeal be transferred to and tried before itself.
(2) The High Court [[?may]] act either on the report of the lower Court, or on the application of a party interested, or on its own initiative:
Provided that no application [[!shall]] lie to the High Court for transferring a case from one Criminal Court to another Criminal Court in the same sessions division, unless an application for such transfer has been made to the Sessions Judge and rejected by him.
(3) Every application for an order under sub-section (1) [[!shall]] be made by motion, which [[!shall]], except when the applicant is the Advocate-General of the State, be supported by affidavit or affirmation.
(4) When such application is made by an accused person, the High Court [[?may]] direct him to execute a bond or bail bond for the payment of any compensation which the High Court [[?may]] award under sub-section (7).
(5) Every accused person making such application [[!shall]] give to the Public Prosecutor notice in writing of the application, together with a copy of the grounds on which it is made; and no order [[!shall]] be made on the merits of the application unless at least twenty-four hours have elapsed between the giving of such notice and the hearing of the application.
(6) Where the application is for the transfer of a case or appeal from any subordinate Court, the High Court [[?may]], if it is satisfied that it is necessary so to do in the interest of justice, order that, pending the disposal of the application the proceedings in the subordinate Court [[!shall]] be stayed, on such terms as the High Court [[?may]] think fit to impose:
Provided that such stay [[!shall]] not affect the subordinate Court's power of remand under section 346.
(7) Where an application for an order under sub-section (1) is dismissed, the High Court [[?may]], if it is of opinion that the application was frivolous or vexatious, order the applicant to pay by way of compensation to any person who has opposed the application such sum as it [[?may]] consider proper in the circumstances of the case.
(8) When the High Court orders under sub-section (1) that a case be transferred from any Court for trial before itself, it [[!shall]] observe in such trial the same procedure which that Court would have observed if the case had not been so transferred.
(9) Nothing in this section [[!shall]] be deemed to affect any order of the Government under section 218."
:::

### In plain English

Section 446 and section 447 both answer the same underlying question -- can a pending case be moved somewhere else -- but at two different altitudes. Section 446 is narrow and high-level: only the Supreme Court can use it, only for genuinely cross-High-Court moves, only on a proper application by the Attorney-General or an interested party, and only where it is expedient for the ends of justice. Section 447 is the High Court's own, much more elaborate toolkit, and it can be triggered in three distinct ways: a fair trial being genuinely impossible where the case currently sits, a real question of unusual legal difficulty on the horizon, or simply that transfer is required by the Sanhita, convenient for everyone involved, or otherwise in the interests of justice. The High Court does not need anyone to ask -- it can act on a lower court's report, a party's application, or purely on its own initiative -- though a same-division transfer has to go through the Sessions Judge first and be rejected there before the High Court will even entertain it. Because an accused person applying for transfer is effectively saying the trial court itself cannot be trusted, the Sanhita builds in real safeguards around that specific situation: notice to the prosecution, a mandatory waiting period, a possible bond requirement, and a genuine risk of having to pay compensation if the application turns out to be frivolous. And once the High Court does pull a case to itself, section 447(8) makes clear it is not starting from scratch procedurally -- it tries the case exactly as the original court would have.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct An accused applies to the High Court under section 447 for transfer of the trial to another district, alleging that a fair trial cannot be had locally. The accused files the application but does not give the Public Prosecutor any notice of it, hoping for a quicker hearing. Can the High Court decide the application on its merits at the first hearing?

::: ruling No. Section 447(5) requires every accused person making such an application to give the Public Prosecutor written notice of the application together with a copy of the grounds, and expressly bars any order on the merits until at least twenty-four hours have elapsed between giving that notice and the hearing. Skipping notice to save time does not work -- it simply means the High Court cannot yet decide the merits, and the accused will need to comply with section 447(5) before the application can be heard substantively.
:::
::::

:::: instinct A party applies to the Supreme Court under section 446 for transfer of a case from a Criminal Court subordinate to one High Court to a Criminal Court subordinate to a different High Court, but the application is made not by the Attorney-General of India and not supported by any affidavit or affirmation. Is the application competent?

::: ruling No, as filed. Section 446(2) restricts who may set the Supreme Court's power in motion -- only the Attorney-General of India or a party interested -- and requires every such application to be made by motion and, except where the applicant is the Attorney-General of India or the Advocate-General of the State, to be supported by affidavit or affirmation. An interested party's application without the required affidavit or affirmation does not satisfy section 446(2), and the defect would need to be cured (by filing a proper affidavit or affirmation) before the Supreme Court can act on the application.
:::

#### The winning ratio

::: info Research gap — no case promoted

The CrPC's substantial body of case law on transfer petitions -- particularly on what constitutes a reasonable apprehension that a fair trial cannot be had -- remains substantively relevant, but every citation must be independently re-verified against the BNSS's renumbered provisions before it is pleaded in any court.
:::

#### Sword and shield

For an **accused** seeking transfer under section 447(1)(a), build the application around concrete, specific facts supporting the apprehension of an unfair trial -- generalised or speculative claims invite both dismissal and, potentially, a frivolous-application compensation order under section 447(7). For a party **opposing** a transfer application, section 447(7)'s compensation sanction is worth raising affirmatively where the application genuinely appears frivolous or vexatious, not merely as an afterthought after the substantive opposition. For any party invoking the **Supreme Court's** section 446 power, remember that standing is narrow -- confirm the applicant is either the Attorney-General of India or a genuinely interested party before investing in the application's substance.

#### The limitation clock

Neither section prescribes a limitation period of its own; a transfer application may typically be made at any point while the case or appeal remains pending in the court sought to be bypassed. The real time-sensitivity here is procedural rather than limitation-based: section 447(5)'s mandatory twenty-four-hour notice period must be observed before an accused-initiated application can be heard on the merits, and this should be factored into any hearing-date planning.

- **Section 448 (this Chapter, cluster 2)** — the Sessions Judge's own, narrower transfer power within a single sessions division, which section 447(2)'s proviso requires an accused to exhaust first for same-division transfers before approaching the High Court.

#### Strategy and drafting

- Before filing a section 447 application on the same-sessions-division ground, confirm and document that an application was actually made to, and rejected by, the Sessions Judge first -- section 447(2)'s proviso makes this a hard precondition, not a preferred practice.
- When drafting an accused-initiated section 447 application, build in the section 447(5) notice-and-affidavit requirements from the outset, including serving the Public Prosecutor and calendaring the twenty-four-hour waiting period, to avoid an avoidable procedural delay at the first hearing.
- Where a genuine risk of an unfair trial exists, consider requesting a section 447(6) stay of the subordinate court's proceedings pending disposal of the transfer application, but be precise that the stay does not touch the subordinate court's power of remand under section 346.
- Before filing any transfer application on thin grounds, weigh the real financial exposure under section 446(3) or section 447(7) if the application is found frivolous or vexatious -- these are not merely theoretical sanctions.

## Sections 448–449 — The Sessions Judge's own power to move a case within the division, and to take back a case or appeal already handed down

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Not every transfer decision needs to go up to the High Court. Section 448 gives a Sessions Judge a parallel, narrower transfer power confined to their own sessions division — moving a particular case from one criminal court to another within that division, again wherever expedient for the ends of justice, and largely following the High Court's own procedural safeguards from section 447 by cross-reference, with one specific adjustment: the compensation cap for a frivolous or vexatious application before a Sessions Judge is capped at ten thousand rupees, rather than left open-ended as under section 447(7). Section 449 is a different kind of power entirely — not transferring a case to a different court's jurisdiction, but letting the Sessions Judge take back a case or appeal that has already been made over to a Chief Judicial Magistrate or an Additional Sessions Judge, subject to a hard timing limit for the Additional Sessions Judge scenario: the recall must happen before the trial or hearing has actually commenced before that Additional Sessions Judge.
:::

::: story A Sessions Judge who changes his mind before the trial starts
A Sessions Judge has made over a case to an Additional Sessions Judge for trial. Before that trial has actually commenced, the Sessions Judge — for reasons of docket management, or a reassessment of which judge should hear the matter — decides to recall the case. Section 449(2) allows exactly this, but only because the trial has not yet begun before the Additional Sessions Judge; had it already started, the recall power under this specific sub-section would no longer be available. Having recalled the case, section 449(3) gives the Sessions Judge the choice to try it personally, or to make it over again to another court in accordance with the Sanhita. In a separate matter, a party in the same sessions division applies to the Sessions Judge (not the High Court) for transfer of a case from one Magistrate's court to another within the division, on the footing that transfer is expedient for the ends of justice; section 448 lets the Sessions Judge grant this directly, without the matter ever needing to go up to the High Court at all.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 448 — Power of Sessions Judge to transfer cases and appeals
"448. (1) Whenever it is made to appear to a Sessions Judge that an order under this sub-section is expedient for the ends of justice, he [[?may]] order that any particular case be transferred from one Criminal Court to another Criminal Court in his sessions division.
(2) The Sessions Judge [[?may]] act either on the report of the lower Court, or on the application of a party interested, or on his own initiative.
(3) The provisions of sub-sections (3), (4), (5), (6), (7) and (9) of section 447 [[!shall]] apply in relation to an application to the Sessions Judge for an order under sub-section (1) as they apply in relation to an application to the High Court for an order under sub-section (1) of section 447, except that sub-section (7) of that section [[!shall]] so apply as if for the word 'sum' occurring therein, the words 'sum not exceeding ten thousand rupees' were substituted."
:::

::: proviso Section 449 — Withdrawal of cases and appeals by Sessions Judges
"449. (1) A Sessions Judge [[?may]] withdraw any case or appeal from, or recall any case or appeal which he has made over to a Chief Judicial Magistrate subordinate to him.
(2) At any time before the trial of the case or the hearing of the appeal has commenced before the Additional Sessions Judge, a Sessions Judge [[?may]] recall any case or appeal which he has made over to any Additional Sessions Judge.
(3) Where a Sessions Judge withdraws or recalls case or appeal under sub-section (1) or sub-section (2), he [[?may]] either try the case in his own Court or hear the appeal himself, or make it over in accordance with the provisions of this Sanhita to another Court for trial or hearing, as the case may be."
:::

### In plain English

Section 448 is essentially section 447 scaled down to division level — same basic trigger (expedient for the ends of justice), same flexible ways of getting the matter moving (report, application, or the Sessions Judge's own initiative), and largely the same procedural safeguards imported wholesale by cross-reference, with one deliberate adjustment: if an application before the Sessions Judge turns out to be frivolous or vexatious, the compensation that can be ordered is capped at ten thousand rupees, rather than left open-ended as it is at the High Court level. Section 449 is a genuinely different power — not about moving a case to a new court's jurisdiction, but about a Sessions Judge taking back something already handed off. Handing a case to a Chief Judicial Magistrate can be undone at any point under sub-section (1); handing a case to an Additional Sessions Judge can only be undone under sub-section (2) before that judge has actually started the trial or hearing — once that has happened, the recall window under this specific power has closed. Either way, once the Sessions Judge has withdrawn or recalled the matter, sub-section (3) gives a clean choice: hear it personally, or send it back out to wherever the Sanhita says it should properly go.

### What changed from the CrPC

::: info Verify before citing
BNSS sections 448–449 are provisionally mapped to CrPC sections 408–409 (CrPC Chapter XXXI, "Transfer of Criminal Cases"). This mapping is unverified against the CrPC bare-act text and must be independently confirmed before use in any filing.
:::

The Sessions Judge's division-level transfer power (with the imported High Court safeguards and the substituted compensation cap) and the two-tier withdrawal/recall power over cases made over to a Chief Judicial Magistrate versus an Additional Sessions Judge both read as continuations of the presumed CrPC scheme. The specific figure of ten thousand rupees in section 448(3) reads as a plausible inflation-era update to whatever monetary cap the CrPC's predecessor provision carried, but this should be treated as a flagged hypothesis, not a confirmed comparison, until the CrPC's actual figure is independently checked.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct A Sessions Judge, acting under section 448, transfers a case from one Magistrate's court to another within the division on his own initiative, without any report from the lower court or any application from a party. Is this a valid exercise of the power?

::: ruling Yes. Section 448(2) expressly allows the Sessions Judge to act either on the report of the lower court, or on the application of a party interested, or on his own initiative -- all three are independently sufficient triggers, and none is a precondition for the others. A transfer ordered purely on the Sessions Judge's own initiative, without any report or application, is squarely within the power as long as the section 448(1) 'expedient for the ends of justice' standard is genuinely met.
:::
::::

:::: instinct A Sessions Judge made over an appeal to an Additional Sessions Judge, and the hearing of that appeal has already begun before the Additional Sessions Judge. The Sessions Judge, dissatisfied with how the hearing is proceeding, now wants to recall the appeal under section 449(2). Can the Sessions Judge do so?

::: ruling No. Section 449(2) permits recall of a case or appeal made over to an Additional Sessions Judge only at any time before the trial of the case or the hearing of the appeal has commenced before that Additional Sessions Judge -- once the hearing has actually begun, this specific recall power is no longer available. The Sessions Judge's dissatisfaction with the proceedings, however well-founded, does not reopen a recall window that section 449(2) has already closed by the hearing having commenced.
:::

#### Sword and shield

For a party seeking a **same-division** transfer, section 448 is generally the faster, more accessible route than going to the High Court, and section 447(2)'s proviso (imported via section 447's cross-reference from Chapter XXXIII cluster 1) in fact requires this route be tried first before the High Court will even entertain the same request. For a party wanting to **prevent** a recall of a case from an Additional Sessions Judge, the clean, bright-line defence is the commencement of trial or hearing -- once that has genuinely happened, section 449(2)'s window has closed as a matter of law, not merely of discretion.

#### The limitation clock

Neither section imposes a filing-limitation period; the operative time constraint in this cluster is section 449(2)'s substantive cutoff (recall must occur before trial or hearing commences before the Additional Sessions Judge), which functions as a hard jurisdictional boundary on the power itself rather than a procedural filing deadline.

- **Section 447 (this Chapter, cluster 1)** — supplies, by cross-reference in section 448(3), the procedural safeguards (application form, accused-applicant notice, stay power, frivolous-application sanction) that govern a Sessions Judge-level transfer application, subject to the substituted ten-thousand-rupee compensation cap.
- **Section 450 (this Chapter, cluster 3)** — the parallel, Magistrate-level withdrawal power exercised by a Chief Judicial Magistrate, structurally similar to section 449(1) but one tier down the hierarchy.
- **Section 452 (this Chapter, cluster 3)** — the mandatory reasons-recording duty that attaches to every order made under section 448 or section 449.

#### Strategy and drafting

- Before approaching the High Court for a same-division transfer, confirm the section 448 route before the Sessions Judge has actually been tried and failed -- skipping this step risks the application being rejected outright at the High Court on the section 447(2) proviso alone.

- In advising a client on the financial exposure of a frivolous section 448 application, note the ten-thousand-rupee cap explicitly -- it is materially different from the open-ended exposure under an equivalent High Court application, and clients should be advised accordingly.

## Sections 450–452 — Withdrawal of cases by Magistrates, and the duty to explain every transfer-related order

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute This final cluster pushes the withdrawal-and-recall logic of section 449 down to the Magistrate level, in two distinct tracks, and then closes the whole Chapter with a single accountability rule that applies across all of it. Section 450(1) lets a Chief Judicial Magistrate withdraw a case from, or recall a case made over to, a subordinate Magistrate, and then either handle it personally or send it to another competent Magistrate. Section 450(2) gives a narrower, self-referential power to a Judicial Magistrate: recalling a case that Magistrate had earlier made over to a different Magistrate under section 212(2). Section 451 covers the Executive Magistrate track — a District Magistrate or Sub-divisional Magistrate can make over a proceeding to a subordinate Magistrate for disposal, and can equally withdraw or recall it, disposing of it personally or sending it elsewhere. Section 452 is the accountability backstop for the entire Chapter's Magistrate-level powers: any order made under section 448, 449, 450, or 451 must come with recorded reasons.
:::

::: story A Chief Judicial Magistrate taking a case back to redistribute the caseload
A Chief Judicial Magistrate had earlier made over a particular case to a subordinate Magistrate. Recognising that a different Magistrate is better positioned to handle it — perhaps due to case complexity or docket balance — the Chief Judicial Magistrate withdraws the case under section 450(1) and refers it to another competent Magistrate for inquiry or trial. Because this is an order under section 450, section 452 requires the Chief Judicial Magistrate to record reasons for making it — a bare withdrawal order without any stated basis would not satisfy this requirement. In a separate district, a District Magistrate has a proceeding pending before him and decides, under section 451(a), to make it over to a Magistrate subordinate to him for disposal; later, dissatisfied with the pace of that disposal, the District Magistrate withdraws it back under section 451(b) and disposes of the matter personally — again, with the same section 452 duty to record reasons attaching to that withdrawal order.
:::

(2) Any Judicial Magistrate [[?may]] recall any case made over by him under sub-section (2) of section 212 to any other Magistrate and [[?may]] inquire into or try such cases himself."

### In plain English

Sections 450 and 451 are really the Magistrate-level mirror of section 449's Sessions-Judge withdrawal power, split along the same judicial-versus-executive line that runs through the rest of the Sanhita's Magistrate provisions. A Chief Judicial Magistrate has a broad power under section 450(1) to pull back any case made over to a subordinate Magistrate and either handle it personally or send it elsewhere competent. An ordinary Judicial Magistrate has a narrower, more specific power under section 450(2) — recalling only a case that Magistrate personally made over under the particular route in section 212(2). On the Executive Magistrate side, section 451 gives District and Sub-divisional Magistrates a clean two-part power: make a proceeding over to someone subordinate in the first place, and separately, take it back later if needed. What ties this entire Chapter's Magistrate-and-Sessions-Judge powers together is section 452 — a short but absolute rule that nobody exercising any of these powers, from a straightforward section 448 division transfer through to a section 451 Executive Magistrate withdrawal, gets to do so silently. Reasons have to be recorded every time, which is what makes each of these otherwise fairly open-textured discretionary powers reviewable rather than opaque.

### What changed from the CrPC

BNSS sections 450–452 are provisionally mapped to CrPC sections 410–412 (CrPC Chapter XXXI, "Transfer of Criminal Cases"). This mapping is unverified against the CrPC bare-act text and must be independently confirmed before use in any filing.

The Chief Judicial Magistrate's and Judicial Magistrate's withdrawal/recall powers, the Executive Magistrate's making-over and withdrawal power, and the blanket reasons-to-be-recorded rule spanning sections 448–451 all read as continuations of the presumed CrPC scheme. Section 450(2)'s cross-reference to "sub-section (2) of section 212" should be independently re-verified against the current BNSS numbering before being relied upon, since the making-over mechanism it points to sits in a different chapter and its exact scope has not been re-checked here.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct A Judicial Magistrate had earlier made over a case to another Magistrate under a provision other than sub-section (2) of section 212. The Judicial Magistrate now wishes to recall that case under section 450(2). Can he do so?

::: ruling No, not under section 450(2) as such. Section 450(2) is specifically confined to recalling a case made over by that Judicial Magistrate under sub-section (2) of section 212 -- it is not a general recall power over every case the Magistrate has ever made over by any route. If the original making-over happened under a different provision, section 450(2) simply does not supply the recall power for that case, and the Magistrate would need to identify whichever other provision (if any) might apply instead.
:::
::::

:::: instinct A District Magistrate makes an order under section 451(b) withdrawing a case from a subordinate Magistrate, but the written order simply states the case is withdrawn without giving any reason for the withdrawal. Is the order valid as made?

::: ruling No, not as made. Section 452 requires a Sessions Judge or Magistrate making an order under section 448, 449, 450, or 451 to record his reasons for making it -- this duty applies squarely to a section 451(b) withdrawal order, and a bare order of withdrawal with no stated reasons does not satisfy it. The District Magistrate would need to record the actual reasons for the withdrawal, whether by amending the order or issuing a proper reasoned order, before it can be treated as validly made under section 451(b).
:::

#### Sword and shield

For a party challenging a **withdrawal or recall order** at any level in this Chapter, section 452 is a clean, universally applicable procedural check -- an order under sections 448 through 451 that lacks recorded reasons is vulnerable on that ground alone, independent of whether the underlying decision to withdraw or transfer was otherwise sound. For an officer **making** such an order, building the reasons into the order itself at the time of making it (rather than reconstructing them later if challenged) is the safest practice, since section 452 speaks of recording reasons for making the order, not justifying it after the fact.

#### The limitation clock

None of sections 450–452 impose a filing-limitation period; these are ongoing case-management powers exercisable at appropriate points during a proceeding's pendency, not time-gated applications.

#### Strategy and drafting

- When drafting any withdrawal, recall, or making-over order under sections 448 through 451, build the reasons directly into the body of the order rather than treating them as a separate or optional step -- section 452 makes this a condition of a validly made order, not a best practice.
- Before relying on section 450(2) to support a recall, trace the specific provision under which the case was originally made over -- if it was not section 212(2), a different power (or none at all under this cluster) actually applies.
- When challenging a Magistrate-level withdrawal or making-over order, check first and specifically for a recorded-reasons defect under section 452 -- it is often the fastest and cleanest ground available, ahead of any substantive challenge to the underlying decision.
- For Executive Magistrates exercising section 451, keep the making-over and withdrawal decisions procedurally distinct and separately reasoned -- collapsing both into a single undifferentiated order risks obscuring which specific reasons support which specific action, inviting challenge on clarity grounds alone.
