# Chapter XXVIII — Provisions as to Offences Affecting the Administration of Justice

## Sections 379–381 — The Court's own complaint mechanism for offences against public justice

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute A witness lies under oath, or a document is forged and produced in evidence, or a party fabricates false evidence for use in a pending proceeding — all offences within section 215(1)(b)'s closed list, which section 215 itself already established can only be prosecuted on a complaint from the Court concerned, not from any private citizen or even a police FIR. Section 379 is that complaint mechanism in operation: it tells the Court exactly how to act on its own gatekeeping power — record a finding, make a written complaint, send it to a Magistrate of the first class, secure the accused's appearance (or send him in custody if the offence is non-bailable), and bind over the witnesses who will need to testify about it. Section 380 gives the person aggrieved by the Court's decision — whether it refused to complain, or whether it made a complaint against him — a right of appeal to the Court's own superior, with that superior's decision final and immune from revision. Section 381 is a small but practically important adjunct: whoever hears the application or the appeal can also award costs.
:::

::: story The forged document that triggers its own prosecution
A civil suit turns on a document filed by the plaintiff that the defendant says is forged. At the conclusion of the trial, the Court forms the opinion — on an application from the defendant, or entirely on its own — that it is expedient in the interests of justice to inquire into whether the plaintiff fabricated the document for use in that very proceeding, an offence within section 215(1)(b)'s list. The Court conducts a short preliminary inquiry, records a finding that a prosecution is warranted, and makes a written complaint, forwarding it to the Magistrate of the first class with jurisdiction. Because the alleged offence is bailable, the Court takes security for the plaintiff's appearance before the Magistrate rather than sending him into custody, and binds over the defendant and the document examiner to appear and give evidence. The plaintiff, aggrieved, appeals under section 380 to the Court to which the trial Court is subordinate — but the superior Court, after hearing both sides, declines to interfere, and that decision is final: no revision lies against it. Throughout, both the trial Court and the superior Court on appeal award costs against the losing side on the complaint question, exercising the power section 381 gives them.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

> **379. Procedure in cases mentioned in section 215.** (1) When, upon an application made to it in this behalf or otherwise, any Court is of opinion that it is expedient in the interests of justice that an inquiry should be made into any offence referred to in clause (b) of sub-section (1) of section 215, which appears to have been committed in or in relation to a proceeding in that Court or, as the case may be, in respect of a document produced or given in evidence in a proceeding in that Court, such Court [[?may]], after such preliminary inquiry, if any, as it thinks necessary —
> (a) record a finding to that effect;
> (b) make a complaint thereof in writing;
> (c) send it to a Magistrate of the first class having jurisdiction;
> (d) take sufficient security for the appearance of the accused before such Magistrate, or if the alleged offence is non-bailable and the Court thinks it necessary so to do, send the accused in custody to such Magistrate; and
> (e) bind over any person to appear and give evidence before such Magistrate.
>
> (2) The power conferred on a Court by sub-section (1) in respect of an offence [[?may]], in any case where that Court has neither made a complaint under sub-section (1) in respect of that offence nor rejected an application for the making of such complaint, be exercised by the Court to which such former Court is subordinate within the meaning of sub-section (4) of section 215.
>
> (3) A complaint made under this section [[!shall]] be signed —
> (a) where the Court making the complaint is a High Court, by such officer of the Court as the Court may appoint;
> (b) in any other case, by the presiding officer of the Court or by such officer of the Court as the Court may authorise in writing in this behalf.
>
> (4) In this section, "Court" has the same meaning as in section 215.
>
> **380. Appeal.** (1) Any person on whose application any Court other than a High Court has refused to make a complaint under sub-section (1) or sub-section (2) of section 379, or against whom such a complaint has been made by such Court, [[?may]] appeal to the Court to which such former Court is subordinate within the meaning of sub-section (4) of section 215, and the superior Court [[?may]] thereupon, after notice to the parties concerned, direct the withdrawal of the complaint, or, as the case may be, making of the complaint which such former Court might have made under section 379, and, if it makes such complaint, the provisions of that section [[!shall]] apply accordingly.
>
> (2) An order under this section, and subject to any such order, an order under section 379, [[!shall]] be final, and [[!shall]] not be subject to revision.
>
> **381. Power to order costs.** Any Court dealing with an application made to it for filing a complaint under section 379 or an appeal under section 380, [[!shall]] have power to make such order as to costs as may be just.

::: proviso Section 379 applies only to section 215(1)(b) offences — the Court's own-complaint machinery for offences against public justice and against documents given in evidence, not the separate section 215(1)(a) band of offences against public servants
:::

::: proviso Section 380's finality is absolute — "shall not be subject to revision" closes off the ordinary revisional route entirely, leaving no second look beyond the appeal itself
:::

### In plain English

Some offences are so tied up with the machinery of justice itself — lying under oath, forging a document that ends up in a court file — that the ordinary rule of "anyone can complain" is deliberately switched off. Section 215 already decided that only the Court can start that prosecution. Section 379 is simply the mechanics of the Court actually doing it: look into it if it seems worth looking into, record what you found, write out a formal complaint, hand it to a Magistrate of the first class, and make sure the accused and the witnesses will actually show up when the Magistrate takes over. If the Court refuses to act, or if it does act and the accused thinks that was wrong, section 380 gives a right of appeal to the Court one level up — but only one level, and only once: whatever that superior Court decides is the last word, not subject to a further revision petition. And whichever Court is dealing with the application or the appeal can make the losing side pay costs, under section 381.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct A private citizen, convinced that a witness in a completed civil trial committed perjury, files a complaint directly with a Magistrate without any complaint or reference from the trial Court itself.
::: ruling The private complaint does not satisfy section 215(1)(b) — it needed to come from the Court
Section 215(1)(b) restricts standing for offences against public justice committed in or in relation to a Court proceeding to the Court itself, an officer it authorises, or a superior Court — section 379 is that restriction in operational form. A private citizen's complaint, however accurate, does not substitute for the Court's own complaint and cannot be the basis for the Magistrate taking cognizance of this category of offence.
:::
::::

:::: instinct A trial Court refuses to make a section 379 complaint against a witness the losing party suspected of perjury. The losing party, dissatisfied, files a revision petition directly with the High Court against that refusal, without first appealing to the Court's immediate superior.
::: ruling The revision petition is misconceived — section 380 requires an appeal first, and forecloses revision even after that appeal
Section 380(1) gives the aggrieved party a right of appeal to the Court to which the refusing Court is subordinate. Section 380(2) then makes that superior Court's decision (and, subject to it, the original section 379 order) final and expressly not subject to revision — the revisional route is closed at both stages, not merely bypassed by going straight to a superior forum.
:::
::::

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

⚠️ Research gap — no citation is promoted for this cluster. The section 379–381 complaint mechanism is itself largely procedural (how the Court exercises a power section 215 already confers), and this guide has not identified a Supreme Court authority specifically on this machinery, as distinct from the substantive section 215(1)(b) standing question already addressed with a promoted citation in Chapter XV.

#### Sword and shield

As a **sword**, a party who believes the opposing side has forged a document or given false evidence in the very proceeding at hand should apply to that Court directly under section 379(1) rather than attempting a private complaint or an FIR, which section 215(1)(b) forecloses for this category of offence. As a **shield**, a person facing a section 379 complaint — or facing the Court's refusal to make one against an opponent — has exactly one further forum: the appeal under section 380, and should treat that as the final opportunity to be heard, since no revision lies beyond it.

#### The limitation clock

No independent limitation period runs under sections 379–381. Section 379(1) is not time-bound by the section itself, though a Court considering it "expedient in the interests of justice" will, in practice, act promptly after the offence appears rather than long afterward — delay bears on the merits of expediency, not on a fixed statutory clock.

#### Interlocking matrix

Section 379 is the operative machinery for the standing restriction already built in Chapter XV's section 215(1)(b), and section 379(4)'s definition of "Court" borrows directly from section 215 itself. Section 382, built in the next cluster of this chapter, governs what the Magistrate of the first class does once a section 379 or section 380 complaint reaches him.

#### Strategy and drafting

**Model checklist for invoking or resisting the section 379 complaint mechanism:**

1. Before applying under section 379(1), confirm the suspected offence actually falls within section 215(1)(b)'s closed list — this machinery has no application to offences outside that list.
2. Draft the application to identify precisely what preliminary inquiry, if any, is being requested, and why it is expedient in the interests of justice — a bare assertion of wrongdoing is not itself the standard.
3. If the Court refuses to complain, or makes a complaint that is being resisted, prepare the section 380 appeal as the sole remaining avenue — do not plan around a subsequent revision petition, since none lies.
4. Raise the question of costs under section 381 affirmatively at the hearing on the application or the appeal, rather than assuming the point will be addressed without being argued.
