# Chapter XIII — Information to the Police and Their Powers to Investigate

## Section 173 — Information in cognizable cases

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute A police officer cannot tell you to "come back tomorrow" once what you have described is a cognizable offence — the moment he decides that, writing it down stops being his choice.

Anita walks into a police station at 11 p.m. to report that her neighbour, Bharat, broke into her house an hour earlier and threatened her with a knife. The constable on duty listens, then tells her the station "doesn't have jurisdiction" because Bharat lives two streets over, in the next station's area, and she should go there instead. It is close to midnight. The other station is four kilometres away, and she has no way to get there tonight.

Nothing in the law required that answer. If what Anita described amounts to a cognizable offence — one the police can investigate, and arrest for, without a magistrate's order first — the officer's only job at that moment is to write it down, read it back to her, and get her signature. Whether the crime actually happened, whether Bharat has a defence, whether this is really an old property dispute dressed up as a crime — none of that is his decision to make before he registers it. That question belongs to the investigation that comes after. It does not belong to the doorway.

The one thing almost everyone gets wrong, including some officers, is treating registration and investigation as the same step — with registration as the reward for a complaint that already looks strong enough. They are not the same step. Registration is a formality triggered by the *content* of the information, not a verdict on its truth. An officer who declines to register a cognizable-offence complaint because he suspects it is exaggerated, because it is inconvenient, or because he would rather send it to a station he thinks is someone else's problem, is not being cautious. He has no discretion to be cautious with. On the facts as told to him, the statute has already decided.

:::

::: story Two kinds of "information", and why the door Anita walked through matters more than what she actually said

Every police station keeps two different books, and which one a complaint goes into changes almost everything about what happens after.

If what is reported is a **cognizable offence** — broadly, one serious enough that the police may arrest without a warrant and investigate on their own initiative, without waiting for a magistrate to tell them to — the information goes into what practitioners still call, out of CrPC-era habit, the FIR register. Registering it does three things at once: it starts the clock on the investigation, it fixes a public, tamper-resistant record of what was alleged and when, and it activates a set of coercive powers — arrest, search, seizure — that the police do not otherwise have. None of those three consequences depends on the complaint later turning out to be true. They attach the moment the information is written down and signed.

If what is reported is a **non-cognizable offence** — typically the less serious end of the spectrum, where the police need a magistrate's order before they can investigate at all — the information goes into a general diary instead, and the complainant is directed to a magistrate. No FIR, no independent police investigation, no arrest power. A station has not failed a complainant by doing this if the offence genuinely is non-cognizable; it has simply followed a different, equally lawful track.

The trouble was never really the rule. The trouble was officers applying it correctly on the law but silently adding conditions the law never contained — come back with a witness; this is a civil matter, not a police matter; you should try the station nearer to where he lives. That last excuse is the oldest trick in the book, and it has a name in every police manual and every writ petition written against it: telling a genuine cognizable-offence complainant that jurisdiction lies elsewhere, in the hope that she gives up before reaching the right door. For decades this was fought station by station, with administrative circulars on one side and writ petitions on the other. What has actually changed at the door Anita is standing at — and exactly how far it has changed — is the subject of the next two stages.

:::

### The Act, decompiled

Read the text below slowly and watch for three things: the word **"irrespective"** in the very first sentence, which is doing more work than its length suggests; the split between the mechanics for information given **orally** and information given **electronically**, which are not the same procedure wearing two hats; and the word **"may"** governing the preliminary-enquiry power in sub-section (3), sitting directly beside a **"shall"**-governed duty to register in sub-section (1) — two different verbs, two different regimes, in the same section.

> **173. Information in cognizable cases.** (1) Every information relating to the commission of a [[=cognizable offence]], [[!irrespective of the area where the offence is committed]], [[?may be given orally or by electronic communication]] to an officer in charge of a police station, and if given—
>
> (i) orally, it [[!shall be reduced to writing]] by him or under his direction, and be read over to the informant; and every such information, whether given in writing or reduced to writing as aforesaid, [[!shall be signed by the person giving it]];
>
> (ii) by electronic communication, it [[!shall be taken on record]] by him on being signed within three days by the person giving it,
>
> and the substance thereof shall be entered in a book to be kept by such officer in such form as the State Government may by rules prescribe in this behalf:
>
> Provided that if the information is given by the woman against whom an offence under section 64, section 65, section 66, section 67, section 68, section 69, section 70, section 71, section 74, section 75, section 76, section 77, section 78, section 79 or section 124 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 is alleged to have been committed or attempted, then such information [[!shall be recorded, by a woman police officer or any woman officer]]:
>
> Provided further that—
>
> (a) in the event that the person against whom an offence under section 64, section 65, section 66, section 67, section 68, section 69, section 70, section 71, section 74, section 75, section 76, section 77, section 78, section 79 or section 124 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 is alleged to have been committed or attempted, is temporarily or permanently mentally or physically disabled, then such information shall be recorded by a police officer, at the residence of the person seeking to report such offence or at a convenient place of such person's choice, in the presence of an interpreter or a special educator, as the case may be;
>
> (b) the recording of such information [[!shall be videographed]];
>
> (c) the police officer shall get the statement of the person recorded by a Magistrate under clause (a) of sub-section (6) of section 183 as soon as possible.
>
> (2) A copy of the information as recorded under sub-section (1) [[!shall be given forthwith, free of cost]], to the informant or the victim.
>
> (3) Without prejudice to the provisions contained in section 175, on receipt of information relating to the commission of any [[=cognizable offence]], which is made punishable for three years or more but less than seven years, the officer in charge of the police station [[?may with the prior permission from an officer not below the rank of Deputy Superintendent of Police]], considering the nature and gravity of the offence,—
>
> (i) proceed to conduct preliminary enquiry to ascertain whether there exists a prima facie case for proceeding in the matter within a period of fourteen days; or
>
> (ii) proceed with investigation when there exists a prima facie case.
>
> (4) Any person aggrieved by a refusal on the part of an officer in charge of a police station to record the information referred to in sub-section (1), may send the substance of such information, in writing and by post, to the Superintendent of Police concerned who, if satisfied that such information discloses the commission of a [[=cognizable offence]], shall either investigate the case himself or direct an investigation to be made by any police officer subordinate to him, in the manner provided by this Sanhita, and such officer shall have all the powers of an officer in charge of the police station in relation to that offence failing which such aggrieved person may make an application to the Magistrate.

**Statutory mechanics.**

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| "may be given orally or by electronic communication" | Discretionary — the informant's choice | The complainant is not confined to walking into a station; a call, e-mail or online complaint counts, subject to the sub-section (1)(ii) formalities below |
| "irrespective of the area where the offence is committed" | Mandatory — removes the territorial gate | No station may refuse to register on the ground that the offence occurred within another station's limits — the statutory zero-FIR rule |
| "shall be reduced to writing… shall be signed" | Mandatory | Once information is given orally, the writing and the signature are not optional formalities layered on top of the duty — they are the duty |
| "shall be taken on record… on being signed within three days" | Mandatory, but time-boxed differently | Electronic information is provisionally on record from the moment it is received; the signature is a three-day-later formality, not a precondition to registration |
| "shall be recorded, by a woman police officer" (first proviso) | Mandatory, offence-triggered | Activated automatically by the BNS provision alleged, not by any request the informant has to make |
| "may… proceed to conduct preliminary enquiry" (sub-section 3) | Discretionary, and gated | Available at all only if the three conditions unpacked below are satisfied — see the first proviso block |
| "shall be given forthwith, free of cost" (sub-section 2) | Mandatory | Runs from the moment of registration, not from the completion of investigation or any later stage |

::: proviso The three gates that decide whether "let us look into it first" is ever lawful

Sub-section (3) is the clause every reluctant station reaches for, because it is the only place in the section where the word "may" appears next to the word "cognizable." Reaching for it lawfully requires all three of the following, not any one:

1. **The offence must carry a specific punishment band** — three years or more, but less than seven. An offence punishable with less than three years, or with seven years or more, is not eligible for this route at all; sub-section (1)'s plain registration duty is the only path.
2. **Permission must come from an officer not below the rank of Deputy Superintendent of Police**, obtained *before* the enquiry begins, not sought afterwards to regularise it. A Station House Officer cannot authorise himself.
3. **The enquiry is capped at fourteen days**, and it must end in one of exactly two outcomes named in the text — a decision to investigate, because a prima facie case exists, or (read with section 175) closure. There is no third outcome, and no extension provision anywhere in the section.

Miss any one of the three and the station has no lawful preliminary-enquiry route open to it. It is back to the plain rule in sub-section (1): register first, question the facts afterward.

:::

::: proviso What "irrespective of the area" actually removes, and what it does not

This phrase abolishes exactly one excuse: territorial jurisdiction as a ground for refusing to *register*. It does not abolish territorial jurisdiction generally, and it does not require the registering station to *investigate* the matter itself to conclusion.

The ordinary sequence once a station registers information about an offence committed outside its own limits is to register it — this is now non-negotiable — and then transfer it to the station that does have territorial jurisdiction over the offence, for investigation. What "irrespective of the area" removes is the ability to use the transfer step as a reason to refuse the registration step. Before, a complainant could be bounced between two stations, each pointing at the other, with no FIR registered anywhere while the argument continued. After, there is always at least one FIR on record the moment information is given, wherever it was given, and the argument about which station investigates happens on top of that record rather than instead of it.

:::

### In plain English

**"Cognizable" is the master switch, and it is decided before anything else.** Everything in this section — the mandatory writing, the mandatory signature, the free copy, the whole apparatus — only turns on if what is described is a cognizable offence in the first place. Get that classification wrong and every downstream argument about registration is being had about the wrong provision; the real fight, for a genuinely non-cognizable complaint, is about section 174 and the route to a Magistrate, not about this section at all.

**Two modes of giving information, and they are not one procedure with two doors.** Oral information becomes law the moment it is written down, read back, and signed — a single continuous act. Electronic information is treated as provisionally on the record from the moment it arrives, with the signature following within three days. The practical effect is that a complainant who reports online or by e-mail late at night does not lose priority to one who manages to reach a station in person; the record exists from receipt, and the three days is a formality catching up to it, not a waiting period before anything counts.

**"Irrespective of the area" is the statutory answer to the jurisdiction excuse**, and it does exactly one job: it removes territorial jurisdiction as a ground for refusing to register. It was never, even before this Act, a ground any station was actually entitled to use — but it took years of circulars and litigation to establish that as a matter of practice. Putting the words in the Act itself removes the argument at the source rather than relying on a complainant to know the case law that says the same thing.

**The two provisos protecting vulnerable informants are triggered automatically, not by request.** The moment the information alleges one of the listed BNS offences, three things follow without Anita or anyone else having to ask for them: a woman officer records it if the informant is a woman, the recording happens at a place and in a manner suited to a disabled informant if that is who is reporting, and the recording is videographed. The Act places the burden of getting this right on the station, not on the person reporting a crime to know what she is entitled to demand.

**The preliminary enquiry in sub-section (3) is a narrow, time-boxed deferral — not a second opinion on whether to register.** It exists for one punishment band only, requires permission pitched above the level of the investigating officer himself, and is capped at fourteen days with exactly two lawful endpoints. Every one of those limits is a limit on how long registration can be deferred, not a description of when registration becomes optional.

**The refusal remedy in sub-section (4) is a chain, not a single lever.** A complainant whose information is refused does not go straight to court. The text contemplates the Superintendent of Police first, and only on that failing does it contemplate a Magistrate — and, read with section 175(3), a Magistrate who acts on affidavit evidence, not on a bare application. A practitioner who skips straight to a Magistrate without first trying the Superintendent is not following a shortcut; he is skipping a step the text itself lays out in order.

### What changed from the CrPC

> **CrPC 1973, s. 154 → BNSS 2023, s. 173.** *(one-to-one mapping, verified against the bare Act text in this repository — not a flagged or hypothetical number.)*

**Delta bucket: substantively amended.** The registration mechanics that CrPC practitioners already know survive largely intact — but the section now carries a genuinely new power, sub-section (3)'s preliminary enquiry, that has no CrPC ancestor at all, plus a new mode of giving information and a textual answer to the jurisdiction excuse that the CrPC never gave. A provision can be "substantively amended" without every sentence in it changing; it is enough that a condition, a power or a safeguard has been added, and here three have been.

**The core registration duty — renumbered with drafting changes.** The obligation to reduce oral information to writing, read it back, obtain a signature, and enter it in the register is, in substance, the CrPC obligation wearing 2023 punctuation. CrPC-era authority on what counts as valid registration continues to apply to this part of the text without qualification.

**Two additions with no CrPC counterpart at all — the words actually changed:**

| CrPC 1973, s. 154(1) | BNSS 2023, s. 173(1) |
|---|---|
| "Every information relating to the commission of a cognizable offence, **if given orally** to an officer in charge of a police station, shall be reduced to writing by him or under his direction, and be read over to the informant…" | "Every information relating to the commission of a cognizable offence, **irrespective of the area where the offence is committed**, **may be given orally or by electronic communication** to an officer in charge of a police station, and if given— (i) orally, it shall be reduced to writing by him or under his direction, and be read over to the informant… (ii) **by electronic communication, it shall be taken on record by him on being signed within three days by the person giving it**…" |
| *(sub-section (1) contains no equivalent — this entire power is new)* | "**(3) Without prejudice to the provisions contained in section 175**, on receipt of information relating to the commission of any cognizable offence, which is made punishable for three years or more but less than seven years, the officer in charge of the police station **may with the prior permission** from an officer not below the rank of Deputy Superintendent of Police… **proceed to conduct preliminary enquiry**… **within a period of fourteen days**…" |
| s. 154(3): "…shall either investigate the case himself or direct an investigation to be made by any police officer subordinate to him, in the manner provided by this Code…" | s. 173(4): "…in the manner provided by this Sanhita, and such officer shall have all the powers of an officer in charge of the police station in relation to that offence **failing which such aggrieved person may make an application to the Magistrate**." |

**What a practitioner must now do differently.** Three things, in order of how often they will actually come up. First, a complainant is no longer confined to the station whose jurisdiction she can prove at the counter — advise a client to report by e-mail or registered online complaint the moment an in-person report is refused, and keep the transmission record; "irrespective of the area" and the electronic-communication route now work together to remove the two excuses stations have historically used against each other. Second, do not assume a police "enquiry" before registration is automatically improper — check the punishment band first. If it sits inside three-to-seven years and proper Deputy Superintendent-level permission was obtained, a fourteen-day enquiry is lawful and the fight is about whether the fourteen days and the permission were real, not about whether an enquiry could happen at all. Third, if a refusal-to-register application ever needs to go to a Magistrate, draft the affidavit from the outset — section 175(3) makes it part of the remedy, not an optional attachment, and an application filed without one risks going nowhere regardless of how strong the underlying complaint is.

**Lalita Kumari, and precisely how much of it survives.**

*Lalita Kumari* v. *Government of Uttar Pradesh*, (2014) 2 SCC 1, is a five-judge Constitution Bench decision construing **CrPC s. 154(1)**. Its holding, stated as the operative test: registration of an FIR is mandatory the moment information discloses the commission of a cognizable offence, and no preliminary enquiry is permissible at that stage to first verify the truth of the information. The Bench carved out a narrow, closed list of situations in which a preliminary enquiry may precede registration — illustratively, matrimonial and family disputes, commercial and financial disputes, medical negligence cases, corruption cases, and cases with abnormal and unexplained delay in reporting — and held that any such enquiry must be time-bound (the Bench used seven days as the outer limit) and its outcome, including any delay, must be reflected in the General Diary.

BNSS s. 173(3) now gives a preliminary-enquiry power the force of the Act itself for the first time — but on a **different gate entirely**. *Lalita Kumari*'s categories are defined by the **nature of the dispute**. Section 173(3) is defined by the **quantum of punishment**: three to seven years, full stop, regardless of whether the case is matrimonial, commercial, or none of those things. The permission mechanism is new too — Deputy Superintendent-level clearance appears nowhere in *Lalita Kumari* — and the time limit has moved from the Bench's seven days to a statutory fourteen.

**Whether *Lalita Kumari*'s categories survive as an independent basis for a preliminary enquiry outside the three-to-seven-year band is, honestly, open.** There is no reported decision yet construing this question on the BNSS text. Two readings are both textually available: that Parliament, by enacting a specific and narrower statutory power, occupied the field and left no room for a broader judge-made category outside it; or that a Constitution Bench's general interpretation of the mandatory-registration rule under s. 154(1) is not automatically extinguished by Parliament adding one particular, narrower statutory carve-out elsewhere in the same section. Do not tell a client either position is settled. Argue the second in the alternative — it is available on principle — but be ready for the first as the opposing argument, because it is available too.

### Test your instinct

::::instinct The complaint that was written down, signed and copied — but never called an "FIR"

On 14 August 2024, Anita reports to Inspector Farhan that Bharat forced entry into her flat and stole jewellery worth ₹4,20,000. Farhan writes it down exactly as she narrates it, reads it back to her, and she signs it. He hands her a copy the same evening, free of cost, exactly as sub-section (2) requires. But the document he hands her is headed "Complaint Register Entry," not "First Information Report," and when her lawyer calls the station a week later to ask for the FIR number, the duty officer says there isn't one yet — "it's still under process."

Anita's lawyer wants to know: has an FIR actually been registered, whatever the station is calling it, or is the station right that something further needs to happen first?

:::ruling Reveal the ruling
An FIR has been registered. Section 173 defines registration by what was *done* — information reduced to writing, read over, signed, and a copy given — not by what label the station's own paperwork uses. *Lalita Kumari* is squarely in point on this exact evasion: the Constitution Bench held that the moment information disclosing a cognizable offence is reduced to writing, read over, and signed under sub-section (1), the FIR stands registered, and no further internal step — a stamp, a separate "FIR number" issued later, a supervisor's sign-off — is a precondition to that. A station cannot invent an intermediate, unregistered-looking category for information that has already satisfied every element the text requires. Anita's lawyer should demand the FIR number in writing and, if refused, treat the refusal itself as the sub-section (4) grievance, not as evidence that no FIR exists.

::::instinct The true complaint that a lawful fourteen-day enquiry screened out anyway

On 3 January 2025, Anita reports that Bharat, her business partner, diverted ₹18 lakh of partnership funds into a personal account — an offence under the BNS provision on criminal breach of trust, punishable with imprisonment of up to five years, comfortably inside the three-to-seven-year band. Inspector Farhan obtains permission from the Deputy Superintendent of Police the same day, in writing, and opens a preliminary enquiry. He examines the bank statements Anita provides, but Bharat's account shows the transfer as a documented loan repayment under a partnership clause Anita insists is forged. On day thirteen, Farhan closes the enquiry, records that no prima facie case is made out, and declines to register an FIR. Anita's account later turns out, on a civil audit eighteen months on, to have been entirely true — the loan document was fabricated — but by then the criminal limitation position has hardened and the money is gone.

Was anything done unlawfully here, given how it turned out?

:::ruling Reveal the ruling
No. Every gate in sub-section (3) was satisfied — the punishment band, the prior Deputy Superintendent-level permission, the fourteen-day cap — and the enquiry reached a genuine, reasoned conclusion within that window. The section does not ask whether the conclusion was *correct*; it asks whether the conditions for reaching it lawfully were met. This is the deliberate cost of the three-to-seven-year carve-out: Parliament created a filter precisely so that police are not compelled to launch a full investigation, with its coercive powers, on every complaint in that punishment band before any documentary sifting happens — and a filter that can screen out weak-looking cases will, by design, sometimes screen out a true one that looked weak on day thirteen. Anita's only lawful next step is sub-section (4) — approaching the Superintendent of Police, who forms an independent view on the same or better material — not a claim that the enquiry itself was unlawful. This is exactly the kind of case where a technically correct complainant gets nowhere on the first attempt, and the honest explanation is that the statute traded a class of these cases for fewer frivolous investigations, not that anyone broke the law.

::::instinct "We don't do zero FIRs anymore, that rule was only for the old Code"

Bharat assaults Anita in City A on 20 February 2025. Anita, who lives and works in City B, reports it at her local station the same night. The duty officer tells her, "Zero FIR was a CrPC thing — under the new Sanhita we don't do that, you'll have to go to City A." Anita's brother, who read a news article about the BNSS a year ago, is fairly sure this is wrong but cannot say why.

Is the officer's statement correct?

:::ruling Reveal the ruling
No, and the error is worth naming precisely because it inverts what actually happened. "Zero FIR" was never a creature of the CrPC's text — the CrPC contained no such phrase and no such statutory mechanism; it existed only as administrative practice built on circulars and writ relief, station by station. What the BNSS did was the opposite of abolishing it: sub-section (1)'s words "irrespective of the area where the offence is committed" now put exactly that obligation into the Act itself, for every cognizable offence, with no separate application or special procedure required to invoke it. There is no narrower "zero FIR" category layered on top of ordinary registration that a station can decline to operate — there is only ordinary registration under sub-section (1), which no longer tolerates a territorial excuse at all. The officer has the history backwards.

::::instinct The e-mail complaint and the arrest that came before the signature

At 1.50 a.m. on 9 June 2025, Anita sends a detailed e-mail to the local police station's registered complaint address describing an assault by Bharat earlier that night, with a photograph of her injuries attached. Inspector Farhan reads it at 7 a.m., treats it as information under sub-section (1)(ii), and arrests Bharat that afternoon. Anita is called to the station and formally signs the record on 11 June — two days after the arrest, but within the three-day window the text allows. Bharat's counsel argues the arrest was unlawful because there was, as of the afternoon of 9 June, no validly registered FIR to arrest on — the information was not yet signed.

Is counsel right?

:::ruling Reveal the ruling
No. Sub-section (1)(ii) treats electronically communicated information as taken on record from the point it is received, with the signature to follow within three days — the signature authenticates a record that already exists; it does not create one that did not. Reading the clause the way Bharat's counsel wants — no valid record, and therefore no police power, until the signature — would mean an officer cannot lawfully act on an urgent electronic complaint for up to three full days, which defeats the entire point of adding electronic communication as a recognised mode in the first place. The arrest stood on a validly recorded cognizable-offence complaint from 7 a.m. on 9 June; the signature obtained on 11 June satisfied a formality that was already running its own, separate clock.

::::instinct The rural station with no woman officer on the night shift

Deepa reports, at 10 p.m. on 5 April 2025, an offence under one of the BNS provisions listed in the first proviso to sub-section (1). The only officer on duty at the rural outpost is a male constable; the station's one woman sub-inspector is off-duty until the following morning, and there is no functioning camera on site that night. The constable tells Deepa to come back at 9 a.m., when Sub-Inspector arrives and a phone with a working camera can be arranged, and records nothing in the meantime.

Is it lawful to make her wait until the morning?

:::ruling Reveal the ruling
No. The proviso governs *who* records the information and *how* — a woman officer, videographed, and, where the informant is disabled, at a place of her choosing with an interpreter or special educator present — but none of that suspends the sub-section (1) duty to register *at all*. The registration obligation is not conditioned on staffing convenience. The correct course was to record the information immediately, by whatever channel was available that night — including, if necessary, treating it as information under sub-section (1)(ii) or contacting a woman officer from a neighbouring station to attend without delay — and to arrange the fuller proviso compliance as promptly as practicable, not to leave the offence unregistered for eleven hours because the roster did not suit the moment Deepa walked in. A station that offers "come back in the morning" as its answer to a proviso-triggering complaint has confused a safeguard for the informant with an excuse for itself.

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

**Lalita Kumari v. Government of Uttar Pradesh**, (2014) 2 SCC 1 — Constitution Bench of five judges (Sathasivam C.J., and Chauhan, Desai, Gogoi and Bobde JJ.), deciding a batch of writ petitions after conflicting High Court practice on whether police could sit on information before registering it.

**Ratio.** Registration of an FIR is mandatory under section 154 CrPC (now BNSS s. 173) if the information discloses a cognizable offence, and no preliminary enquiry is permissible in such a situation — the sole exception being a narrow, court-created category of cases (matrimonial and family disputes, commercial and financial disputes, corruption cases, cases with abnormal delay, and medical negligence) where a preliminary enquiry may be made **before** registration, provided it is confined to verifying whether a cognizable offence is disclosed at all and is completed within a maximum of seven days.

**Practitioner takeaway.** *"Registration is the rule, preliminary enquiry the exception, and the exception exists to test whether an offence is disclosed at all — not to test whether the complaint will succeed."*

**BNSS caveat.** *Lalita Kumari* is a CrPC-era judgment construing s. 154, which s. 173 replaces under the **substantively amended** bucket identified in the concordance above. Its core holding — that registration is not discretionary once a cognizable offence is disclosed — survives untouched, because sub-section (1) preserves the "shall" that the judgment was built on. What does **not** straightforwardly survive is the judgment's own list of categories and its seven-day enquiry cap: sub-section (3) now supplies a different, narrower gate (offences punishable with three to seven years, DSP-level permission, fourteen days), and which of the two regimes governs a case outside that punishment band is the open question flagged in the concordance. Cite the ratio on the mandatory-registration point without qualification; cite the categories-and-seven-days point only with the caveat that its field has been statutorily narrowed.

#### Sword and shield

| | Offensive use (complainant / prosecution) | Defensive use (accused) |
|---|---|---|
| At the registration stage | Insist on an FIR number and a free copy under sub-section (2) the moment oral information is signed; refuse to accept a "let us look into it first" response for a cognizable offence — that has no basis in the text. | Where registration is sought to pressure a civil dispute into criminal colour, invoke the *Lalita Kumari* categories (matrimonial, commercial, financial) to argue a preliminary enquiry should have preceded registration, and that its absence taints the FIR's genesis. |
| Once an enquiry is invoked | If the offence falls outside the three-to-seven-year band, argue sub-section (3) has no application at all and registration was due immediately — any "enquiry" conducted is itself irregular delay, usable to show mala fide or an attempt to dilute evidence. | If within the band, demand proof that DSP-level permission was obtained *before* the enquiry began and that it closed within fourteen days; a permission obtained after the fact, or an enquiry that overran, is a ground to attack everything built on it. |
| On refusal to register | Use the sub-section (4) chain — Superintendent, then a Magistrate on affidavit under s. 175(3) — as a paper trail: each unanswered written complaint is itself evidence for the eventual Magistrate application. | — |
| On the special-informant provisos | A woman or a person with disability reporting a listed offence can insist on a woman officer, a suitable venue and manner, and videography, without having to ask — silence on the point at the trial stage is itself a defect in the prosecution's compliance. | Absence of videography or of a woman officer where the proviso applied can be argued at trial as a departure from mandatory procedure, going to the weight of the FIR rather than its validity outright. |

#### The limitation clock

| Event | Period | Source | Consequence of default |
|---|---|---|---|
| Signing an FIR recorded from oral information | Immediate — part of the same continuous act of recording, reading over and signing | s. 173(1) | An FIR read over but not signed, or signed without being read over, is not a completed FIR under the text. |
| Signature on electronically-recorded information | Within **three days** of the information being received | Proviso to s. 173(1) | The three days is a formality catching up to a record that already exists from receipt — it is not a grace period for deciding whether to record it at all. |
| Preliminary enquiry under sub-section (3) | Capped at **fourteen days** from receipt of the information | s. 173(3) | An enquiry running past day fourteen is itself an irregularity and a ground of complaint under sub-section (4), regardless of what it finds. |
| Free copy of the FIR to the informant | "Forthwith" — no numeric period, but read as without avoidable delay given the mandatory character of sub-section (2) | s. 173(2) | Delay in supplying the copy is independently actionable; it is not cured by the FIR having been correctly registered. |
| Completion of investigation, offences under BNS ss. 64, 65, 66, 67, 68, 70, 71 or POCSO ss. 4, 6, 8, 10 | **Two months** from the date the information was recorded | s. 193(2) | This is the clock that starts running the moment s. 173 registration happens — the FIR date fixes the deadline for the report that follows it. |

#### Interlocking matrix

| Connects to | Relationship |
|---|---|
| **BNSS s. 174** | The non-cognizable counterpart. Wrong classification at the s. 173 stage sends a genuinely cognizable complaint down the s. 174 diary-and-Magistrate-referral track instead of registration — the two sections are mutually exclusive doors, not sequential steps. |
| **BNSS s. 175(1) & (3)** | Sub-section (1) is the power to investigate that an FIR under s. 173 activates. Sub-section (3) is the mechanism that closes the loop on a s. 173(4) refusal: a Magistrate empowered under s. 210 orders investigation only "after considering the application supported by an affidavit made under sub-section (4) of section 173" — the affidavit requirement is not implied, it is cross-referenced by number. |
| **BNSS s. 183** | Statements and confessions recorded in the course of the investigation that the FIR opens; the FIR date is the anchor against which the "course of investigation" in s. 183 is measured. |
| **BNSS s. 193** | Sets the investigation-completion clock (see limitation table above), running from the same date the s. 173 information was recorded. |
| **BNSS s. 210** | Cognizance by a Magistrate on a police report is the eventual destination of an investigation that began with s. 173 registration; it is also the section that identifies which Magistrate is "empowered" for purposes of the s. 175(3) affidavit application. |
| **BNS 2023 offence provisions** | Determine cognizability itself (First Schedule classification) and, for the two provisos, determine whether the offence is on the listed set (BNS ss. 64, 65, 66, 67, 68, 70, 71, 74, 76, 79, or under s. 4 of the Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961) that triggers the woman-officer, suitable-venue and videography requirements. |
| **BSA 2023** | The FIR itself is not substantive evidence of the facts stated, only a prior statement usable to corroborate or contradict the informant under the BSA's provisions on previous statements — a point worth pleading precisely rather than treating the FIR as if it were testimony. |
| **Applicable forms / filings** | The written complaint to the Superintendent under s. 173(4); the Magistrate application under s. 173(4) read with s. 175(3), which must be **supported by an affidavit** — not a bare petition. |

#### The authorities

**Priyanka Srivastava v. State of Uttar Pradesh**, (2015) 6 SCC 287 — two-judge bench (Dipak Misra and Prafulla C. Pant JJ.). Held that an application under s. 156(3) CrPC (the predecessor mechanism a complainant used to compel investigation via a Magistrate, now restructured through ss. 173(4) and 175(3) BNSS) must be supported by an affidavit, so that a complainant who makes a false averment can be held accountable — the Court was troubled by the routine, unaccountable use of such applications to pressure the police outside the regular process. **How it is used:** the affidavit requirement the Court read into practice is now written directly into the BNSS text at s. 173(4) and cross-referenced in s. 175(3); an application without one can be rejected on that ground alone, and *Priyanka Srivastava* is the authority for why the requirement exists — to deter casual or coercive use of the Magistrate route.

#### Strategy and drafting

**Drafting traps.**

1. Do not plead "the police refused to register an FIR" without first showing the written complaint to the Superintendent that sub-section (4) requires as the first step. A Magistrate application filed without that step, and without having gone through s. 175(3)'s affidavit route, invites a threshold objection that the remedy was never properly invoked.
2. Do not treat a preliminary enquiry as available by default. If the offence alleged carries less than three years or seven years or more, sub-section (3) simply does not apply, and any delay dressed up as an "enquiry" is itself pleadable as irregularity — say so affirmatively rather than only rebutting it if raised.
3. Do not draft a s. 173(4)/s. 175(3) application as a bare petition. It must be **supported by an affidavit**; an unsupported application is vulnerable to outright rejection independent of its merits, per *Priyanka Srivastava*'s reasoning now codified in the text.
4. Where the informant is a woman or a person with disability and the offence is on the listed set, plead the proviso compliance (or its absence) explicitly and early — do not assume the reader or the court will infer it from the offence alone.

**Timing.** File the Superintendent complaint the moment refusal is apparent — the fourteen-day preliminary-enquiry clock and the two-month investigation clock under s. 193(2) both run from dates fixed independently of when a complainant gets around to escalating, so delay in escalating does not toll anything in the complainant's favour.

**Model grounds checklist for a section 173(4) / section 175(3) Magistrate application:**

1. That the information given to the officer in charge discloses a cognizable offence, identifying the specific BNS provision(s).
2. That the information was tendered in writing (or reduced to writing and signature sought, as applicable) and the date and manner of tendering.
3. That the officer in charge refused, or failed within a reasonable time, to register the information.
4. That a written complaint was thereafter sent to the Superintendent of Police under sub-section (4), with its date, and that the Superintendent failed to investigate or direct investigation.
5. That no preliminary enquiry under sub-section (3) was permissible on the facts, or, if one was conducted, that it exceeded the fourteen-day limit or lacked the required DSP-level prior permission.
6. A verification clause and a supporting affidavit under sub-section (4) of section 173, sworn in the form the Magistrate under section 210 is empowered to act upon per section 175(3).
