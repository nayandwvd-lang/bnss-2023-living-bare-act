# Chapter XII — Preventive Action of the Police

## Sections 168–169 — The general duty to prevent a cognizable offence, and the duty to pass the word up the chain when one is being planned

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Two short duty-imposing sections that sit upstream of every arrest and investigation chapter: what a police officer must do the moment he learns an offence is coming, before it has happened.
Chapter XII is the Sanhita's shortest functional chapter, and its plainest. Sections 168 and 169 do not create any new power to arrest, search or seize — those come later, in section 170 and beyond. They instead fix two duties that operate at the earliest possible moment: the general, ever-present duty on every police officer to try to stop a cognizable offence he becomes aware of, and the narrower, reporting duty that applies when an officer learns of a design — a plan — to commit one, requiring him to pass that information up and across the chain of command rather than sit on it.
:::

::: story The constable who watched and did nothing
A constable on beat patrol overhears two men in a tea stall openly discussing a plan to rob a shop later that evening. He recognises the offence being planned as cognizable, but decides it is not his beat and walks on without telling anyone. When the robbery occurs as planned, the constable's inaction becomes the subject of a departmental inquiry. Section 168 does not wait for the offence to occur before imposing a duty — the duty to do his best to prevent it existed the moment he became aware of the plan — and section 169 separately required him to communicate that information to his superior officer, and to any other officer whose duty it was to prevent or take cognizance of such an offence. He did neither.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 168 — Police to prevent cognizable offences
"168. Every police officer [[?may]] interpose for the purpose of preventing, and [[!shall]], to the best of his ability, prevent, the commission of any cognizable offence."
:::

::: proviso Section 169 — Information of design to commit cognizable offences
"169. Every police officer receiving information of a design to commit any cognizable offence [[!shall]] communicate such information to the police officer to whom he is subordinate, and to any other officer whose duty it is to prevent or take cognizance of the commission of any such offence."
:::

### In plain English

Section 168 is deliberately written with both a permissive limb and a mandatory limb sitting side by side: an officer "may interpose" — meaning he has the discretion to physically step in and act — but he "shall, to the best of his ability, prevent" the offence, which is not discretionary at all. The permissive language covers the choice of method; the mandatory language covers the outcome he must be trying to achieve. An officer who does nothing at all when he could reasonably have intervened has not simply declined to exercise a discretion — he has failed a duty. Section 169 covers an earlier moment in time: information of a plan, before anything has actually started. Here the officer's own personal duty is not to single-handedly stop the plan, but to pass the information along — to his own superior, and separately to any other officer whose job it actually is to prevent or take cognizance of that kind of offence — so that whoever is properly positioned to act on it can do so.

### What changed from the CrPC

::: info Verify before citing
BNSS sections 168 and 169 are provisionally mapped to CrPC sections 149 and 150 respectively. This mapping is unverified against the CrPC bare-act text and must be independently confirmed before use in any filing.
:::

Both sections read as close carry-forwards of their presumed CrPC ancestors, with no substantive change visible in the operative language. Chapter XII as a whole is one of the shorter, less-restructured chapters in the Sanhita — see the note under sections 171–172 below for the one point in this chapter where the BNSS does appear to depart from the CrPC's presumed structure.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct The officer who heard the plan third-hand
A Head Constable hears from a police informant that two named individuals are planning a cognizable offence later that week. The Head Constable, uncertain whether the information is reliable, decides to wait and see rather than pass it up the chain, reasoning that reporting unverified information would waste his superior's time.
::: ruling What actually happens
Section 169's duty is triggered by "receiving information of a design," not by the officer's own satisfaction that the information is reliable. The reliability of the information is a matter for the officer or authority who receives the report to assess, not a threshold the receiving officer may impose on himself before deciding whether to communicate it at all. The Head Constable's decision to sit on the information is not a permitted exercise of judgment under section 169; the duty to communicate arose the moment he received it.
:::
::::

:::: instinct The off-duty officer who saw a robbery in progress
A police officer, off duty and in plain clothes, witnesses a robbery taking place in front of him. He considers intervening but decides that, being off duty, the matter is not his responsibility, and instead continues walking.
::: ruling What actually happens
Section 168 does not condition the duty to prevent on the officer being on duty or in uniform at the time — it applies to "every police officer" who becomes aware of a cognizable offence being committed. The officer's off-duty status does not suspend the best-efforts duty to prevent the offence; his decision to walk on is not shielded by the fact that he was not formally on patrol at the time.
:::
::::

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

::: info Research gap — no case promoted
No single Supreme Court authority is promoted to a winning ratio for this cluster. Sections 168 and 169 are rarely the direct subject of appellate litigation; they typically surface as background context when an officer's conduct — action taken, or inaction — is examined in a separate proceeding, whether disciplinary or connected to a later substantive charge.
:::

#### Sword and shield

As a **sword**, a complainant or disciplinary authority can point to section 168's mandatory "shall...prevent" language to argue that an officer's total inaction in the face of a cognizable offence he was aware of was itself a dereliction, independent of whatever offence actually followed. As a **shield**, an officer who did act — even imperfectly, or without success — can rely on section 168's "to the best of his ability" standard, since the section does not guarantee an outcome and does not penalise a genuine, reasonable attempt that nonetheless failed to prevent the offence.

#### The limitation clock

No independent limitation period attaches to ss. 168–169; the operative timing question is how promptly the officer communicated information of a design under section 169, since the section fixes no numbered period and instead depends on the surrounding facts of when the information was received and when it was passed on.

#### Interlocking matrix

| Situation | Governing provision | What follows |
|---|---|---|
| Cognizable offence being committed, or imminent, within the officer's awareness | Section 168 | Best-efforts duty to prevent; discretion only over method, not over whether to try |
| Information received of a design (plan) to commit a cognizable offence | Section 169 | Mandatory duty to communicate to the officer's own superior, and to any other officer whose duty it is to prevent or take cognizance |

#### Strategy and drafting

**Drafting traps.**

1. Do not conflate section 168's "may interpose" with an absence of duty — the permissive language governs only the choice of method; the duty to try to prevent the offence is mandatory.
2. Do not treat section 169's communication duty as contingent on the officer's own assessment of the information's reliability — the duty is triggered by receipt of the information, not by the officer's confidence in it.
3. Do not overlook that section 169 requires communication to two distinct recipients — the officer's own superior, and separately any other officer whose duty it is to prevent or take cognizance — not merely one or the other.

**Timing.** Where an officer's conduct under section 168 or 169 is challenged, establish precisely when he became aware of the offence or the design, since both duties are measured from that moment, not from any later point of convenience.

**Model checklist for reviewing an officer's preventive conduct:**

1. Confirm whether the officer was aware of an offence in progress or imminent (section 168) or of a design to commit one (section 169) — the two sections impose different duties.
2. Where section 168 is engaged, assess what the officer actually did against a best-efforts standard, not an outcome-guarantee standard.
3. Where section 169 is engaged, confirm whether the officer communicated to both required recipients, and how promptly.

## Section 170 — The narrow power to arrest someone before the offence he is planning has actually happened

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute One tightly-conditioned power: arresting a person for what he is planning to do, not for what he has already done, with a hard 24-hour outer limit unless some other provision extends it.
Section 170 is the sharpest tool in this chapter, and the only one in Chapter XII that authorises an arrest. It lets a police officer arrest a person who is merely designing — planning — a cognizable offence, without a Magistrate's order and without a warrant, but only where the officer forms the view that the offence cannot otherwise be prevented. Because this is an arrest made before any offence has occurred, section 170(2) immediately pairs the power with a firm 24-hour detention ceiling, unless some other provision of the Sanhita or other law independently authorises holding the person longer.
:::

::: story The arrest that had to be justified by what didn't happen
A police officer learns that a person is actively planning to commit a serious cognizable offence within hours, and concludes that there is no other practical way to stop it — no time to approach a Magistrate, no lesser step available. He arrests the person under section 170 without a warrant. The arrested person later challenges the arrest, arguing no offence had actually been committed. The challenge misunderstands the section: section 170 is expressly built for exactly this situation — a design, not yet executed — and the officer's own assessment that the offence "cannot be otherwise prevented" is the operative condition, not the prior commission of any offence.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 170 — Arrest to prevent the commission of cognizable offences
"170. (1) A police officer knowing of a design to commit any cognizable offence [[?may]] arrest, without orders from a Magistrate and without a warrant, the person so designing, if it appears to such officer that the commission of the offence cannot be otherwise prevented.
(2) No person arrested under sub-section (1) [[!shall]] be detained in custody for a period exceeding twenty-four hours from the time of his arrest unless his further detention is required or authorised under any other provisions of this Sanhita or of any other law for the time being in force."
:::

### In plain English

Section 170 is a preventive-arrest power, and preventive powers are always narrower than reactive ones — this is why the section builds in two separate conditions before an arrest is even lawful. First, the officer must actually know of a design: a real plan, not a hunch. Second, and just as important, it must appear to the officer that the offence cannot otherwise be prevented — if some lesser step, short of arrest, would stop the offence just as well, the necessity condition is not met. Section 170(2) then does something the chapter does nowhere else: it puts a hard clock on the resulting custody. Twenty-four hours from the moment of arrest is the default outer limit, and the only way past it is if some other provision of the Sanhita, or some other law altogether, independently authorises holding the person longer — section 170 itself does not extend its own deadline.

### What changed from the CrPC

::: info Verify before citing
BNSS section 170 is provisionally mapped to CrPC section 151. This mapping is unverified against the CrPC bare-act text and must be independently confirmed before use in any filing.
:::

The section reads as a close carry-forward of its presumed CrPC ancestor, including the twenty-four hour detention ceiling and the "cannot otherwise be prevented" necessity threshold. No substantive change is apparent from the operative text.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct The arrest that could have waited for a Magistrate
An officer learns, several days in advance, of a design to commit a cognizable offence. There is ample time to approach a Magistrate for orders before the planned date. Instead, the officer arrests the person immediately under section 170, without attempting to seek any Magistrate's involvement.
::: ruling What actually happens
Section 170(1)'s necessity condition — that "it appears to such officer that the commission of the offence cannot be otherwise prevented" — is squarely in issue here. Where there is genuinely enough time to approach a Magistrate and the officer simply chose not to, the necessity for an immediate warrantless arrest is weaker, and an officer relying on section 170 should be prepared to justify, on the specific facts, why the offence could not otherwise have been prevented notwithstanding the available time. The mere existence of a design is not, by itself, sufficient; the necessity condition carries independent weight.
:::
::::

:::: instinct Held for thirty hours without any further order
A person arrested under section 170 is held in custody for thirty hours. No order under any other provision of the Sanhita or any other law has been obtained to authorise detention beyond twenty-four hours; the delay is attributed to administrative backlog at the police station.
::: ruling What actually happens
Section 170(2) fixes twenty-four hours from the time of arrest as the outer limit, subject only to detention that is independently required or authorised under another provision. Administrative backlog is not itself an independent authorisation under any other provision — it is precisely the kind of practical difficulty the twenty-four hour rule is designed to guard against. The detention beyond the twenty-fourth hour, on these facts, is not saved by the backlog explanation.
:::
::::

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

::: info Research gap — no case promoted
No single Supreme Court authority is promoted to a winning ratio for this cluster. Section 170 is most often litigated as one strand within a broader challenge to the legality of an arrest and subsequent detention, rather than as the sole ground of a reported appellate decision.
:::

#### Sword and shield

As a **sword**, a person challenging his arrest can argue that the officer had no genuine "design" before him, or that the necessity condition — that the offence "cannot be otherwise prevented" — was not actually met on the facts, defeating the arrest's legality at the threshold. As a **shield**, the arresting officer can point to section 170(2)'s own text: so long as the detention did not exceed twenty-four hours from arrest, or was independently authorised beyond that point under another provision, the detention itself is not open to challenge purely on duration grounds.

#### The limitation clock

Section 170(2)'s twenty-four hour rule is the operative clock in this section — not a limitation period in the conventional sense, but a hard ceiling on custody that runs from the moment of arrest and must be affirmatively displaced by an independent authorisation, not assumed to extend by default.

| Situation | Governing provision | What follows |

| Person arrested under section 170(1) reaches twenty-four hours in custody | Section 170(2) | Detention becomes unlawful unless independently required or authorised under another provision of the Sanhita or other law |

#### Strategy and drafting

**Drafting traps.**

1. Do not treat the existence of a design alone as sufficient to justify a section 170 arrest — the necessity condition, that the offence cannot otherwise be prevented, must independently be shown.
2. Do not assume the twenty-four hour ceiling in section 170(2) extends automatically for administrative reasons — it yields only to an independent legal authorisation under another provision.
3. Do not overlook that section 170(2)'s clock runs from the time of arrest, not from the time of production before a Magistrate or any other authority.

**Timing.** Any challenge to a section 170 arrest on necessity grounds should be raised as early as possible, ideally before the twenty-four hour window closes, since a detention that later becomes independently authorised under another provision may narrow the practical relief available.

**Model checklist for reviewing a section 170 arrest:**

1. Confirm the officer's basis for knowing of an actual design, not a mere suspicion.
2. Confirm the officer's stated basis for concluding the offence could not otherwise be prevented.
3. Track the twenty-four hour clock from the moment of arrest, and identify any independent provision relied upon to justify detention beyond it.

## Sections 171–172 — Stepping in to protect public property, and the general duty to obey a police officer's lawful directions under this Chapter

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute The chapter closes with a property-protection power and a general obedience-and-enforcement provision that ties the whole chapter together — anyone who ignores a lawful direction given under it can be detained or removed.
Section 171 gives a police officer his own authority to step in when he sees public property, or a navigation mark, being damaged or interfered with, without needing anyone's order. Section 172 then generalises: it binds all persons to conform to any lawful direction a police officer gives while carrying out his duty under this Chapter, and backs that duty with a real enforcement tool — the officer may detain or remove a person who resists or ignores the direction, and either bring him before a Magistrate or, in minor cases, release him within twenty-four hours.
:::

::: story The buoy, the boat owner, and the direction he ignored
A police officer sees a person attempting to remove a navigation buoy from a river for scrap value. Acting under section 171, the officer directs the person to stop and step away from the buoy. The person refuses and continues. Section 172 gives the officer a specific answer to the refusal: because the direction was given in fulfilment of the officer's duty under this Chapter — here, preventing injury to a public navigation mark under section 171 — the person was bound to conform to it, and his refusal entitles the officer to detain or remove him, bringing him before a Magistrate or releasing him within twenty-four hours if the matter is petty.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

::: proviso Section 171 — Prevention of injury to public property
"171. A police officer [[?may]] of his own authority interpose to prevent any injury attempted to be committed in his view to any public property, movable or immovable, or the removal or injury of any public landmark, buoy or other mark used for navigation."
:::

::: proviso Section 172 — Persons bound to conform to lawful directions of police
"172. (1) All persons [[!shall]] be bound to conform to the lawful directions of a police officer given in fulfilment of any of his duty under this Chapter.
(2) A police officer [[?may]] detain or remove any person resisting, refusing, ignoring or disregarding to conform to any direction given by him under sub-section (1) and [[?may]] either take such person before a Magistrate or, in petty cases, release him as soon as possible within a period of twenty-four hours."
:::

### In plain English

Section 171 is a companion to section 168's general prevention duty, narrowed to a specific target: public property, and specifically including landmarks and navigation marks like buoys, which matter because damage to them can endanger navigation generally, not just the particular object. The officer does not need anyone's order — his own presence and observation of the attempted injury is enough to authorise him to step in. Section 172 then does the structural work of making the whole chapter enforceable: it is not enough for an officer to have the power to act or the power to direct someone under sections 168 through 171 if the person on the receiving end can simply ignore him. Section 172(1) fixes that by making compliance with any lawful direction given under this Chapter's duties a binding obligation on "all persons," and section 172(2) gives the officer a proportionate response to disobedience — detain or remove the resisting person, then either produce him before a Magistrate for the matter to be dealt with formally, or, where the matter is genuinely petty, release him within a firm twenty-four hour outer limit rather than holding him indefinitely.

### What changed from the CrPC

::: info Verify before citing
BNSS section 171 is provisionally mapped to CrPC section 152. Section 172 does not appear to correspond to CrPC section 153, which is understood to have addressed a different subject — inspection of weights and measures — rather than a general duty to conform to police directions. This correspondence, and in particular whether section 172 is a genuinely new BNSS addition to this chapter or corresponds to some other CrPC provision located elsewhere in that Code, is unverified against the CrPC bare-act text and must be independently confirmed before use in any filing.
:::

If the understanding above is correct, section 172 is the one point in Chapter XII where the BNSS structure departs from the CrPC's presumed Chapter XI: the CrPC's closing provision on weights and measures inspection does not appear to have been carried forward into this chapter, and BNSS section 172 — a general obedience-and-enforcement provision tying together the whole chapter's preventive duties — takes its place instead. Practitioners should treat this as a flagged, unverified structural observation rather than a settled proposition until checked directly against the CrPC bare-act text.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct The direction that had nothing to do with this Chapter
A police officer, in the course of an unrelated traffic-management task with no connection to preventing an offence, a breach of the peace, or injury to public property, directs a pedestrian to move along. The pedestrian refuses, and the officer seeks to detain him under section 172(2).
::: ruling What actually happens
Section 172(1) binds a person to conform to a lawful direction given "in fulfilment of any of his duty under this Chapter" — meaning a duty arising under sections 168 to 171 specifically. A direction connected to routine traffic management, unconnected to any Chapter XII duty, does not fall within section 172(1) merely because a police officer gave it. Without a section 172(1) direction properly grounded in this Chapter, the section 172(2) detain-or-remove power is not available on these facts.
:::
::::

:::: instinct Released after thirty hours instead of twenty-four
A person detained under section 172(2) for a genuinely petty matter is held for thirty hours before being released, well past the twenty-four hour figure the section contemplates for such cases.
::: ruling What actually happens
Section 172(2) requires that in petty cases the person be released "as soon as possible within a period of twenty-four hours" — the twenty-four hour figure operates as an outer limit for petty-case release, not a target that can be comfortably exceeded. A thirty-hour detention for a matter the officer himself has treated as petty is not consistent with the section's own terms, and the delay would need to be independently justified rather than treated as within the ordinary course.
:::

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

::: info Research gap — no case promoted
No single Supreme Court authority is promoted to a winning ratio for this cluster. Sections 171 and 172 are narrow, mechanical provisions that are more commonly litigated as background to a substantive obstruction or property-damage charge than as the direct subject of appellate authority in their own right.
:::

#### Sword and shield

As a **sword**, a prosecuting authority can rely on section 172(1) and (2) together to establish that a person's resistance to a lawful Chapter XII direction was itself unlawful, supporting the detain-or-remove action taken and any connected charge. As a **shield**, a person detained under section 172(2) can challenge the detention by showing the underlying direction did not, in fact, arise from a duty under this Chapter, or that a petty-case release was not effected within the twenty-four hour outer limit the section itself sets.

#### The limitation clock

No independent limitation period attaches to ss. 171–172; the operative timing constraint is section 172(2)'s twenty-four hour outer limit for releasing a person detained in a petty case, which should be treated as a firm ceiling rather than a loose guideline.

#### Strategy and drafting

**Drafting traps.**

1. Do not assume every police direction attracts section 172(1)'s duty to conform — confirm the direction was actually given in fulfilment of a duty under this Chapter specifically.
2. Do not treat section 172(2)'s twenty-four hour figure for petty-case release as a target rather than an outer limit — the section requires release "as soon as possible" within that period, not merely before it expires.
3. Do not overlook that section 171's power depends on the officer's own view of the attempted injury — a report from a third party, without the officer's own observation, sits less comfortably within the section's "in his view" language.

**Timing.** Where a section 172(2) detention is challenged, establish promptly whether the matter was genuinely petty and, if so, whether release occurred within the twenty-four hour outer limit, since delay in raising this narrows the practical value of the challenge.

**Model checklist for reviewing a section 171 or 172 action:**

1. Confirm the officer's own observation of the attempted injury to public property or a navigation mark under section 171.
2. Confirm the direction relied upon under section 172(1) was genuinely given in fulfilment of a Chapter XII duty.
3. Where detention followed under section 172(2), confirm whether the matter was treated as petty and, if so, whether release occurred within twenty-four hours.
