# Chapter V — Arrest of Persons

## Section 35 — When police may arrest without warrant

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Not every cognizable offence gets an arrest anymore — for the seven-years-or-less band, the officer must justify the arrest, not just the offence.
For decades, the working assumption on the ground was simple: cognizable offence means the police can arrest, and "can" quietly became "does." A dowry-harassment complaint, a cheque-bounce dispute dressed up as cheating, a minor scuffle charged as voluntarily causing hurt — all cognizable, all resulting in arrest as a matter of routine, often before the accused had even been asked a single question. Arrest became the investigation's opening move rather than one of its later options, and it came with real costs: a night or more in lock-up, a police record, a family's reputation, sometimes a job — inflicted before a single fact had been tested.

Section 35 answers a narrower but sharper question than "can the police arrest." For the large middle band of offences — punishable with less than seven years, or up to seven years — it asks: **is arrest actually necessary**, against a specific, closed checklist, and can the officer justify it in writing if he says yes? If he cannot honestly tick one of those boxes, the statute's default is not arrest at all — it is a written notice to appear.
:::

::: story Bikram gets a notice instead of handcuffs
Anita's shopkeeper neighbour, Bikram, is accused by a business rival of criminal breach of trust over a disputed consignment — an offence that, on this telling, carries a maximum of five years. The investigating officer has credible information against him. Ten years ago, this would very likely have ended with Bikram spending a night in the lock-up before anyone heard his side. Under section 35, the officer's first move is not the handcuffs — it is the checklist. Is Bikram likely to reoffend, tamper with the ledger evidence, threaten the complainant, or abscond? If the officer cannot honestly say yes to at least one of those specific grounds, section 35(3) requires a notice instead: come in on a stated date, answer questions, and — so long as Bikram keeps complying — he is not to be arrested for this offence at all.
:::

> **35. When police may arrest without warrant.**—(1) Any police officer may without an order from a Magistrate and without a warrant, arrest any person—
>
> (a) who commits, in the presence of a police officer, a cognizable offence; or
>
> (b) against whom a reasonable complaint has been made, or credible information has been received, or a reasonable suspicion exists that he has committed a cognizable offence punishable with imprisonment for a term which may be less than seven years or which may extend to seven years whether with or without fine, if the following conditions are satisfied, namely:—
>
> (i) the police officer has reason to believe on the basis of such complaint, information, or suspicion that such person has committed the said offence;
>
> (ii) the police officer is satisfied that such arrest is [[!necessary]]—
>
> (a) to prevent such person from committing any further offence; or
>
> (b) for proper investigation of the offence; or
>
> (c) to prevent such person from causing the evidence of the offence to disappear or tampering with such evidence in any manner; or
>
> (d) to prevent such person from making any inducement, threat or promise to any person acquainted with the facts of the case so as to dissuade him from disclosing such facts to the Court or to the police officer; or
>
> (e) as unless such person is arrested, his presence in the Court whenever required cannot be ensured,
>
> and the police officer [[!shall]] record while making such arrest, his reasons in writing:
>
> Provided that a police officer [[!shall]], in all cases where the arrest of a person is not required under the provisions of this sub-section, record the reasons in writing for not making the arrest; or
>
> (c) against whom credible information has been received that he has committed a cognizable offence punishable with imprisonment for a term which may extend to more than seven years whether with or without fine or with death sentence and the police officer has reason to believe on the basis of that information that such person has committed the said offence; or
>
> (d) who has been proclaimed as an offender either under this Sanhita or by order of the State Government; or
>
> (e) in whose possession anything is found which may reasonably be suspected to be stolen property and who may reasonably be suspected of having committed an offence with reference to such thing; or
>
> (f) who obstructs a police officer while in the execution of his duty, or who has escaped, or attempts to escape, from lawful custody; or
>
> (g) who is reasonably suspected of being a deserter from any of the Armed Forces of the Union; or
>
> (h) who has been concerned in, or against whom a reasonable complaint has been made, or credible information has been received, or a reasonable suspicion exists, of his having been concerned in, any act committed at any place out of India which, if committed in India, would have been punishable as an offence, and for which he is, under any law relating to extradition, or otherwise, liable to be apprehended or detained in custody in India; or
>
> (i) who, being a released convict, commits a breach of any rule made under sub-section (5) of section 394; or
>
> (j) for whose arrest any requisition, whether written or oral, has been received from another police officer, provided that the requisition specifies the person to be arrested and the offence or other cause for which the arrest is to be made and it appears therefrom that the person might lawfully be arrested without a warrant by the officer who issued the requisition.
>
> ::: proviso The seven-year line is the whole ballgame
> Clause (b) is not one ground among ten equal grounds — it is the ground that governs the largest share of everyday arrests: the entire middle band of offences carrying up to seven years. For this band alone, arrest is conditional on the closed five-item necessity checklist at (i)–(ii), and on recording reasons in writing either way — for arresting, or for not arresting. Clauses (a) and (c)–(j) are separate, freestanding grounds that do not carry this checklist; do not import clause (b)'s necessity test into an arrest actually made under one of the other clauses, and do not let an arrest made under another clause's label smuggle past what was, in substance, a clause (b) arrest that skipped the checklist.
> :::
>
> (2) Subject to the provisions of section 39, no person concerned in a non-cognizable offence or against whom a complaint has been made or credible information has been received or reasonable suspicion exists of his having so concerned, [[!shall]] be arrested except under a warrant or order of a Magistrate.
>
> (3) The police officer [[!shall]], in all cases where the arrest of a person is not required under sub-section (1), issue a notice directing the person against whom a reasonable complaint has been made, or credible information has been received, or a reasonable suspicion exists that he has committed a cognizable offence, to appear before him or at such other place as may be specified in the notice.
>
> (4) Where such a notice is issued to any person, it [[!shall]] be the duty of that person to comply with the terms of the notice.
>
> (5) Where such person complies and continues to comply with the notice, he [[!shall]] not be arrested in respect of the offence referred to in the notice unless, for reasons to be recorded, the police officer is of the opinion that he ought to be arrested.
>
> (6) Where such person, at any time, fails to comply with the terms of the notice or is unwilling to identify himself, the police officer [[?may]], subject to such orders as may have been passed by a competent Court in this behalf, arrest him for the offence mentioned in the notice.
>
> ::: proviso Compliance is a shield, but not an unconditional one
> Sub-section (5) protects a complying noticee from arrest on the notified offence — but only "unless, for reasons to be recorded, the police officer is of the opinion that he ought to be arrested." That escape clause means compliance narrows the officer's discretion and forces it onto the record; it does not eliminate the discretion outright. A practitioner defending a noticee who complied and was arrested anyway should demand the recorded reasons specifically, since their absence is itself a clean procedural challenge.
> :::
>
> (7) No arrest shall be made without prior permission of an officer not below the rank of Deputy Superintendent of Police in case of an offence which is punishable for imprisonment of less than three years and such person is infirm or is above sixty years of age.

### In plain English

Section 35 is best read as three separate tracks bolted together under one heading. The **first track**, clause (a) and clauses (c) through (j), covers arrest grounds that do not depend on offence severity at all — an offence committed in an officer's presence, a proclaimed offender, a person with suspected stolen property, someone obstructing or escaping custody, and a handful of other specific situations. The **second track**, clause (b), is the one that actually reshaped everyday policing: for the vast majority of offences — anything carrying up to seven years — arrest is not automatic just because a complaint or credible information exists. The officer must be able to point to one of five specific necessity grounds and write down his reasons, whichever way he decides. The **third track**, sub-sections (3) through (6), is the notice-of-appearance mechanism that functions as clause (b)'s default: if arrest is not shown to be necessary, the person gets a notice instead, and staying in compliance with it is a genuine, if conditional, shield against arrest on that offence.

Sub-section (7) layers a fourth protection on top of all of this for a narrow but real category — elderly or infirm persons facing a minor charge — requiring a senior officer's sign-off before arrest at all.

### What changed from the CrPC

BNSS section 35 corresponds to CrPC sections 41 and 41A read together. ⚠️ *Cross-reference against a verified CrPC-to-BNSS concordance table before relying on this mapping in a filing.*

This confirms, rather than merely restates, the roadmap's working hypothesis that the notice-of-appearance mechanism (previously its own section, CrPC s. 41A, introduced by the 2010 CrPC amendment codifying *Joginder Kumar*) is now folded into section 35 itself, as sub-sections (3)–(6), rather than standing as a separate section. This is a **renumbering with structural consolidation** rather than a substantively new mechanism — the necessity checklist at clause (b)(ii) and the notice regime both existed in the amended CrPC after 2010; what has changed is that they now sit inside the same section as the general arrest power, making the connection between "is arrest necessary" and "arrest without warrant" textually explicit rather than split across two sections.

### Test your instinct

:::: instinct The complaint is credible, but the officer skips the checklist
A police officer receives a credible complaint that a shopkeeper committed criminal intimidation, an offence punishable with up to two years. The officer arrests him immediately, citing only "credible information has been received" without recording any reasons addressing the five necessity grounds in clause (b)(ii).
::: ruling What actually happens
Credible information alone satisfies only the threshold condition at (b)(i) — it does not substitute for the separate, mandatory satisfaction-and-recording requirement at (b)(ii). An arrest under clause (b) without reasons addressing at least one of the five necessity grounds is not a minor irregularity to be cured later; it is a failure to meet a condition precedent the statute itself makes explicit, and it is precisely the pattern *Arnesh Kumar* was written to stop.
:::
::::

:::: instinct Compliance with the notice, followed by arrest anyway
A person served with a section 35(3) notice appears every time as directed, answers questions, and causes no obstruction. Three months later, without any new development in the case, the police arrest him on the same offence, citing sub-section (5)'s exception but recording no actual reasons on file.
::: ruling What actually happens
Sub-section (5)'s exception requires reasons to be recorded — a bare invocation of the exception without anything on the record demonstrating why arrest suddenly became necessary despite continued compliance does not satisfy the sub-section. Continued, unbroken compliance is meant to function as a real shield, not a formality the police can set aside by simply reciting the exception's language. The absence of a documented change in circumstances is a strong basis to challenge the arrest.
:::
::::

:::: instinct The seventy-year-old, minor offence, no DSP permission
A seventy-year-old man is arrested for an offence punishable with two years' imprisonment. The arresting officer, a Sub-Inspector, did not seek or obtain permission from any officer of the rank of Deputy Superintendent of Police or above before making the arrest.
::: ruling What actually happens
Sub-section (7) is a freestanding, mandatory precondition that applies regardless of whether clause (b)'s necessity checklist was otherwise satisfied — it protects a specific vulnerable category (infirm persons and those above sixty) against arrest for minor offences without senior oversight. An arrest made in violation of sub-section (7) is not saved merely because the arresting officer separately satisfied the clause (b) necessity conditions; the DSP-permission requirement is additional, not alternative, and its absence is independently actionable.
:::
::::

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

**Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar**, (2014) 8 SCC 273. The Supreme Court held that arrest should not be made in a routine, casual and cavalier manner merely because an offence is cognizable, and directed that for offences punishable with imprisonment of seven years or less, police officers must be satisfied that arrest is necessary as per the specific parameters now carried forward in clause (b)(ii) — to prevent further offences, for proper investigation, to prevent tampering with evidence, to prevent inducement of witnesses, or to ensure presence in court — and must record their reasons; the Court further directed Magistrates authorising further detention to record their satisfaction that these conditions were met.

**Practitioner takeaway.** In any arrest under clause (b) where the police record does not show reasoned satisfaction of a specific (b)(ii) necessity ground, plead *Arnesh Kumar* by name: "an arrest made without recording the necessity conditions this Court mandated in Arnesh Kumar is not a curable irregularity but a failure of a condition precedent to the arrest itself."

#### Sword and shield

**As a sword (complainant):** where the accused fails to comply with a section 35(3) notice, or is unwilling to identify himself, sub-section (6) gives a clean textual basis to press for arrest — non-compliance is not merely unhelpful, it is the specific trigger the statute names.

**As a shield (accused):** for any clause (b) offence, demand the recorded necessity reasons at the very first opportunity — at remand, at bail, or in a quashing petition. Absence of recorded reasons addressing a specific (b)(ii) ground is, per *Arnesh Kumar*, not a technicality but a basis to challenge the arrest and press for release. For elderly or infirm clients facing minor charges, check sub-section (7) compliance independently of everything else — it is a separate, additional safeguard.

#### The limitation clock

Section 35 itself sets no limitation period, but it interlocks tightly with two: the section 39 twenty-four-hour window to ascertain a false-name arrestee's true identity, and the section 58/187 twenty-four-hour production requirement that begins running from the moment of any arrest made under this section, regardless of which clause was invoked.

#### Interlocking matrix

| Trigger | Linked provision | Practical consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Offence punishable up to seven years, arrest sought | Section 35(1)(b)–(2) | Necessity checklist and written reasons required |
| Necessity not shown | Section 35(3)–(5) | Notice to appear issued instead of arrest |
| Non-compliance with notice | Section 35(6) | Arrest becomes available on the notified offence |
| Infirm/elderly person, minor offence | Section 35(7) | DSP-rank prior permission required before arrest |
| Any arrest under this section | Sections 36–38 | Memorandum-of-arrest, notification, and counsel-access duties attach |
| Any arrest under this section | Sections 58, 187 | Twenty-four-hour production clock begins running |

#### The authorities

**Joginder Kumar v. State of U.P.**, (1994) 4 SCC 260 — the foundational authority holding that the existence of a power to arrest is different from the justification for its exercise, and that an arresting officer must be able to justify the arrest apart from his power to do so.

::: info Verify before citing
The citation for *Joginder Kumar v. State of U.P.* is recorded from the drafter's working knowledge and should be independently verified against the official SCC report before being relied upon in a filing.
:::

#### Strategy and drafting

**Drafting traps.**

1. Do not treat clause (b)'s necessity checklist as satisfied by the mere recitation of its language in the arrest memo — the reasons must engage the specific facts of the case, not repeat the statutory words as a formality.
2. Do not overlook sub-section (7) as a separate ground for challenge, distinct from any clause (b) necessity argument, wherever the arrestee is over sixty or infirm and the offence carries under three years.
3. Do not assume that compliance with a section 35(3) notice guarantees immunity from arrest — sub-section (5)'s exception remains available, but demand the recorded reasons if arrest follows compliance.
4. Do not conflate an arrest under clauses (a) or (c)–(j) with a clause (b) arrest when pleading — the necessity checklist and its *Arnesh Kumar* jurisprudence attach specifically to clause (b), not to the other grounds.

**Timing.** Raise an *Arnesh Kumar*-style necessity challenge at the earliest possible stage — ideally at the first remand hearing, since a belated challenge after a long detention is harder to frame as going to the legality of the arrest itself rather than to the conditions of continued custody.

**Model prayer checklist for challenging a clause (b) arrest:**

1. Identify the specific offence and confirm it falls within the "up to seven years" band governed by clause (b).
2. Obtain the arrest memorandum and check whether it records reasons addressing a specific (b)(ii) necessity ground, not merely the statutory language.
3. Check whether a section 35(3) notice was issued first, and if not, whether the record explains why not.
4. Where the arrestee is over sixty or infirm and the offence carries under three years, independently verify DSP-rank permission was obtained under sub-section (7).
5. Frame any deficiency found as a failure of a condition precedent under *Arnesh Kumar*, not as a mere procedural irregularity.
